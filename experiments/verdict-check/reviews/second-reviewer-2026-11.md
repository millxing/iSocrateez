## Part 3. The two method questions

**1. Is a forced choice fair to Aristotle and Confucius?** Yes, provided the test distinguishes the **action** from the **reason for it**. Both thinkers make definite judgments. A sound test can ask an app to do the same. The danger is scoring a modern case against a single answer when the writer has left out the fact that would decide it.

I would keep the forced choice and add one sentence to the prompt: **“Choose A or B. Name the fact that matters most to this thinker. What change to that fact would most likely reverse the answer?”** Then test the reversal. Give Aristotle one music club founded to prepare an elite ensemble for performance and another founded to introduce untrained members to music. Hold the numbers and costs fixed. An app that can explain why the club’s purpose changes the force of the six players’ claim shows more understanding than one that reliably picks six whenever Aristotle’s name appears. [Politics III.12](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.3.three.html)

I would also keep the **Open** items in a separate part of the test. They can reveal a different failure: an app confidently claiming that a philosopher settled a question the text leaves open. And I would keep a few cases where all four should agree, such as returning the wallet. Otherwise, the test rewards an app for manufacturing differences. Agreement on all 12 original dilemmas is a reason to investigate. The stronger evidence is whether the app **keeps giving the same advice when a fact that matters to one philosopher changes**.

**2. Which classic case would I add?** **Confucius on the ceremonial cap and the bow, *Analects* 9.3.** It gives you two short decisions and the philosopher’s own answer to each.

| Question for the app | Confucius’s recorded judgment |
|---|---|
| Ritual prescribes a linen cap. People now use a less costly silk cap. Which would you wear? | **Use silk.** Confucius accepts the economical change. |
| Ritual prescribes bowing before ascending the hall. People now bow after ascending. When would you bow? | **Bow before ascending.** Confucius judges the newer practice arrogant. |

The pair catches two opposite mistakes. An app that treats Confucius as a rigid traditionalist gets the cap wrong. An app that treats him as someone who always adapts to current custom gets the bow wrong. The same passage supplies both verdicts and both reasons. That makes it a stronger test than asking reviewers to infer what he would have said about a modern workplace. [Analects 9.3](https://classics.mit.edu/Confucius/analects.2.2.html)
