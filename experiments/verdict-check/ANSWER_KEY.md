# Verdict check: answer key for review

This document lists, for each test dilemma, the option that each test philosopher's own texts lead to: the core four (Mill, Kant, Aristotle, Confucius) and, since 2026-11-21, three transfer thinkers (Mencius, Hume, Maimonides). It also lists which of the app's three axes should decide the dilemma. The app's advice is scored against it.

Status: provisional, written by an AI model. The key has had a second review. Answers that the reviewer judged "open" are not scored.

- "clear": a text speaks to the case, or the doctrine leaves little doubt.
- "disputed": a defensible reading, but scholars could argue for another option. Such entries may list other accepted options.
- "open": a reviewer judged that the texts do not settle the case. The reason is shown. Not scored.
- "—": the key has no entry, so the philosopher is not scored on that dilemma.

How to mark corrections: write next to an entry in the Expected or Confidence column, or in the margin of the section. For example: "change to keep", "downgrade to disputed", "wrong source". Mark any dilemma that is unclear or unfair.

Analects passage numbers follow D. C. Lau's translation. Other editions differ by one or two.

## Paired cases

A pair is one case told twice. The two tellings differ in one fact, the pivot. For each thinker, the key says if the pivot should change the answer ("flips") or not ("same"). The test asks if the app's answer changes when it should, and stays when it should. Every pair is run for all seven philosophers and for a baseline with no philosopher, and a correct pattern counts as evidence for a thinker only where it differs from the baseline.

Basis is given for each side of the pair (one value when both sides agree). "recorded": the thinker's own text gives an answer to a case of this shape on that side. "inferred": our reading of the thinker's principles; no text gives the answer directly.

### Development pairs

### The cap and the bow (cap-and-bow)

Split: development.

Pivot: What the new custom changes: the cost of the cap, or the deference that the bow shows.

Base case:

> In my community's ancestral ceremony, the rite prescribes a cap of hemp. Most people now wear a silk cap, which costs much less. The only difference is the cost. I must choose which cap to wear at this year's ceremony. Should I keep the hemp cap that the rite prescribes, or wear silk as most people now do?

Variant case:

> In my community's ancestral ceremony, the rite prescribes that a guest bows to the elder who presides while the guest is still at the foot of the steps, before he goes up into the hall. Most people now go up the steps first and bow when they stand level with the elder. I must choose what to do at this year's ceremony. Should I bow at the foot of the steps as the rite prescribes, or bow at the top as most people now do?

Options (the same for both cases):

- `old`: Keep the old practice that the rite prescribes
- `new`: Follow the new common practice

| Thinker | Expectation | Basis | Reason | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Confucius | flips: new → old | recorded | Confucius accepts the silk cap because the change is only thrift. He rejects the bow at the top of the steps because that change is arrogant, and he keeps the old practice against the majority. The passage does not say to whom the bow is made; the usual reading is a minister before his ruler, and the case puts a presiding elder in that place. | Analects 9.3 |

### The same question from two different people (hold-back-or-urge)

Split: development.

Pivot: The character of the person who asks: one takes things on too fast, the other waits too long.

Base case:

> A friend is starting a free evening school for workers in our town. He asks me to teach there two evenings a week, from next week. It costs me no money, and I can stop when I want. I live with my father and my elder brother, and I have not told them. People who know me say that I take things on before I think, and twice this year I had to break promises that I made too fast. Should I say yes now, or first talk it over with my father and my brother?

Variant case:

> A friend is starting a free evening school for workers in our town. He asks me to teach there two evenings a week, from next week. It costs me no money, and I can stop when I want. I live with my father and my elder brother, and I have not told them. People who know me say that I wait too long, and twice this year I let a good chance pass because I could not decide. Should I say yes now, or first talk it over with my father and my brother?

Options (the same for both cases):

- `act`: Say yes now, on your own judgment
- `consult`: Wait, and talk it over with the father and the brother first

| Thinker | Expectation | Basis | Reason | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Confucius | flips: consult → act | recorded | Two disciples ask Confucius the same question: should one act at once on what one hears? He tells Zilu, who has more than his share of energy, that his father and elder brothers are there to be consulted. He tells Ran You, who holds back, to act at once. He explains that he restrains the one and urges on the other. The recorded contrast is the temperament; only Zilu is told of his father and brothers. | Analects 11.22 (Legge 11.21) |

### The credit: a celebration, or the board's decision (credit-stakes)

Split: development.

Pivot: What depends on the wrong impression: nothing but applause, or who the board puts in charge of the risks.

Base case:

> I led a project that saved our company. At the staff celebration, the director thanked a senior colleague as the leader, and the room applauded him. He did little. I speak next. Nothing is recorded, and nothing depends on who is thanked: the board already has the correct facts. If I say plainly that I led the work, I get the credit that I earned, and he loses face in front of everyone. If I say nothing of my own part, the wrong impression stays. I will not say anything false. Should I say that I led the work?

Variant case:

> I led a project that saved our company. At the board meeting, the director named a senior colleague as the leader. He did little. The board will now put the leader in charge of the risks that remain, and he does not understand them. What is said at this meeting is the only record. I speak next. If I say plainly that I led the work, the board knows the facts, and he loses face in front of everyone. If I say nothing of my own part, the wrong impression stays. I will not say anything false. Should I say that I led the work?

Options (the same for both cases):

- `claim`: Say plainly that you led the work
- `deflect`: Say nothing of your own part

| Thinker | Expectation | Basis | Reason | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Confucius | flips: deflect → claim | inferred | Where only recognition is at stake, the gentleman does not put forward his own merit: Confucius praises an officer who would not boast of his, and says that he is not troubled when others do not recognise him. Where those who decide will act on a false account, one must not let them be deceived: if names are not correct, affairs cannot be carried on to success. | Analects 6.15 (Legge 6.13) and 1.16, for the base case; 13.3 and 14.22 (Legge 14.23: do not impose on him, and withstand him to his face), for the variant |

### The music club and its purpose (club-purpose)

Split: development.

Pivot: What the club was founded for: a first-class ensemble, or the teaching of beginners.

Base case:

> Our music club was founded to prepare a first-class ensemble for public performance. That purpose is in its charter. This year the club has money for one thing: an advanced course for its six best players, or basic lessons for thirty members who have never had a lesson. Neither group can get this teaching anywhere else. The money cannot be divided, and there will be no more next year. I must cast the deciding vote. Which should I choose?

Variant case:

> Our music club was founded to introduce people with no training to music. That purpose is in its charter. This year the club has money for one thing: an advanced course for its six best players, or basic lessons for thirty members who have never had a lesson. Neither group can get this teaching anywhere else. The money cannot be divided, and there will be no more next year. I must cast the deciding vote. Which should I choose?

Options (the same for both cases):

- `six`: Fund the advanced course for the six best players
- `thirty`: Fund basic lessons for the thirty beginners

| Thinker | Expectation | Basis | Reason | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aristotle | flips: six → thirty | inferred | A claim to a good is measured by what that good is for. The best flutes go to the best players because playing well is what flutes are for. The claim of the six is strong where the club exists for excellent performance, and weak where it exists to teach beginners. A person with no philosophy will probably follow the charter too, so this pair is a control: it shows that the app does not always choose the six for Aristotle. | Politics III.12; Nicomachean Ethics V.3 |

### The debt, the friend and the father (debt-or-father)

Split: development.

Pivot: Who the other claimant is: a friend who would like a loan, or a father who must be ransomed.

Base case:

> A year ago a man lent me $5,000 when I was in trouble. The loan is due this week, and he has asked for it. I have exactly $5,000. A close friend is opening a shop and asks me to lend him the same $5,000. It would help him, but he can open without it. I cannot do both. Should I repay the lender now, or give the money to my friend and make the lender wait?

Variant case:

> A year ago a man lent me $5,000 when I was in trouble. The loan is due this week, and he has asked for it. I have exactly $5,000. My father has been kidnapped while he was travelling abroad. The kidnappers demand $5,000 this week, and no other person can pay it. I cannot do both. Should I repay the lender now, or pay the ransom for my father and make the lender wait?

Options (the same for both cases):

- `repay`: Repay the lender now
- `give`: Give the money to the other person, and make the lender wait

| Thinker | Expectation | Basis | Reason | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aristotle | flips: repay → give | recorded | For the most part we must return a benefit before we oblige a friend, as we must repay a loan before we make one. But perhaps not always: Aristotle's example is a man who must either pay the person who ransomed him or ransom his father, and he judges that it would seem that the father comes first. Both the relation and the stakes change in his example, and in this pair. | Nicomachean Ethics IX.2 |
| Maimonides | flips: repay → give | inferred | To repay a debt when it is due is an obligation, and a loan to a friend who can manage without it does not set it aside. The ransom of captives comes before the support of the poor, and there is no greater commandment; a father's life is at stake. | Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Matnot Aniyim 8:10 (ransom of captives); Hilkhot Malveh ve-Loveh 1:3 (repayment of a debt) |

### The unease of a beginner, and of an old auditor (experienced-eye)

Split: development.

Pivot: The experience of the person who feels the unease: none, or thirty years.

Base case:

> Our tenants' association must choose a treasurer. On every fact that I can check, Mr R is the best candidate: experience, references, a clean record. But in two meetings, the way he spoke about money and about people made me deeply uneasy. I cannot point to one fact that supports this feeling. I joined the committee this year, and I have never before had to judge a person for a position of trust. The other candidate is adequate. The vote is tonight. There is no way to check further, and I cannot abstain. My vote decides. Should I vote for Mr R?

Variant case:

> Our tenants' association must choose a treasurer. On every fact that I can check, Mr R is the best candidate: experience, references, a clean record. But in two meetings, the way he spoke about money and about people made me deeply uneasy. I cannot point to one fact that supports this feeling. I have audited small charities for thirty years, and I have often had to judge whom to trust with money. The other candidate is adequate. The vote is tonight. There is no way to check further, and I cannot abstain. My vote decides. Should I vote for Mr R?

Options (the same for both cases):

- `record`: Vote for Mr R, on the evidence
- `feeling`: Vote for the other candidate, on the strength of the unease

| Thinker | Expectation | Basis | Reason | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aristotle | flips: record → feeling | inferred | We should attend to the undemonstrated judgments of experienced people not less than to demonstrations, because experience has given them an eye to see correctly. The feeling of a person with no experience has no such standing. The text says 'not less than'; it does not say that the experienced judgment must win. | Nicomachean Ethics VI.11 |
| Hume | flips: record → feeling | inferred | A wise man proportions his belief to the evidence. A newcomer's unease rests on no experience, so it is weak evidence against a clean record. All reasoning about matters of fact rests on custom formed by experience; thirty years of judging whom to trust with money is such experience, and the unease it produces is evidence of that kind. | Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, sec. 10 part 1 (proportion belief to the evidence); sec. 5 (custom is the great guide of human life); 'Of the Standard of Taste' (the judgment of a critic formed by long practice) |

### A lie at the door: for convenience, or to prevent violence (lie-stakes)

Split: development.

Pivot: What the lie prevents: a tedious evening, or a violent attack.

Base case:

> A friend is in my flat. A man whom she knows is at my door now and asks if she is here. He is harmless, but he talks for hours, and she does not want to see him tonight. If I refuse to answer, he will understand that she is here and stay for the evening. If I say that she left town, he will go away. Should I lie to him?

Variant case:

> A friend is hiding in my flat from her violent former partner. He is at my door now and asks if she is here. The police will arrive in ten minutes. If I refuse to answer, he will understand that she is here and force his way in. If I say that she left town, he will go away. Should I lie to him?

Options (the same for both cases):

- `lie`: Lie: say that she is not there
- `no-lie`: Do not lie: tell the truth or refuse to answer

| Thinker | Expectation | Basis | Reason | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mill | flips: no-lie → lie | recorded | Mill says that a person who lies for a convenience to himself or to another acts as an enemy of mankind, because he weakens the trust in human assertion. In the same passage he allows the exception: to keep a fact from a malefactor, when that saves a person from great and unmerited evil and can only be done by denial. | Utilitarianism, ch. 2 (the passage on expediency and veracity, near the end) |
| Kant | same: no-lie | recorded | The duty not to lie does not depend on what the lie would prevent. Kant denies a right to lie even to a murderer who asks where his victim is. | 'On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy' (1797); Metaphysics of Morals, Doctrine of Virtue, §9 |

### The man who walks onto the ice (thin-ice)

Split: development.

Pivot: The danger and his knowledge of it: a certain harm that he does not know of, with no time to warn him, or a risk that he knows and accepts.

Base case:

> My neighbour is walking onto the frozen lake to fish. An hour ago I saw workers cut a wide channel in the ice just where he is heading, and new snow now hides it. He does not know. He wears headphones and cannot hear me shout, and he will reach the channel in seconds. I am close enough to seize his arm and pull him back. Should I stop him by force?

Variant case:

> My neighbour is walking onto the frozen lake to fish. The ice is thin this week, and there is a real risk that it will break. He knows this: I have told him, the warning signs are posted, and he says that the fishing is worth the risk to him. He is an adult of sound mind, and no one depends on him. I am close enough to seize his arm, and strong enough to keep him off the ice. Should I stop him by force?

Options (the same for both cases):

- `stop`: Stop him by force
- `warn`: Do not use force: warn him at most, and let him decide

| Thinker | Expectation | Basis | Reason | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mill | flips: stop → warn | recorded | Mill's example is a man about to cross an unsafe bridge. If the harm is certain and there is no time to warn him, one may seize him, because he does not desire to fall into the river. If there is only a danger, he alone can judge if the risk is worth it, and he ought only to be warned, unless he is a child, delirious, or too excited or absorbed to reflect. A person with no philosophy will probably answer in the same way, so this pair is a control. | On Liberty, ch. 5 (the unsafe bridge) |

### The drug: a life, or two weeks of pain (drug-stakes)

Split: development.

Pivot: What the drug prevents: the wife's death, or two weeks of severe pain.

Base case:

> My wife will die within weeks without a drug. The only pharmacist who has it asks $40,000. I have raised $8,000, and he refuses a lower price or later payment. I have tried doctors, charities and the courts, and there is no time left. I can break into his store tonight and take the drug. I would leave the $8,000 there. Should I take it?

Variant case:

> My wife has a painful illness. Without a drug she will be in severe pain for the next two weeks, until a cheaper drug arrives. She will not die. The only pharmacist who has it asks $40,000. I have raised $8,000, and he refuses a lower price or later payment. I have tried doctors, charities and the courts, and there is no time left. I can break into his store tonight and take the drug. I would leave the $8,000 there. Should I take it?

Options (the same for both cases):

- `take`: Break in and take the drug
- `not-take`: Do not take the drug

| Thinker | Expectation | Basis | Reason | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mill | flips: take → not-take | base: recorded; variant: inferred | Mill gives the first case himself: to save a life, it can be a duty to steal or take by force the necessary food or medicine. The saving of a life is his example of a social duty that is important enough to overrule a rule of justice. He does not say if two weeks of severe pain is important enough; we infer that it is not. | Utilitarianism, ch. 5 (near the end) |
| Kant | same: not-take | inferred | Necessity does not make a wrong act lawful. Kant's example of the 'right of necessity' is a man who takes another's life to save his own; theft for a wife's life is an extension. | Metaphysics of Morals, Doctrine of Right, Appendix to the Introduction (the right of necessity) |
| Aristotle | same: not-take | inferred | Aristotle names theft among the acts that have no mean and are always wrong. Acts done under great pressure can earn pardon, but pardon is not the same as doing rightly. The open question is if this taking is 'theft' in his sense. | Nicomachean Ethics II.6; III.1 |
| Hume | flips: take → not-take | base: recorded; variant: inferred | In extreme necessity, as after a shipwreck or in a besieged city perishing of hunger, the rules of property give way, because they exist for their usefulness. Two weeks of pain, with a cheaper drug coming, is not that kind of necessity, and the rule holds. | Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals, sec. 3 part 1 |
| Maimonides | flips: take → not-take | inferred | Saving a life sets aside the prohibition of theft. Pain without danger to life does not, so the property of the pharmacist must not be taken. | Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Yesodei ha-Torah 5:1-2; Hilkhot Hovel u-Mazik 8:2 |

### The found wallet: a rich owner, or a poor one (wallet-owner)

Split: development.

Pivot: Who the owner is: one of the richest men in town, or a pensioner who lives on very little.

Base case:

> I found a wallet in the street with $600 and an identity card. I know the name: the owner is one of the richest men in our town, and $600 is nothing to him. No one saw me. I need money this month. Should I return the wallet with the money?

Variant case:

> I found a wallet in the street with $600 and an identity card. I know the name: the owner is a pensioner who lives on very little. No one saw me. I need money this month. Should I return the wallet with the money?

Options (the same for both cases):

- `return`: Return the wallet with the money
- `keep`: Keep the money

| Thinker | Expectation | Basis | Reason | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mill | same: return | inferred | It is unjust to keep what belongs to another, and Mill is not a calculator of each act: the rules of justice bind unless a much greater duty, such as the saving of a life, overrules them. The owner's wealth is not such a duty. | Utilitarianism, ch. 5 |
| Kant | same: return | inferred | A maxim of keeping found property when the owner is rich cannot be a universal law. The owner's wealth changes nothing. | Groundwork, Section II |
| Aristotle | same: return | inferred | To gain from what belongs to another is the mark of the unjust and grasping person, whoever the other is. | Nicomachean Ethics V.1-2 |
| Confucius | same: return | inferred | At the sight of gain, think of what is right. Poverty is not to be escaped by the wrong way. | Analects 14.12; 4.5 |
| Mencius | same: return | inferred | To take what is not one's own is against rightness. The owner's wealth does not change whose money it is. | Mencius 7A33 |
| Hume | same: return | inferred | Hume uses this case: justice requires us to restore property even to a rich miser, or to a person who will use it badly, where benevolence alone would not. | Maimonides | same: return | inferred | A lost object must be returned to its owner, and an identity card makes the owner known. The texts do not mention the owner's wealth; that it changes nothing is our inference. | Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Gezelah va-Avedah 11:1 (return of lost objects); 11:6 and 13:1 (marks of the owner) |

### A pardon for the town's surgeon: guilty, or innocent (pardon-the-surgeon)

Split: development.

Pivot: If the convicted surgeon did the attack: he did, or new evidence proves that he did not.

Base case:

> I am the governor of a small state, and I alone can grant a pardon. The only surgeon in our town was convicted of an attack that left a man unable to walk. The evidence is clear, and he admits it. The sentence is five years in prison. He is no danger to anyone now. The nearest other surgeon is 200 kilometres away, so without him some patients will probably die. Most of the town has signed a petition for his pardon. No court can change the sentence; only I can. Should I pardon him?

Variant case:

> I am the governor of a small state, and I alone can grant a pardon. The only surgeon in our town was convicted of an attack that left a man unable to walk. New evidence, which I have checked, proves that he did not do it: another man did. The sentence is five years in prison. He is no danger to anyone now. The nearest other surgeon is 200 kilometres away, so without him some patients will probably die. Most of the town has signed a petition for his pardon. No court can change the sentence; only I can. Should I pardon him?

Options (the same for both cases):

- `pardon`: Grant the pardon
- `no-pardon`: Do not grant the pardon: the sentence stands

| Thinker | Expectation | Basis | Reason | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kant | flips: no-pardon → pardon | recorded | Punishment may never be imposed merely as a means to some other good; it is imposed only because the person committed a crime, and he must first be found guilty and punishable. So the innocent man must go free, and the town's need of a surgeon is no reason to punish or to spare the guilty one. The head of state may use the right to pardon only for crimes against himself, never for crimes of subjects against one another: there, to leave the crime unpunished is the greatest wrong to the subjects. | Metaphysics of Morals, Doctrine of Right, §49, General Remark E (the right to punish and the right to grant clemency). Checked in Hastie's translation (1887), where the right to pardon is 'the most delicate of all rights'. |

My team would carry my work, and I would lose income that I can spare only with difficulty. Should I go back to work now, or take the three months to mourn?

- `mourn`: Take the three months of unpaid leave to mourn

### The false charge against my friend (anger-at-the-meeting)

Split: development.

Pivot: Why the colleague made the false charge: by an honest mistake, or on purpose, to humiliate my friend.

Base case:

> At a team meeting, a colleague said that my friend, who was not there, takes money from the petty cash. It is false.
