## 1. Core advice

**Tensions to hold in view.** Several goods pull against each other here, and none dissolves cleanly:

- **Equality vs. need.** Identical limits are procedurally simple and hard to accuse of favoritism, but they treat unequal needs as equal. A household with dialysis or oxygen equipment is not in the same position as one without.
- **Aggregate welfare vs. individual claims.** The farm's employment and harvest may produce the largest total benefit, but that benefit rests on tighter limits for particular households who did nothing to deserve it.
- **Subsistence vs. livelihood.** Residents growing food they depend on and workers depending on farm wages both face real loss, but of different kinds. Neither is mere preference.
- **Uncertainty.** With no reliable forecast, an allocation that looks efficient now may be wasteful if the drought runs long. Irreversible losses (a destroyed harvest, a medical harm) weigh differently from recoverable ones.

**A tiered allocation, treated as provisional:**

1. **First tier: protected baseline.** Guarantee every household enough for drinking, sanitation, and cooking. Add documented medical needs on top of this, verified simply and privately.
2. **Second tier: livelihood and food production.** Allocate to the farm and to household food growers based on evidence, not the farm's own claim alone. Ask what the minimum water is to save the core harvest (for instance, prioritizing perennial crops or the portion that can realistically be saved), and what is lost at each level of reduction.
3. **Third tier: discretionary use.** Lawns, pools, car washing, and ornamental use are cut first and deepest.

**Who bears the greatest sacrifice?** Those whose use is least tied to survival or livelihood, and those best able to absorb loss, which includes the farm's own owners relative to their workers. The farm should receive less than it asks, conditioned on efficiency measures such as drip irrigation, shifting crops, and shared reporting of use, and the owners should carry a meaningful share of the loss rather than passing it entirely to households or workers. Consider wage-protection commitments if the farm gets priority water on the basis of employment.

**Build in revision.** Set review points (weekly or biweekly) tied to reservoir levels, so the allocation changes as conditions do.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans strongly toward outcomes, strongly toward the individual, and moderately toward reason. That fits a pragmatist approach in the tradition of James and Dewey: treat the allocation as an experiment, judge it by how it actually works in people's lives, and revise it.

- **Outcomes:** The tiering aims at minimizing serious harm (medical, food, income loss) instead of honoring equality as a formal rule for its own sake.
- **Individual:** Medical exemptions and household-level food needs get weight as claims of particular people, not as rounding errors in a town total. The farm's value is counted through the individuals whose jobs depend on it, not as an institution's entitlement.
- **Reason:** Verification, minimum-need estimates, and scheduled reviews keep intuition about "who seems deserving" from steering the outcome.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Outcome-reasoning drift.** Aggregate arguments can quietly license sacrificing a few households for a larger sum. Your individual-leaning commitments should act as a check: ask who specifically pays, and whether they can bear it.
- **Self-serving data.** The farm has an interest in overstating harm. Households also have incentives to overstate need. Require evidence from both.
- **Legitimacy.** A sound allocation that residents see as imposed or opaque may collapse through noncompliance. Publish the criteria and reasoning.
- **Conflict of interest.** If you have ties to the farm, its workers, or particular households, disclose them or recuse yourself from those decisions.
- **Irreversibility under uncertainty.** If the drought lengthens, early generosity to the farm cannot be recovered. Prefer staged releases over a single large allocation.
- **Workers vs. owners.** Employment is the strongest argument for the farm, but it only justifies water if the benefit actually reaches workers.
