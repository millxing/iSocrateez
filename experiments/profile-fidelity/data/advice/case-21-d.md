## Core advice

Return the wallet with everything in it, including the cash. You can do this without facing him in person: hand it to the police, drop it in his mailbox or at his office with a short note, or mail it using the address on his ID.

The tensions here are real and shouldn't be flattened:

- **Your need vs. his wealth.** You are two months behind on rent, and he is a landlord who has harmed someone you love. Four hundred dollars means far more to you than to him. A utilitarian does weigh that asymmetry.
- **Desert vs. consequence.** He may deserve some comeuppance, but a stranger's wallet is not an instrument of justice for your sister. Keeping the money would be private retribution, not a measured response to his conduct.
- **Your wellbeing vs. your integrity.** The cash could relieve real pressure. But it could also cost you something less visible: how you see yourself, and your standing to condemn what he did.
- **Who is the loser?** The wallet's contents are not only cash. They include his ID, cards, and possibly documents that are costly to replace. Those are harms to him that bring you no benefit.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

You weigh outcomes heavily, so the question is which option yields the best overall results, not which rule applies. Consider the likely consequences:

- **Keeping it** gives you a short-term gain, but the relief is small relative to a two-month arrears problem. It leaves you carrying a secret and a standing temptation to rationalize future takings. It also confirms the logic that harm to him is fair game, which is a logic that can be turned on you.
- **Returning it without the cash** is the worst hybrid. It still harms him, and it adds the risk that you are identified as the one who took it. It also reads as theft with a veneer of justice.
- **Returning it intact** costs you $400 you never had and spares him disruption. It preserves your credibility, which matters if you ever want to press for fair treatment for your sister or yourself.

Your balance between individual and collective concerns points the same way: a norm of returning found property sustains the trust that everyone, including vulnerable renters, relies on. Your balance between reason and intuition matters too. Your anger is a legitimate signal that he has wronged people, but it should not be the thing that decides what you do with property that isn't his to forfeit and isn't yours to take.

## What to watch for

- **Motivated reasoning.** "He deserves it" and "I need it more" are each partly true, which makes them persuasive. Notice if you're building a case for the outcome you already want.
- **Treating your hardship as separate from this decision.** Your rent problem is real and deserves its own solution. If you return the wallet, don't leave the arrears unaddressed.
- **Anonymity as the deciding factor.** That nobody saw you changes the risk, not the ethics. It is worth asking what you would do if someone were watching, and whether that answer is the one you actually endorse.
- **Resentment.** Returning the wallet will probably sting. That's compatible with doing the right thing and doesn't mean you chose wrongly.
