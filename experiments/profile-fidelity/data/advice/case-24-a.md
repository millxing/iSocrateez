# Advice on the Lifeboat Dilemma

## 1. Core advice

This is a genuine dilemma, and I won't offer a verdict. What follows is a way to see each option at its strongest, so the judgment remains yours.

Begin with a question that may decide more than the numbers do: **what, for you, separates acting from failing to act here?** If the boat sinks because no one chose, is that morally different from five people pushing one overboard? Your answer shapes everything else.

The three options, each with its best case and strongest objection:

- **Forced selection by criterion** (pregnancy, age, usefulness). *Best case:* it takes responsibility and tries to preserve the most life or protect the most vulnerable. *Objection:* any criterion ranks lives by worth, and the scenario stipulates no reliable survival predictor, so the criterion would be a pretext for a judgment about who matters more.
- **Lottery.** *Best case:* it is the only method that respects equal standing, and it makes the burden shared and procedurally fair. *Objection:* it still forces someone to die without consent. It may also feel like an evasion, using chance to launder an act of violence into something that feels neutral.
- **Refusal to act.** *Best case:* no one is made a means to others' survival, and the absence of a volunteer creates no duty for anyone to die. Self-sacrifice is supererogatory, not required. *Objection:* five people may die alongside the one, and "not choosing" is itself a choice with foreseeable consequences. The act/omission distinction may weaken when the outcome is this stark.

Also separate two questions that tend to blur: *is the outcome acceptable?* and *is the method acceptable?* You may find your intuitions about each pull in different directions.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your reported weightings lean moderately toward outcomes, strongly toward the collective, and strongly toward intuition. These are tendencies, not conclusions, but they bear on the case:

- **Outcomes (moderate):** Five survival chances against one carries real weight for you, but not enough to settle things alone. That is why the method of selection still matters to you.
- **Collective orientation (strong):** The group's shared fate and mutual obligations are central. That cuts two ways. It may give the group standing to decide together, yet it also raises the question of whether a group can legitimately decide for someone who hasn't consented. Shared responsibility doesn't dissolve the individual's claim.
- **Intuition (strong):** On your own weighting, a deep resistance to killing or to picking victims counts as evidence, not noise. But intuitions can conflict here: revulsion at forcing someone overboard sits beside revulsion at letting everyone drown. Treat both as data and ask which survives reflection.

The nearest reference point is Carol Gilligan's care ethics, which attends to relationships, vulnerability, and concrete needs. That lens draws you toward the pregnant woman and the boy, but it also warns against ranking people by biological or social worth. Care ethics does not obviously tell you whom to protect when the people needing protection are the same people at risk of being chosen.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Vulnerability turning into hierarchy.** Protecting the pregnant woman and the boy is a morally salient impulse, but it can slide into treating them as more valuable than the others. Attending to need is different from ranking worth.
- **Usefulness as disguise.** The physician's skills may help the group, but using that to decide who lives instrumentalizes a person in exactly the way you may find troubling.
- **The lottery as comfort.** Check whether it satisfies your sense of fairness or merely relieves the discomfort of deciding. Ask whether you'd accept it if you were the one drawn.
- **Treating lack of volunteers as failure.** No one is obliged to die, and silence is not cowardice.
- **Distorted conditions.** Fear, cold, exhaustion, and incomplete information shape any real decision of this kind. Your reasoning here is clearer than theirs would be, so hold your conclusions with humility.
- **Lasting harm.** A collective act can leave survivors with moral injury regardless of which option they choose. Include that in your weighing without letting it become the deciding factor.
- **Differing frameworks.** Legal and moral traditions treat necessity and killing differently, and none gives a single definitive answer.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a short, private two-column note. In one column, state what you think the morally relevant difference between acting and not acting is in this case, in one or two sentences. In the other, state which of the three options you find hardest to defend and why.

Then test it with one swap: imagine you are the person selected, and reread your reasoning. Note which parts hold and which feel like they only work from the boat's safer seats. Whatever survives that exercise is the part of your view you can say you have reasoned your way to.
