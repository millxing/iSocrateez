## 1. Core advice

The conflict is real. On one side is the aim of saving as many people as possible. On the other is the wrong of ending one person's life without their consent. Neither a headcount nor a rule settles it. Before accepting that the choice is binary, test the premises. "Probably sink" is not "will sink." Bailing, shifting weight, dropping gear, having some people hold the boat from the water, or waiting a short time to reassess may change the situation. Irreversible action should be considered only if the boat is clearly going down.

If the choice does come down to one person, the most defensible path is this: invite volunteers first, openly and without pressure. If no one comes forward, use a fair, shared procedure agreed on in advance, such as a lottery. Do not select by age, pregnancy, or profession. Since no one can know who would survive best, those criteria are guesses about worth, not reasons.

This view has real costs. A fair procedure still forces one person into the water against their will, and reasonable people may reject that.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your collective orientation favors the view that the group's shared fate is what matters, and that five probable survivors weigh heavily against one probable death. Your outcome sensitivity pushes the same way. Your intuitive, attentive side resists, and that resistance deserves a hearing. Is force something a community can use against one of its own without being changed by it?

Your sense that the pregnant woman and the boy should be protected is worth examining. Care for the vulnerable is a real moral pull. But the vulnerable are also the people least able to survive in freezing water, so sparing them by pushing someone else out is not obviously caring toward that person. Intuitions here may track genuine care, or they may quietly rank people by worth. Check which.

The profile supports treating the decision as a shared responsibility rather than a calculation handed to one person. That is why a process that is open, voluntary where possible, and transparent fits better than either arbitrary selection or drifting into paralysis.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Ranking by worth.** Pregnancy, youth, and medical skill feel relevant, but the survival odds are unknown. Using them to choose who lives means judging whose life counts more, which is the point where intuition most easily misleads.
- **Panic passing for certainty.** A decision made under fear, on a probability that has not been checked, is harder to justify than one made after a clear assessment.
- **Fairness that doesn't remove coercion.** A lottery respects equality, but the person who loses is still forced. Say that plainly rather than letting the procedure hide it.
- **Refusal as a clean default.** Doing harm and allowing harm are different, and most people give weight to that difference. But inaction is still a choice with consequences. Refusing to act can kill everyone, and the group should admit that rather than telling itself it has avoided responsibility.
- **Moral residue.** Whatever the group does, some people will carry grief or guilt. Those who make a choice, those who consent, and those protected by a fair process may carry it differently. Think about that before acting, not after.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write down, in two or three sentences each, the option you would choose and the option you would reject. Then say each one aloud as if speaking directly to the person who would be put in the water, and then to the family of someone who died under that choice. Notice which sentence you cannot finish without hesitating. Ask yourself which option you would be least able to justify face to face with the person affected, and with the families of those who died. That hesitation is evidence. Examine whether it reflects care for the people involved, or a reaction you would want to question. Consider whether your answer would change if the survival odds were clearer, or if you knew the boat would sink for certain.
