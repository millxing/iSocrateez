## Core advice

Your tension is real, and neither option is clean. Walking out risks harm to identifiable patients this week. Staying in means continuing to work within a system you believe is already injuring patients, and weakening a action meant to change it. Neither choice gets you out of responsibility.

I lean toward this: **join the strike, but only after you've tested whether the harm to this week's patients can be reduced, and be prepared to own the remaining risk.** That means finding out whether the union has agreed emergency or life-preserving cover, whether managers have contingency plans, and whether you can contribute safely to those arrangements (for example through a structured handover or by volunteering for agreed cover). If cover is credible, the case for striking strengthens. If the ward would be left plainly unsafe and no cover exists, the case for staying, or for a partial form of participation, becomes much stronger.

Several tensions should stay in view:

- **Identifiable vs. statistical patients.** This week's patients are concrete to you. Future patients harmed by chronic understaffing are real but abstract. Abstraction doesn't make them matter less, but it does make the pull toward staying feel stronger than it may deserve.
- **Acts vs. omissions.** Striking feels like causing harm. But if understaffing is already causing harm, staying is not neutral either.
- **Uncertain payoff.** You don't know that the strike will succeed, or that it will succeed soon enough to offset short-term costs.
- **Your conscience vs. the collective vote.** The union's decision is not a verdict you must obey, and your colleagues crossing the line don't settle the matter for you either.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

You weigh outcomes moderately and give strong weight to individual judgment. From an existentialist angle, the question is not "what did the union decide?" or "what do patients need?" as if either answered it for you. You are choosing, and the choice defines what kind of nurse and person you are.

Sartre's warning about bad faith applies in both directions. "The union decided, so I had no choice" is an evasion. So is "my patients need me" if it is a shield against the discomfort of acting on your beliefs. Beauvoir's emphasis on freedom entangled with others' freedom matters too: your choice affects colleagues who strike, colleagues who cross, and patients who cannot choose at all. Your outcomes-leaning side asks you to take the consequences seriously rather than treating the strike as a purely symbolic act of integrity.

## What to watch for

- **Rationalization.** Notice which option you're drawn to because it's easier socially or emotionally, and test your reasoning against that.
- **Moralizing colleagues.** Resist contempt for those who cross the line, or for those who strike. People are weighing the same conflict differently.
- **Unexamined facts.** Don't decide on assumptions about cover, notice periods, or legal and professional obligations. Check them with the union and your professional body's guidance.
- **Professional duty.** Your obligations around safe handover and not abandoning patients mid-care are constraints on how you strike, not just on whether you do.
- **Residual guilt.** Whatever you choose, some cost will remain. That doesn't mean you chose wrongly.

## A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Ask your union rep and your ward manager two questions: *What life-preserving cover is planned for this ward during the strike?* and *What would safe handover look like for my current patients?* Then write down, in a few sentences, the conditions under which you'd strike, the conditions under which you'd stay, and which one the facts support. Let that decision rest on what you find rather than on pressure from either side.
