## Core advice

Treat this as a question of how to distribute harm fairly, not as a choice between equal limits and favoring the farm. Use a layered allocation:

1. **Floor (non-negotiable):** Every household gets enough water for drinking, cooking, and sanitation, plus verified medical needs such as dialysis or infant care. These harms are severe, often irreversible, and fall on identifiable people, so they come first.
2. **Household food growing:** A capped allocation for kitchen gardens that provide real food, scaled to household size and reviewed regularly.
3. **Farm:** Neither a blank check nor an automatic cut. Ask the farm to document its water needs: what minimum volume preserves what share of the harvest, and whether losses rise gradually or hit a cliff at some threshold. Allocate in stages, favoring the most water-efficient or highest-value plantings, and tie the allocation to verified figures.
4. **Discretionary use** (lawns, pools, non-essential fills, including by businesses and wealthier households): cut first and deepest.
5. **Reserve and review:** Hold back a reserve, set review dates, and define triggers tied to reservoir levels so the scheme can be revised as rain does or does not arrive.

The strongest consequence-based case is probably a differentiated, evidence-checked, reviewable scheme in which the farm receives priority only under conditions: guaranteed wages for workers, public disclosure of its water use, deeper cuts for its owners' discretionary uses, and food sharing with the town.

The opposing considerations are real. Equal limits are easy to understand, hard to capture, and do not depend on trusting anyone's forecast. Extra water for the farm shifts costs onto households that did not choose the risk, and the farm's claim about its harvest is a forecast the town cannot easily check. Reasonable people may weigh the farm's irreversible losses differently from the diffuse but real burden on households.

On who bears the greatest sacrifice, the answer depends on the principle you adopt:
- **Capacity to absorb loss:** high-use consumers, owners, and discretionary users bear the most.
- **Protection of basic needs:** the burden falls on the margins of ordinary households, never on the floor.
- **Shared proportional cuts:** everyone, including the farm, takes the same percentage reduction. This is simple but can hit households with the least slack hardest.
- **Conditional priority:** the farm shares the burden by accepting conditions that protect workers and the town.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

Your emphasis on outcomes shows up in the focus on what actually happens to particular people: the dialysis patient, the family whose garden feeds them, the worker whose wage disappears. Your concern for individuals is why the design refuses to sacrifice identifiable people for an abstraction like "the town" or "the economy," while still recognizing that workers' livelihoods are individual stakes on the farm's side. Your preference for reasoned analysis is why the farm's claim is treated as a hypothesis to test rather than accepted or dismissed.

## What to watch for

- **Unverified claims on both sides.** Ask the farm for data and seek an independent check, without assuming bad faith. Medical claims need verification too. Verification should not erode the floor, but it also should not be waved through.
- **Conflicts of interest.** If anyone deciding the allocation owns land, holds a stake in the farm, or has a close tie to it, disclose it and step back from that decision.
- **Legitimacy.** An unequal allocation can be resented even when it is correct. Publish the criteria and the data.
- **Cliff effects.** A small cut may be harmless while crossing a threshold destroys the crop. Find that threshold before setting limits.
- **Drift.** Temporary measures often become permanent. Build in sunset clauses.
- **Front-loading.** Generous early allocation to the farm can exhaust reserves if the drought lasts.
