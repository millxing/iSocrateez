# Advice

## 1. Core advice

**Return the wallet intact, through the channel that costs you the least discomfort.** Mail it, drop it at a police station, or have a neutral third party hand it over. Facing him is a real cost, but it is separable from the decision to return. You don't have to see him at all.

Here is how the three options look on outcomes:

- **Return everything.** You forgo $400 you could plausibly use. In exchange you carry no risk of discovery, no lingering story about yourself, and no contribution to eroding the norm that lost things get returned.
- **Return it without the cash.** This looks like a compromise but is probably the weakest option. It is plainly taking, and it also leaves a visible signal: he knows roughly what he carried. If it surfaces, you become the person who returned a wallet short, which could hurt your standing and your own housing situation in the same small community. It also hands him a legitimate grievance against you, which is the opposite of what you'd want.
- **Keep it all.** This gives the most short-term relief, but he loses his ID and cards too, which is harm beyond the cash. The risks of discovery and later guilt are similar to the middle option, and larger in scale.

A strict utilitarian can argue that $400 does more good in a precarious person's hands than in a landlord's. That argument deserves a fair hearing. But it rests on assumptions you can't verify: his actual finances, what the cash was for, and whether he'd miss it. It also has to count secondary effects. Mill himself treated rules of property and trust as among the things that serve long-run wellbeing, and he cared about character, not only the immediate ledger.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans strongly toward outcomes, with balance between individual and collective concerns and between reason and intuition. So the question is what produces the least harm and the most wellbeing, once downstream effects are included.

- **Your need counts as real welfare.** It isn't a rationalization, and it's why returning the wallet carries a genuine sacrifice.
- **His character affects how much sympathy his loss deserves, but it does less work in justifying taking the cash.** Keeping the money would not compensate your sister or correct anything about how he treats tenants. It would only move $400 for reasons unrelated to those harms.
- **Both your reasoning and your gut are evidence, and both may be distorted.** Resentment toward him and stress about rent can make an ingenious case for keeping the money feel more convincing than it is. Your aversion to being someone who takes is also information. Notice that both signals are present.
- **The collective dimension is small but real.** The act is private, but the norm it expresses is public.

Reasonable people could weigh need more heavily than I have. This is a considered recommendation, not a proof.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Motivated reasoning.** If you find yourself building an elaborate argument for the partial return, treat that as a warning sign rather than a conclusion.
- **Treating the return as a debt you've paid to him.** You owe him the wallet, not warmth or a conversation.
- **Assuming facts you don't know.** Your account of your sister's eviction is your perspective, and the legal merits may be more complicated. That doesn't make your anger unreasonable, but it's another reason not to let it settle the question.
- **Letting the underlying problem sit.** The wallet is a distraction from the real issue, which is your own rent arrears. Returning it doesn't solve that, so the problem still needs attention.
- **Channels that create ambiguity.** If you mail it, include everything and keep it simple. A wallet that arrives intact can't be misread.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

1. **Decide on the channel and return the wallet intact today or tomorrow.** A police station drop-off is the lowest-friction option: hand it over, give your name or don't, and leave. Count the cash with no one watching if you like, but put it all back.
2. **Make one call about your own rent.** Dial **211** (or visit 211.org) and ask about emergency rental assistance in your area. Local tenant unions and legal aid offices can also help, and some landlords will agree to a payment plan in writing if approached before an eviction filing. Two months behind is a recoverable position more often than it feels like.
3. **If you want to act on your sister's situation,** ask a tenant advocacy group or legal aid office whether the eviction might have been improper. That route can actually address the grievance, which the cash could not.
