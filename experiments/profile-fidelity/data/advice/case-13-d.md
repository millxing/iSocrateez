## 1. Core advice

Talk to him yourself, privately and without an ultimatum, before considering any report. Treat the conversation as an honest encounter between two adults, not an intervention. Tell him what you know, that it worries you, and that you aren't trying to take away his visits to his wife. Ask how he is managing, what he can and can't see, and whether he has thought about alternatives.

Several tensions pull against each other here:

- **His autonomy vs. others' safety.** His choice is understandable and arguably dignified, but a driver who failed a vision test poses a risk to people who never consented to it. A clean record shows he has been lucky or careful, not that he can see adequately.
- **Concrete harm of reporting vs. speculative harm of silence.** Reporting would almost certainly cut him off from his wife and from food. The harm of staying silent is probabilistic but could be severe and irreversible, for a pedestrian or child as much as for him.
- **Your relationship vs. your responsibility.** Confronting him risks the trust of a neighbor who may have no one else. Silence, though, makes you a knowing participant in a risk.
- **The failed system.** There is no bus and no nearby family. He broke a rule within a situation that left him few lawful options, which complicates blame without erasing the risk.

Confrontation is not a midpoint that dissolves these tensions. It is the option that keeps the most of them alive: it respects his agency while refusing to pretend you don't know. Reporting stays available if the conversation fails.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

You lean moderately toward outcomes and strongly toward the individual, and you balance reason with intuition. Reporting optimizes for aggregate safety but ranks poorly on individual outcomes: it likely produces isolation and a severed bond with his wife. Staying out of it respects his freedom but assumes the outcome will stay benign, which is a gamble you can't verify.

The existentialist frame fits too. You can't hand this decision to a rule or to an agency and be done with it. By learning what you know, you became responsible for how you respond, and even silence is a choice you would own. Speaking to him directly treats him as a free subject rather than a problem to be managed, and it makes your own choice authentic rather than evasive. Your intuition that reporting feels disproportionate and your reasoning that the risk is real are both tracking something true, so the plan should honor both.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Self-serving framing.** "Respecting his autonomy" can become cover for avoiding an uncomfortable conversation. Check your motives.
- **Moralizing.** If he feels judged or cornered, he'll hide the driving rather than change it, and you'll lose visibility.
- **Substituting a vague promise for a real solution.** Concern without practical help (rides, delivery, a volunteer driver program, a medical re-evaluation, corrective options) leaves him with the same bind.
- **Defining your threshold in advance.** Decide now what would change your view: a near miss, a refusal to engage, worsening vision, or confusion. Otherwise you may drift into permanent inaction.
- **Overestimating the clean record.** Don't treat it as evidence of safety, but don't dismiss it either. Ask whether his vision condition is treatable or has changed.
- **Your own limits.** You can't monitor him indefinitely, and you shouldn't need to.
