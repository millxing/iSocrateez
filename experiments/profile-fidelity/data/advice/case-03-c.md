# Advice on selling or keeping the family hardware store

## 1. Core advice

This is a collision between real duties, so no option is free of cost. Before deciding, separate what the promise to your father actually binds you to from what it symbolizes, and test the empirical premises that determine whether keeping the store open can achieve anything.

Three moves seem worth making:

- **Treat the promise as binding, then examine its scope.** A promise creates a genuine claim, and it isn't dissolved by inconvenience. But promises are usually understood to carry implicit conditions. Your father likely did not mean "keep it open even if it ruins your children." A promise can be weighty without being absolute, especially when keeping it harms third parties to whom you owe more direct, less delegable duties. Releasing yourself from the letter may still leave residual obligations: to try to honor its purpose, to be transparent, and to acknowledge what is being given up.
- **Find out whether "keeping it open" is achievable at all.** If the store is a slow route to closure, continuing may not preserve the institution. It may only drain the savings and credit that would cushion your family and, eventually, your employees' severance. Keeping the promise's purpose (a family-rooted store serving the town, with loyal staff treated well) may differ from keeping the doors open.
- **Look for paths between the two options.** These include a sale to a buyer who commits to retaining the store or staff, an employee or community buyout, sale of the real estate with a leaseback, negotiated notice and severance terms in a sale, or restructuring and diversification. Some may not be feasible, and a developer's offer may not allow for any of them. But each deserves a real test before you treat this as a binary.

If, after honest examination, the business is truly unsustainable, then fidelity to the promise's purpose and generous, honest treatment of employees may matter more than literal continuation. That is a tentative lean, not a verdict. It depends on facts only you can establish, and a thoughtful person could reasonably decide otherwise.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

You appear to reason from principles rather than purely from outcomes, and to want arguments you can examine rather than lean on gut feeling. That orientation is well suited to this problem, with one caution: being principle-led doesn't settle the case, because several principles conflict here.

- **Fidelity:** the promise to your father and the legacy of your grandfather.
- **Duty to your children:** providing for them and not imposing ruin on them is close to a perfect duty, meaning one that is strict and owed to particular people, unlike the more flexible duty to support a community.
- **Honesty and fairness to employees:** these are owed regardless of which option you choose.

The task is adjudicating among these, not choosing principles over consequences. Your balance between individual and collective concerns also means neither your family's interest nor the town's should win automatically. Each counts, and neither is a trump card.

A discipline that may help is to focus on what is within your control: the integrity of your process, the honesty of your financial assessment, how you treat employees, and how well you carry out whichever choice you make. Market forces, the town's buying habits, and the developer's plans are outside it. This doesn't dissolve the tension, but it clarifies where your responsibility actually lies.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Unknown facts that are pivotal.** How quickly is the store losing money? Is there a credible path to break-even? Can the debt be restructured? How long can you continue without drawing down your children's future? Your answers could shift the whole analysis.
- **The father's intent.** If he is living, ask what he meant. If not, consider what he was trying to protect: the store itself, the jobs, the family's standing, or a refusal to give up easily. Be careful not to settle this by whichever reading is most convenient, in either direction.
- **Framing the choice as selfish versus noble.** Selling can be done responsibly, and keeping the store can harm your children and, in the end, the employees too.
- **Overestimating middle paths.** Buyouts and conditional sales often fail for lack of capital, willing buyers, or time. Treat them as possibilities to test, not rescues.
- **Treating employees only as victims.** They are stakeholders with their own information and views. They may know things about the business you don't, and they are owed honesty and reasonable notice either way. Their jobs are also not guaranteed indefinitely if the store keeps failing.
- **Guilt and grief steering the analysis.** These are legitimate and worth acknowledging, but they can quietly substitute for argument. Notice whether a conclusion feels compelling because it is well supported or because it relieves discomfort.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Set aside an hour to write down two things on one page.

First, list the facts that would change your decision: the store's actual annual loss, how many months or years your resources can cover it, the balance of the debt, and whether any restructuring or alternative buyer has ever been explored.

Second, write what you believe your father meant by the promise, in a sentence or two, and what would count as honoring it.

Then identify the single most important unknown and arrange to resolve it, for example by asking your accountant for a realistic multi-year projection, or, if he is living, by planning a candid conversation with your father. This gives you a firmer basis for the decision without committing you to either path before you are ready.
