## Core advice

Tell her the truth, but shape it around the actual decision she is facing. She is asking you to read a book, yet what hangs on your reading is whether to quit her job and spend down her remaining savings. Honest feedback is owed to her precisely because she has told you your opinion matters most.

Several tensions pull against each other here:

- **Honesty vs. care.** Encouragement would be kind now and potentially costly later. Bluntness could be truthful and still wound a fragile person more than it informs her.
- **Her autonomy vs. your influence.** It is her life and her risk to take. But autonomy depends on accurate information, and she has given you the role of a trusted reader. Withholding your judgment would quietly make the choice for her.
- **Your fallibility vs. your duty to speak.** You may be wrong. Taste is not market prediction, and plenty of poor-seeming books sell while good ones don't. This argues for humility in how you speak, not for silence.
- **Her present fragility vs. her future wellbeing.** Waiting for a "better time" may mean she acts before she hears you.

Separate the two judgments you are conflating: *the book's quality* and *the wisdom of quitting her job*. You can be honest about the first, specific and non-fatalistic, while treating the second as something she should decide with open eyes rather than something you veto.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

You balance outcomes and principles, and this case rewards both: honesty is a principle worth keeping, and the consequences of false encouragement (lost income, deeper disillusionment later, damage to trust) are real. Your lean toward the individual and toward intuition suggests that your sense of what she can bear and what the friendship requires is worth trusting, within reason.

The existentialist frame is useful. Sartre would call praise you don't believe a form of bad faith, one that treats her as a fragile object to be managed rather than a free agent who can choose. Beauvoir's emphasis on mutual freedom points the same way: respecting her means giving her the real materials for her choice, then letting the choice be hers. Authenticity here is not harshness. It is refusing to deceive her while remaining present.

## What to watch for

- **Disguising a verdict as a question.** Vague hedging ("it's really... interesting") can be worse than either honesty or praise. She will read it anyway.
- **Overstating certainty.** "It won't sell" is a prediction; "here is where it lost me" is an observation. Stay with what you actually know.
- **Letting honesty become dominance.** You are not trying to stop her. If she goes ahead anyway, the friendship should survive that.
- **Ignoring real strengths.** If there are things that work, naming them is accuracy, not cushioning.
- **Her fragility.** Gauge whether she is in crisis. If she seems at real risk, put support first and the manuscript conversation second, though not indefinitely.
- **Your own motives.** Check for envy, avoidance of conflict, or a wish to protect yourself from her disappointment.

## A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Tell her you've finished (or will finish) the book and ask for a conversation in person or by phone, not a text. Before it, write down three specific things that work, the two or three biggest problems you see, and the one message you most need her to hear: *separate the question of whether to keep writing from the question of whether to quit your job now.* Then open with something like: "I want to give you my real reading, because you asked me and because I take it seriously. Some of it is hard. Is now a good time?" That gives her a moment of consent and a chance to prepare, without delaying the truth.
