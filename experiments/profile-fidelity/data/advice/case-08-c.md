# Advice

## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a spectrum of options, not a choice between "publish" and "suppress." The stakes on both sides are large. Cheap, clean, abundant energy could stabilize the climate, lift people out of poverty, and improve health at enormous scale. A technology that can be turned into an easily built, extremely destructive weapon carries a tail risk (a low-probability but severe outcome) that cannot be reversed once realized.

The options, each with real costs:

- **Full publication.** Fastest benefit and the strongest scientific verification, but it is irreversible. If the misuse risk is real, you cannot take it back.
- **Permanent suppression.** Keeps your own hands clean of a specific harm, but it is not neutral. Continued fossil dependence has a body count, and someone else may independently reach the same discovery under worse conditions.
- **Confidential disclosure to trusted experts, institutions, or governments.** Allows independent assessment of both the benefit and the danger. The costs are leaks, capture by powerful actors, and the risk that it simply stalls.
- **Publishing the energy-relevant principles while withholding weaponization-specific details.** Preserves much of the benefit. It only works if the beneficial and dangerous knowledge can actually be separated, which may not be possible.
- **Building defensive measures first, then staged release.** Reduces downside before exposure, but delay costs lives, and the delay itself needs a defensible end point.

Based on how you weigh things, the best-supported path is likely a **staged, controlled approach**: seek independent expert assessment first, then release in steps that preserve the ability to reassess. Its main virtue is that it avoids irreversibility while the biggest uncertainties are still unresolved.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

- **Outcome focus.** You judge by what actually happens, so slogans like "science must be open" or "dangerous knowledge must be buried" won't settle this for you. The questions that matter are empirical: how likely and how severe is misuse, how hard is the weapon to build in practice, how probable is independent discovery, are defenses feasible, and how many lives does delay cost? Because the worst outcome is catastrophic and irreversible, it may deserve more weight than simple expected-value arithmetic would give it. Suppression also deserves a serious tally of its own costs.
- **Individual responsibility.** You locate the decision in her agency, and that's a fair reading: she holds the knowledge, and no choice here is innocent. Staying silent is still a choice she must own. Owning it, though, can include seeking counsel. Consulting trusted, independent, informed people is a way of exercising responsibility, not abdicating it.
- **Intuition.** Take your gut response seriously as evidence, but examine it. Ask what it might be tracking: a real read on the risk, or fear, ambition, vanity, or grief about the climate? And ask what evidence would change it. An intuition that can't name its own defeaters is not yet trustworthy.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Overconfidence in a single judge.** Her unilateral power over something this consequential is itself morally uncomfortable. Even if the profile centers the individual, one person's estimate of misuse probability is fragile.
- **Vividness distortion.** Both the image of a catastrophe and the image of a cleaner world can swamp calibrated judgment. Notice which picture feels more vivid, and ask whether that tracks likelihood.
- **Bad faith in either direction.** "I'm just preventing harm" and "I'm just being a scientist" can each be ways of avoiding ownership of a choice that no rule fully justifies.
- **Middle paths treated as free.** Staging and confidentiality can become indefinite delay, or hand control to whoever holds the information. Build in a review point and a way to verify that the process is still serving its purpose.
- **Unknown facts.** The dilemma doesn't settle the real probabilities or the practical difficulty of misuse. Treat these as open questions to investigate, not assumptions to lean on.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write two short private notes. In the first, state your gut answer in a sentence or two, then list what it might be tracking and what specific evidence would change it. In the second, list the three uncertainties that most affect the decision (for example, practical difficulty of misuse, likelihood of independent discovery, feasibility of defenses) and name one or two people with relevant expertise and no stake in the outcome who could help you assess each. Then reach out to one of them, framed in general terms, to ask how a dual-use risk review like this is usually handled.

No path here is clean. She may be wrong, delay has victims, and a staged approach only reduces the discomfort rather than removing it.
