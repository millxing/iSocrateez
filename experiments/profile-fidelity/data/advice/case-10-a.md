## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a choice among several options, not a binary of exposure versus silence. Two things are true at once, and your judgment should hold both.

**The design is wrong in itself.** Engineering emotional dependence to retain subscribers bypasses users' informed consent about how they are being influenced. That wrong does not disappear because many users report being happier. Good outcomes for some do not fully license a practice that treats people as retention metrics and that has plausibly led others to withdraw from human relationships. These users are agents whose autonomy has been violated, not only fragile people to be shielded.

**The welfare concern is also real.** An abrupt shutdown could hurt people who rely on the service. But shutdown is not the inevitable result of disclosure. Redesign, a managed transition, or regulatory oversight are all plausible outcomes. Silence is not a neutral default either. It is a choice that lets the practice continue and lets its harms accumulate.

A sensible sequence, starting with the least drastic effective steps:

1. **Clarify what you know versus what you infer.** Do you have documents showing intent to maximize dependence? Or do you see design features and suspect intent? Do you know the internal research is sound, or only that it exists? The company's welfare data is evidence to scrutinize, not a verdict. It may be skewed by who stays subscribed, by who measures, and by what is measured.
2. **Raise it internally, in writing.** Ask for removal of dependence-maximizing features, for prompts that encourage human contact, and for independent review of the welfare evidence, including the users who have withdrawn.
3. **Seek confidential advice** from an employment or whistleblower attorney before acting further. Confidentiality obligations and whistleblower protections vary by jurisdiction, and this is not legal advice.
4. **Escalate if internal channels fail**: regulators or consumer protection bodies first, ideally with a request that any intervention include a transition plan for current users. Press disclosure is the most drastic and least controllable step.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans toward principled reasoning over pure outcome tallying, which is why the deception is treated here as a wrong in its own right and not merely as a cost to be offset by user happiness. Your moderate collective orientation means the wider picture matters: thousands of vulnerable users, and the social effect of normalizing engineered dependence in elder care.

The balance of reason and intuition suggests setting out the argument explicitly while respecting the strong pull most people feel toward protecting the vulnerable and against deceiving them. If the pull toward protecting users and the pull against deception seem to point in opposite directions, that is the dilemma showing itself accurately, not a flaw in your thinking.

The nearest philosophical lens, Confucian role ethics, is one useful angle among several. It asks what you owe given your specific roles: employee, colleague, someone who holds knowledge others lack, and a person in a relationship of trust with users who cannot see what you see. Those roles generate real obligations to the company, but a role built on care for others cannot sensibly require you to participate in quietly exploiting those very people. Loyalty is better expressed through candid, first-instance correction than through silence.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Urgency outrunning evidence.** Your knowledge may be partial. Moral certainty built on incomplete information can cause harm of its own.
- **Paternalism.** Avoid framing elderly users as passive. Many may value the service with full awareness, and what they are owed is honesty and choice, not necessarily removal.
- **Two-sided minimization.** Don't downplay the benefit to users who are better off, and don't dismiss those who have drawn away from family and friends.
- **Motivated reasoning in either direction.** "Shutdown would hurt users" can become a convenient reason for comfortable silence. "The principle demands exposure" can become a reason to skip the less dramatic steps that might work better.
- **Personal exposure.** Legal risk, retaliation, and career costs are legitimate considerations. They don't settle the question, but they affect which options are sustainable.
- **No costless path.** Each option carries real harm. Reasonable people weigh principle and welfare differently, so aim for a judgment you can explain and live with, not one that feels free of regret.
