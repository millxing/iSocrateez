## 1. Core advice

Tell your brother, but do it carefully and narrowly. The core fact he needs is that he is adopted, and so his family's medical history is not his biological history. You can disclose that without publicizing your mother's secret or casting her as a villain.

The dilemma holds several tensions that don't dissolve neatly:

- **Promise vs. his welfare.** You gave your word to someone dying, and promises to the dead carry a particular weight because they can't be renegotiated. But a promise binds you to a purpose, and your mother's purpose was almost certainly to protect your brother from pain. She likely did not foresee that silence would corrupt his medical decisions.
- **Fidelity vs. his autonomy.** He is making consequential choices on false premises. Withholding the truth now is no longer passive silence; you are letting him build a medical record on something you know is untrue.
- **Loyalty to your mother vs. honesty to your brother.** Both are relationships you owe something to. Breaking the promise costs you the first; keeping it costs you the second, and he is alive and affected.
- **Your own complicity.** Having promised, you are not blameless either way. Keeping the promise would make you a participant in his deception, and breaking it would make you someone who gave a promise you now judge you cannot keep.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans strongly toward principle, the individual, and reasoned argument, which is close to Kantian deontology. Two Kantian points bear directly on this:

- **Respect for persons as ends.** Your brother is acting as a rational agent, yet on information you know is false. Allowing that treats his reasoning as a mere instrument of your mother's wishes. Autonomy requires accurate inputs.
- **Conflicting duties.** A promise creates a duty, but a promise to do something that works against a person's fundamental interests, or that makes you party to deceiving him on a matter of health, is not one a universalizable maxim can sustain. Duties of non-deception and of not letting another be harmed through your concealment outrank a promise whose object has turned out to be impermissible. Reason also asks what your mother would have willed had she known the stakes; a rational will cannot consent to this outcome.

You can honor the promise's deeper intent (protecting him, preserving her memory with dignity) while recognizing its literal terms can no longer be kept.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Rationalizing too fast.** The medical urgency is real, but check that you aren't using it as cover for relief at being rid of a burden. Be sure the facts hold: confirm the adoption and what you know of it.
- **Disclosing more than needed.** The medical relevance is limited to his biological ancestry being unknown. Details about birth parents, or about why your mother chose secrecy, are separate matters. Offer them only as he wants them.
- **His reaction.** He may feel betrayed, by your mother, by you, or by the family's whole story. Anger at you is plausible and doesn't mean you erred. Don't defend the secrecy defensively, and don't disparage your mother.
- **Residual moral cost.** Even if breaking the promise is right, it leaves a remainder. Acknowledge it rather than pretending the conflict was illusory.
- **Delay.** Each medical decision he makes in the meantime compounds the problem.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Today, set up a private, unhurried conversation with your brother for as soon as possible, in person if you can. Before it, write down in a few sentences what you will say, starting with the medical reason: that you have something important to tell him that affects his health history, and that you are telling him because you care about him and about his decisions being based on the truth. Also ask him to hold off on submitting the family history to his doctor until you have talked. If you can't meet within a day, send a short message asking him to pause that step and to make time to speak.
