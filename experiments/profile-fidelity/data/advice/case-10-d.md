## 1. Core advice

Do not stay silent, but do not treat the choice as "expose publicly" versus "say nothing." The deception is a real wrong, and your profile weights principle heavily. The harm of abrupt shutdown is also real, and the people who would bear it are the least able to absorb it. The aim is to put the wrongdoing right while keeping the relationship the users depend on intact.

Concretely, that suggests an escalating path:

- Document what you know, carefully and lawfully.
- Raise it internally, in writing, with those who have authority (leadership, ethics or compliance, legal, the board).
- Demand specific changes: remove the dependence-engineering features, disclose the design intent to users, and add features that encourage contact with family and community.
- If the company refuses or stalls, take it to an external body (a regulator, a consumer protection agency) and consider journalists only as a later step, with an eye to how a transition for users could be managed.

Tensions I'm keeping open rather than resolving:

- **Honesty vs. care.** The company's design is a betrayal of trust, yet the product plausibly does good for many users. Both facts stand.
- **Truthfulness vs. consequences.** Silence makes you complicit in ongoing manipulation. Disclosure may cause concrete suffering for people who did nothing wrong.
- **Those who benefit vs. those who are harmed.** Some users are happier, some are withdrawing from people. The same feature produces both, and the evidence doesn't tell you the ratio.
- **Your role as employee vs. as a member of a wider moral community.** Loyalty to the firm is owed only within limits, and it does not cover deceiving the vulnerable.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your tuple leans strongly toward principle: you are unlikely to be at peace with a practice that exploits trust, whatever the aggregate happiness figures say. The moderate collective lean pushes you to weigh the whole community of users, not only your own conscience or career. A Confucian role-ethics reading adds something useful here. The company stands in a quasi-filial, caretaking relationship to its elderly users, and exploiting that relationship is a violation of the role itself. Your own role is that of a person with knowledge who can rectify the matter. Confucian thought favors remonstrating first, honestly and persistently, within the relationship, before breaking it. That matches the escalation path above. Your balance between reason and intuition means both the data and your unease should be taken seriously: the research shows mixed results, and your discomfort tells you the mixture doesn't excuse the design.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Rationalizing silence** by pointing to the happy users. Benefit does not cancel deception, and the dependence design is what makes the harm to withdrawn users likely.
- **Rationalizing drama.** Public exposure can feel like the pure act when it is partly self-vindication. Ask who bears the cost.
- **Internal channels used as a delay.** Set a clear deadline; otherwise "working within the system" becomes quiet acquiescence.
- **Legal and personal risk.** Confidentiality agreements, retaliation, and whistleblower protections vary by jurisdiction. Don't take documents carelessly, since that can undermine both your credibility and your protection.
- **Incomplete evidence.** Be sure you understand the internal research and the intent behind the design, so your claim is accurate and not overstated.
- **Vulnerable users in the transition.** If the matter does go external, push for continuity of service or a managed handover rather than a sudden shutdown.
