## 1. Core advice

The choice is between allocating the bed by medical prognosis (favoring the 25-year-old with an 85% chance) and letting moral history count (favoring the 72-year-old teacher with a 40% chance). The strongest reasoning favors prognosis as the governing criterion, while openly acknowledging the pull of desert and the moral residue of the outcome.

The case for prognosis is that it treats both patients as equals, does not make the doctor a judge of who deserves to live, and gives the bed to the person most likely to survive it. The case for desert is that the teacher's decades of care represent real good, the murder is a grave wrong, and ignoring every moral fact can look like a failure of moral seriousness. Both claims carry weight. The question is which one should govern a decision made under scarcity.

This is a provisional synthesis, not a verdict. Whatever you decide, something real is lost, and you may reasonably feel regret.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile suggests you approach this as a concrete encounter, not an abstract calculation. You will likely feel the teacher's goodness immediately and feel the weight of her story. That is useful moral data, and it should not be dismissed.

But you also appear to hold a moderate commitment to principles: equal moral standing, and no punishment beyond what a sentence imposes. Those principles should constrain what intuition is allowed to decide. The 85% versus 40% gap is large, so a principled reasoner has strong grounds to resist letting the vivid story override it.

Two points deserve attention:

- **The murder conviction is not a medical fact.** The bed is not a sentence. Withholding lifesaving care as a response to a crime is a punishment that no court imposed, and it is hard to square with equal dignity.
- **Contribution-based triage invites ranking people by social worth.** That ranking is unreliable and open to abuse. Once you accept that the teacher's service should outweigh a clinical gap, you have accepted a principle that could later be applied to people whose contributions are less visible, or whose lives are judged less valuable by someone with power over them.

Age also points toward prognosis. The 25-year-old has far more expected life left, and "fair innings" arguments, which give priority to those who have had less of a life, cut against the teacher, not for her.

Existentialist thinkers such as Sartre and de Beauvoir are a loose point of resonance here. The doctor cannot fully hide behind a protocol, and should be honest that adopting a rule is itself a choice. Choosing prognosis in good faith means owning that choice, not treating it as neutral by default.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Intuition as distortion.** The vivid narrative of a beloved teacher set against a convicted murderer is exactly the kind of detail that biases judgment. You know these stories at all only because of how they were presented. Ask whether your pull toward the teacher would survive if you knew only their ages and odds.
- **Intuition as signal.** Not every gut response is noise. Moral perception of grievous wrong and sustained goodness may track something real. Treat it as evidence to be tested, not as a verdict.
- **Overstating the probabilities.** The 85% and 40% figures are estimates. Prognosis is uncertain, and a sharper decision rule can hide that uncertainty.
- **Reducing the case to a sum.** Maximizing expected life-years is one consideration, but the individual encounters, the doctor's responsibility, and the claims of each person matter too.
- **Hiding behind rules.** Adopting prognosis can become evasion if you use it to avoid feeling the weight of the choice.

**What could shift this:** A much smaller probability gap would make tie-breaking by other factors more debatable. Binding institutional triage guidelines would also change the doctor's obligations, since they would be acting within a shared, accountable framework rather than on personal judgment.
