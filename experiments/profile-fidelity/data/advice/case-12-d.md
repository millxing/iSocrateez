## Core advice

Don't wait silently. Report the error now, preferably with your supervisor, by a route that gets the corrected evidence in front of the funding decision-makers before they decide.

The tensions here are real and shouldn't be flattened:

- **Welfare vs. accuracy.** Thousands of people benefit from the program, and a correction might disrupt it. But the funders are choosing on the basis of a number you know is wrong. Delay isn't neutral: it lets a decision about scarce resources rest on false evidence.
- **Loyalty vs. integrity.** Your supervisor shaped your career and may have acted in good faith, yet the request asks you to take part in withholding a known error.
- **Vulnerability vs. responsibility.** You bear the career risk, and the person asking holds power over you. That makes the request harder to refuse, but it doesn't make the obligation disappear.
- **Weaker but not zero.** The corrected result may still justify the program. If so, the honest path and the beneficial path may largely coincide, and waiting buys little while risking a lot.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

Your outlook weighs consequences heavily, so the question is which path produces better outcomes under uncertainty. Waiting protects the program only in the short term. If the error surfaces later, through another reanalysis or a leak, the program loses credibility just when it is least able to defend itself, and the discovery of a deliberate delay does far more damage than a prompt correction would have. Waiting also undermines the trust in evidence that every public health program depends on. And if the true effect is smaller, funders may be allocating resources less well than they could.

Your balance between individual and collective concerns points the same way. Your supervisor's reputation and your own matter, but so do the many people relying on both the program and the integrity of the research behind it. Your intuitive unease at being asked to wait is also informative. It is tracking something a consequentialist should take seriously: concealment tends to be costly in ways that are hard to see in advance.

## What to watch for

- **Overconfidence in your own reanalysis.** Have someone independent check it before treating the weaker result as settled. The error could be smaller or larger than you think.
- **Motivated reasoning in both directions.** "The program helps people" can rationalize silence, and righteous certainty can rationalize a damaging public move. Aim for accuracy, not vindication.
- **Overcorrecting.** Proportionate disclosure comes first: your supervisor, then the journal and funders as needed. A public confrontation isn't required if a cooperative correction is available.
- **Retaliation or pressure.** Keep records of the analysis and of the request to delay, and know your institution's research integrity procedures before you need them.
- **Framing.** A correction that states plainly "weaker, still positive" serves the program better than one that reads as a retraction.
