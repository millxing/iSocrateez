## 1. Core advice

The case for waiting has real force. Thousands of people may depend on this program, funding decisions can be fragile, and your supervisor may be motivated by concern for them rather than self-protection. A consequence-focused view cannot wave that away.

But the same view, followed through to its long-run form, points the other way. The corrected effect is weaker, not zero, so the program may well survive an honest account. Letting funders decide on a figure you know is inflated creates a larger risk: if it later emerges that the error was known and withheld through the decision, the damage could fall on the program, your supervisor, you, and public trust in health research, probably more than a prompt correction would cause now. Delay also sets a precedent that inconvenient errors wait for convenient moments.

The most defensible course is to ensure the corrected result reaches decision-makers before the funding decision, ideally through your supervisor, and framed accurately: the corrected effect, and the value that remains. This is a judgment under uncertainty, not a proof. You cannot know how the funder will react to a smaller effect.

Consider the options in graded order:

1. **Talk with your supervisor again.** Bring the corrected analysis and a framing that emphasizes the surviving benefit. Propose that they notify the funder proactively, with the corrected numbers and a plain account of why the program remains worthwhile. This lets them keep control of the message.
2. **Seek guidance, confidentially if possible.** A research integrity officer, a department head, or a trusted senior mentor can tell you what your institution requires and what protections exist. Don't assume any particular guarantee.
3. **Document carefully.** Record the error, the corrected analysis, when you found it, and what was said to you.
4. **If your supervisor refuses and the decision is imminent,** weigh formal reporting despite the personal risk.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

You judge actions mainly by their effects on well-being, and you give weight to moral gut-sense alongside analysis. Both bear on this case, and they don't line up neatly.

On outcomes, a thorough accounting includes second-order effects: eroded trust, the chance of later exposure, the precedent for suppressing errors, and the cost of decisions made on bad evidence. Once these are counted, delay looks less like the welfare-protecting choice it first appears to be.

On intuition, two pulls are both real data: the sense that knowingly letting a decision rest on a false figure is wrong, and compassion for people who might lose services. Neither should be dismissed as noise. The graded path respects both by seeking a route where the beneficiaries' interests are argued honestly rather than protected through an overstated number.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Your own vulnerability.** Your supervisor has power over your career. Open defiance is risky, and nothing here requires heroic self-sacrifice. Reasonable people could weigh the compassion intuition, or the personal costs, differently than this advice does.
- **Assuming motives.** Don't assume your supervisor is acting in bad faith or that the funder will cut the program. Both are unknown.
- **Disclosure versus publication.** The key question is whether funders learn of the error before deciding, not only when a formal correction appears. A later publication may not satisfy that concern.
- **What would change the analysis:** the program clearly failing to justify itself at the corrected effect size; a funder process that allows a swift update after renewal; or good evidence that the error is too minor to bear on the decision.
- **Overconfidence in either direction.** Neither "delay is harmless" nor "delay is a betrayal" is established. Hold your conclusion with appropriate humility.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a one-page note, for your own records, with the original claim, the corrected result, how you found the error, and a short draft of how the corrected finding could be stated honestly, including the benefit that remains. Then request a private conversation with your supervisor, and bring the note. Propose proactive notice to the funder as a shared solution. If you are unsure how to proceed, contact your institution's research integrity office for a confidential consultation before that meeting.

As you decide, ask which option you could live with after the fact, whatever the outcome. Use that as a test alongside the reasoning, not as a substitute for it.
