## Core advice

Start by settling what you actually know. Did you see the friend obtain the answers, or did you infer it from something indirect? Could there be an innocent explanation, such as a shared study resource or a leaked answer key the friend didn't seek out? Your confidence should match your evidence before you act on it.

If the evidence is solid, the realistic comparison is not "harm to the friend versus no harm." Silence transfers a concrete loss to the honest student, who studied and may lose the scholarship. The question is which harms you're willing to be responsible for and how to minimize them.

A reasonable lean, conditional on the facts above: **give your friend a chance to self-report or withdraw from the scholarship, with a short, firm deadline.** If she doesn't act, report. This path gives the friend a chance to shape her own outcome, which often goes better for her than discovery by others, and it keeps your own conduct from becoming the thing that harms the honest student.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

You weigh outcomes for specific people, so the question is what each path would actually produce:

- **The friend:** Self-disclosure now is usually far less damaging than discovery later. Concealed cheating that surfaces after years of tuition support can mean expulsion, revoked funds, and a permanent record. Acting early may limit the damage.
- **The honest student:** Silence leaves them with a real loss, and their work counts as much as the friend's need.
- **You:** Staying silent makes you a participant in the outcome. Reporting without warning may cost the friendship and leave you feeling you skipped a chance to help. The confrontation path lets you do both: act on fairness and try to protect the friend.

This approach also treats the decision as an inquiry. You try an option, observe what happens, and revise. If the friend refuses, you learn something and act on it, rather than deciding once and never looking again.

The tension between compassion and fairness remains. The friend's poverty makes the harm sharper, but it doesn't change who earned the result. Both points stay true.

## What to watch for

- **Verification:** Acting on a misreading of evidence harms everyone. Check the facts before you speak.
- **The deadline may fail:** Your friend may refuse, stall, or ask you to stay quiet. Decide now what you'll do if that happens, so you don't face the same choice with less time and more pressure.
- **Responsibility is shared:** The friend's loss results partly from her own choice. That doesn't erase your sympathy, but it does mean you aren't responsible for creating her situation.
- **Friendship cost:** Whichever path you take, the friendship will likely change. Don't assume it must end, but don't pretend silence or reporting is free.
- **Looking for alternatives:** Honesty isn't abandonment. Check whether the financial aid office, emergency hardship funds, appeals processes, or other scholarships could cover her. Helping her find these routes may matter as much as the decision itself.
- **Self-check:** Ask what you could live with five years from now, and what you would advise a friend to do if she were in your position. If your answers conflict with your current lean, examine why.
