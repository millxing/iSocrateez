# Rationing water fairly in a drought

## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a question of fair terms for distributing harm, not a choice between "equal" and "unequal." A layered scheme with evidence checks and scheduled reviews is probably the strongest design.

- **Tier 1: a protected floor.** Guarantee drinking, sanitation, and medically necessary water for every household before anything else. Harm here is severe, can be irreversible, and falls on identifiable people. Verify medical needs through a simple clinician-backed process so the floor stays credible, and set a reasonable ceiling on what counts.
- **Tier 2: food and livelihood uses.** Household gardens that families rely on and the farm's operations belong here. Evaluate them by evidence, not by who argues most forcefully.
- **Tier 3: discretionary use.** Lawns, pools, car washing, and other non-essential use take the first and deepest cuts, including any such use by the farm's owners.

For the farm, treat "a sharp reduction will destroy the harvest" as a hypothesis, not a fact, and without presuming bad faith. Ask for documentation:
- How much water preserves what share of the crop?
- Is the loss gradual or a cliff?
- Can water go to the most efficient or highest-value portions (e.g., perennials or orchards that cannot be replanted, rather than annual rows)?
- Can irrigation shift to lower-loss methods or times?

Then offer a partial, staged allocation tied to that evidence, rather than either strict equality or a blank check.

Hold back a reserve, and make every allocation time-limited with explicit triggers (reservoir levels, rainfall) for tightening or loosening. With no reliable forecast, reversibility is worth a lot.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile weighs outcomes heavily and keeps attention on individuals instead of abstractions like "the town" or "the economy." That points toward asking what actually happens to a particular patient, a particular family living off a garden, and a particular worker whose wage supports a household. A flat equal limit is easy to trust, but it treats unlike needs alike. That's hard to defend when the downside for some people is medical harm.

Your moderate lean toward reasoned analysis also supports the verification steps: explicit criteria, documented claims, and revision as information arrives. Your individual focus cuts both ways here. Farm workers are individuals too, and their livelihoods depend on the farm, so protecting the farm is not simply protecting an institution. The evidence check is how you separate real, avoidable harm to those workers from an unverified forecast.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Capture and conflicts of interest.** Equal limits resist favoritism. Any differentiated scheme needs disclosed conflicts (including your own, if you have ties to the farm or to particular households), written criteria, and public reporting.
- **Cost-shifting.** Extra water to the farm means stricter limits for households who did not choose that risk. Weigh jobs saved against the added burden, especially for households near the floor.
- **Medical need as a blanket trump.** Without verification and limits, it can be gamed or can consume the supply. Minimizing it is the opposite error.
- **Cliff effects.** If the farm's loss is a cliff, a small allocation might save most of the harvest, or a partial one might save nothing. Find out before deciding.
- **Irreversibility on both sides.** A destroyed perennial crop and lost jobs may be unrecoverable, while household restrictions are usually survivable. Reasonable people weigh this differently.
- **Front-loading.** Generous early allocation risks empty reserves if the drought persists. Overcaution risks needless loss if rain comes.

**Who bears the greatest sacrifice?** Different principles give different answers, and I'd resist settling this too fast:

| Principle | Who sacrifices most |
|---|---|
| Capacity to absorb loss | High-volume households, discretionary users, the farm's owners |
| Protect basic needs first | Everyone's non-essential use, with the floor untouched |
| Shared proportional cuts | Everyone, farm included, by the same percentage of non-floor use |
| Conditional support for the farm | The farm gets water in exchange for obligations: wage guarantees for workers, transparency, deeper cuts to owners' discretionary use, community benefits such as food sharing |

These can be combined. A common blend is floor first, then proportional cuts to everything above it, with the farm's extra allocation conditioned on evidence and obligations.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a one-page request to the farm asking for its minimum viable water needs: volumes by crop and season, what share of the harvest is saved at 100%, 75%, and 50% of current use, which plantings are irreplaceable, and how many workers depend on each. At the same time, draft the Tier 1 floor and a simple medical-needs verification process.

Before sending, test your own view against three cases and note where your intuition resists:
1. **A dialysis patient** whose household would exceed the equal limit. Does the floor override equality fully?
2. **A family whose garden supplies much of their food.** Does it rank above or below the farm's claim, and why?
3. **A farm worker who loses the job if the harvest fails.** How much extra household restriction elsewhere would you accept to prevent this?

Your answers will show which harms you consider least acceptable, which is the real basis for deciding who should sacrifice most.
