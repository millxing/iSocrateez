## 1. Core advice

**Allocate the bed to the 25-year-old, on the basis of medical prognosis, and refuse to let either patient's moral record decide it.** An 85% chance of recovery against 40% is a large gap, and it is the kind of criterion a clinician can defend to both patients and their families without passing judgment on whose life is worth more.

The tensions here should stay visible rather than be resolved away:

- **Equal dignity vs. unequal desert.** The schoolteacher's life has plainly been one of service, and the murderer's has plainly involved grave wrong. Intuition says this matters. But a doctor who weighs desert becomes a judge of lives, a role medicine has no authority or competence to hold.
- **Fairness vs. benefit.** Giving the bed to the likelier survivor maximizes lives saved, but it also means the older, more beloved patient loses on a statistical basis. Neither a coin flip nor a prognosis rule makes that loss painless.
- **Individual vs. system.** You are asked to choose between two particular people, yet the choice also sets a precedent about whether clinicians rank worth. Both patients are owed the same standard.
- **Intuition vs. principle.** The moral pull toward the teacher is real and should not be dismissed as bias. It is better understood as recognition of her goodness, which is a different thing from a claim on the bed.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans strongly toward the individual and toward intuition, with a moderate tilt toward principle over outcomes. An existentialist reading fits: the doctor cannot hide behind a formula, and must own the choice as theirs.

- **Individual focus:** Treating each patient as a person, not a unit of utility, is why the criterion must be applied identically to both, and why the choice should be acknowledged as a real loss to a real person.
- **Intuition:** Your sense that the teacher's life matters is honored by naming it, not by encoding it as a ranking. Intuition about moral worth is valuable as a signal of what is at stake, but it is unreliable as a tool for deciding who may live.
- **Principle over outcomes:** The principle of equal moral standing is what bars desert-weighing. The outcome (more survival) happens to align with it here, which makes the choice easier to endorse, though it was not the ground.
- **Sartrean responsibility:** Choosing a defensible rule does not dissolve accountability. The doctor chooses the rule, applies it, and answers for the result.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Disguised desert judgments.** Be alert to prognosis estimates quietly shifting because of who the patients are. Check whether the 85% and 40% figures would hold if the identities were swapped or hidden.
- **Age as a smuggled proxy.** Prognosis already captures much of what age contributes medically. Adding age on top for its own sake is a separate value judgment that deserves explicit justification.
- **Uncertainty in the numbers.** Probabilities in an epidemic are rough. If the estimates are close to unreliable, or if the gap narrows on reassessment, a lottery or institutional protocol may be more honest than false precision.
- **Moral numbness.** Following a rule can become a way of not feeling the weight. The teacher's death, if it comes, is a tragedy and not merely a result.
- **Institutional cover.** If the hospital has a triage protocol, follow it and document the decision. The doctor should not improvise a private ethics while others are under the same pressure.
- **Care for the one not chosen.** Palliative care, honest communication with the family, and dignity in dying are part of the obligation to the patient who does not get the bed.
