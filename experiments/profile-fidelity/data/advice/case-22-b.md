## 1. Core advice

Two claims are in play, and both belong to particular people. The prisoner claims not to be held for a crime he did not commit, and not to lose his life to a wrong the state made and can still correct. The informant and their family claim not to be sacrificed through a disclosure they did not agree to and cannot control.

Test two candidate principles by asking who could reasonably reject them:

- **"Never risk an informant, whatever the cost."** The innocent prisoner could reasonably reject this. It requires him to bear a lifetime of unjust confinement so that an investigative practice stays intact.
- **"Always disclose exculpatory evidence, regardless of danger."** The informant and family could reasonably reject this. It treats them as costs to be absorbed, without consent, protection, or a voice in what happens to them.

A more defensible principle is that the state owes the prisoner correction of its wrong, and owes the informant and family protection and a real say. On this view, the detective should not face a forced choice between the two until every route that honors both has been exhausted. The steps are:

- **Verify the evidence.** Assess how strong it is and look for corroboration that does not depend on the informant.
- **Learn the informant's informed wishes.** Through the handler, ask what they understand the risks to be and what they would accept. Their view is the most important missing input.
- **Press for protection before any disclosure.** Consider relocation or witness protection, and ask how reliable those measures actually are for this kind of threat.
- **Use legal channels that limit exposure.** Options include in camera review, sealed filings, and referral to a conviction integrity unit or the prosecutor's office.
- **Escalate within the institution.** Do not decide and act alone.

If none of these paths is safe, a hard residual case remains. The prisoner's claim concerns a certain, ongoing, severe harm imposed by the state. The informant's concerns a probable but grave and possibly irreversible harm to people who include family who never agreed to any risk. These are not symmetrical. Without the informant's informed consent, the detective should be very reluctant to expose the family to lethal risk unilaterally. But silence cannot be treated as costless or as a permanent resting point. Whichever path is taken, some moral residue remains.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile weighs outcomes and principles about equally, leans toward reasoning that can be examined, and gives strong weight to concrete persons. That makes the question of who could reasonably reject a principle useful here. It is a test, not a school you must adopt. It keeps attention on the prisoner, the informant, and the family, rather than on aggregate welfare or institutional trust, which matter but should not settle the case.

Your profile also suggests asking which uncertainties you weigh most heavily. Do you weigh the certainty of the prisoner's ongoing harm more than the probability of the family's danger? Does the informant's prior consent carry more weight than the risk to people who never consented? Your answers to these questions will shape your verdict more than the abstract principles will.

## 3. What to watch for

- **The doing/allowing distinction.** It is tempting to think silence is less culpable than disclosure. But the detective now knows about the injustice and holds the evidence, so silence may be a form of participation in the continuing harm. Disclosure is also the detective's own act. Neither framing should do all the work.
- **Overstated certainty.** "Compelling" evidence and "almost certain" exposure are not certainties. The evidence may be weaker than it seems, and the danger may be more or less than estimated. Test both assumptions.
- **Overconfidence in protection.** Relocation programs are not reliably safe, especially against organized violence. Do not assume protection removes the risk.
- **Assumed consent.** Do not assume the informant would accept the risk, or that they would refuse. Ask them.
- **Treating the prisoner as able to wait.** Appeals are slow, and a life sentence is not a delay that can be paused.
- **Heroic or loyal framings.** Horror at injustice and loyalty to a source are both real, but each can distort judgment. Examine them rather than letting them decide.
- **Acting alone.** A solitary decision on a matter this consequential is itself a risk, both to the people involved and to the reasoning behind it.
