# Advice

## 1. Core advice

The decision turns on two questions that are easy to blur: *Can the library keep its independence while accepting money tied to a hidden content restriction?* and *Is refusal acceptable given the concrete harm to students?*

Here is the strongest case for each option:

- **Accept.** The students are identifiable: those without quiet homes, who lose a place to study for lack of funds this year. Five years of access is a substantial, tangible good, and the removed books are a bounded loss, since critical material may be available elsewhere.
- **Refuse.** A library's core function is to hold and make available a range of material, including material that criticizes powerful interests. A donor who can purchase removals, and who requires secrecy, converts a public institution into an instrument of a private party. The secrecy matters most: a condition that must be hidden is itself evidence that it could not survive public scrutiny. It also sets a precedent that invites the next donor.
- **Compromise.** This is justified only if it passes a real test: the library keeps and makes available books critical of the donor, and the public can know the terms of the arrangement. A compromise that preserves the appearance of independence while still delivering removals, even partial ones, is the same violation in softer language.

My assessment is that accepting the secret condition as offered is very hard to justify. Five years of funding cannot cleanly offset the loss of the institution's central function, and the secrecy removes the public's ability to judge the trade. But refusal carries real, foreseeable costs, so it is only defensible if paired with active effort to protect the students. Refusal should therefore be the starting position, not the end of the analysis: counter-offer on transparent terms, and treat a rejected counter-offer as the point where refusal becomes the answer.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile favors principles over outcomes, the collective over the individual, and reasoned judgment over intuition. That suggests you will want a rule-anchored account of institutional duty, and this analysis offers one: the library has a duty to preserve access, but a more basic duty not to become an instrument of a private party's interests. The first duty is real, but it cannot be discharged by violating the second, because the second is what makes the library's service worth having.

Your collective orientation also cuts the other way, and it should. The students are part of the collective, not an abstraction outside it. Their loss is a public harm, and the library should not treat refusal as costless. A distinctly Stoic point is useful here: the library controls its own conduct, meaning what it removes, what it discloses, and what it seeks, but not whether other funding materializes. That clarifies the choice without settling it: the library can be answerable for acting with integrity and for the effort it makes to mitigate harm, though not for guaranteeing the outcome.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Compromise language that launders the removals.** Phrases like "relocating," "archiving," or "reviewing for balance" can deliver the same result. Ask directly: would a patron still be able to find and borrow books critical of the donor's industry?
- **Secrecy treated as a detail.** A private arrangement may be fine for some donor relationships, but here it conceals the very thing that threatens public trust. Any acceptable deal needs disclosure of its terms.
- **Assuming the students' need can be met elsewhere.** Check this rather than presume it: other rooms, school facilities, partner organizations, or extended hours at other branches may or may not be viable.
- **Treating the offer as neutral.** The restriction is a substantive condition, not a gift without strings.
- **Overstating either side.** The library has legitimate budget constraints, and refusal does harm specific people who had no say in the trade-off. Neither fact should be minimized.
- **Precedent.** Consider what the library would say to the next donor with a similar condition, and whether it could defend that answer publicly.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Draft a short written counter-offer to the donor: the library will gratefully accept the funding for the study room with no conditions on its collection, and will publicly acknowledge the gift on transparent terms. Before sending it, brief the library director or board chair, since the decision is institutional, not yours alone. In parallel, list five to ten alternative sources to contact this week, such as local government, community foundations, school districts, university partners, and other donors, and identify one interim measure, such as reduced hours or a partner space, that would keep the room's most-used hours open if the counter-offer is rejected.
