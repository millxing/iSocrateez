## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a question about your team's working system first, and about two individuals second. Before choosing among the three options, sequence them into a proportionate path:

1. **Clarify expectations for every role.** Define what each person is expected to deliver, by when, and how handoffs work. Much apparent slacking comes from ambiguous ownership, and clear expectations make any later conversation fairer.
2. **Examine evidence you already have.** Look at project trackers, review queues, commit or document histories, and deliverable timelines. These show contribution more directly than activity data, and they impose no new surveillance.
3. **Speak privately with the two people, without accusation.** Describe specific observations (missed handoffs, thin deliverables), ask how they see their workload, and leave room for explanations you can't see, such as health, caregiving, unclear assignments, or being under-resourced.
4. **Give the overburdened colleagues relief now.** Redistribute tasks, adjust deadlines, and acknowledge their extra load explicitly. Delay isn't neutral for them, and this step doesn't depend on settling who is at fault.

Hold monitoring in reserve. If you later consider it, make it narrow, transparent, and role-specific, and announce it openly rather than deploying it across the whole team.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile weighs consequences heavily and gives real weight to the collective. That points to the fairness the team actually experiences, and to the colleagues absorbing the slack. It also points to long-term team functioning, where blanket monitoring carries a serious cost: it can teach people they are watched, reduce candor, and drive out good thinkers whose work looks idle on a screen.

Your more moderate lean toward reason over intuition suggests taking the team's felt sense of unfairness seriously as evidence, while testing it systematically rather than acting on it directly. The pragmatist spirit here is inquiry: try the least invasive response that could resolve the problem, observe what happens, and revise. This also respects the two individuals' dignity, since the collective concern doesn't license treating suspicion as proof.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Activity data mistaken for contribution.** Keystrokes and active-window time reward visible busyness and misread reading, design, and deep thinking.
- **Structural causes.** If the imbalance traces to unclear roles or poor handoffs, individual discipline would fix the wrong thing.
- **Leaking identities.** Tell the team you've heard the workload concern and are acting on it, without hints about who or what evidence is under review.
- **Weak evidence driving formal action.** If conversations and artifacts leave the picture unclear, be cautious about formal steps. Uncertainty is a reason for further inquiry, not for escalation.
- **Monitoring's side effects.** It changes behavior and signals distrust, so even narrow versions carry costs that should be weighed against what they'd actually reveal.
- **Your own uncertainty.** You may be wrong about one or both people, or about how large the imbalance is.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Spend an hour listing, for each role, the three or four deliverables you expect and where evidence of them already lives (tracker, repo, review queue, shared docs). Then pull the last six to eight weeks of that data for the two people and for a couple of comparable colleagues. Note gaps and ambiguities in what was assigned, not conclusions about effort. Use that to draft specific, neutral questions for your private conversations, and send a brief message to the team acknowledging the workload concern and saying you'll follow up within a set period (say, two weeks) with changes. Put a review date on your calendar to check whether the imbalance has eased and adjust.
