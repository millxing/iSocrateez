# Advice

## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a cluster of distinct features whose weight shifts with context, not as a contest between "ownership" and "public good." Separate them first:

- **The community's claim:** the object was taken without consent, and descendants want it for living religious practice. This is a claim about a wrong that remains unremedied and about present self-determination, which gives it unusual force.
- **The museum's stewardship record:** real care over generations, but custody that began in a wrongful taking. That history limits how much entitlement the care can create.
- **Public and economic benefit:** jobs and free school visits are real harms if lost, borne by identifiable people who did not commit the original wrong.
- **Preservation risk:** a genuine empirical question, not a rhetorical one.

On the evidence in this case, the strongest reading is that **the museum should move toward return, with the pace and terms set through a process the community co-owns**, rather than retaining the object for public access. Retention asks descendants to keep bearing the cost of a wrong so that others can keep a benefit. The harms from return are real but can largely be mitigated. The harm of continued retention cannot be mitigated while the object stays.

Between the other two options, I'd treat "unconditional return" and "shared custody" as points on a spectrum. What matters is who holds the decisive authority. Shared custody is a genuine middle path only if title and final say over ceremonial use rest with the community. If the museum keeps title and the community gets "access," it is retention under a cooperative label. A defensible version looks like: transfer of ownership, followed by voluntary agreements the community chooses to enter (loans for exhibition, replicas, documentation, conservation support).

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your outcome-focused lean makes the concrete consequences of each option the thing to examine:

| Option | Likely effects |
|---|---|
| Retention | Jobs and school visits preserved; ongoing denial of ceremonial use; likely continued conflict and reputational damage |
| Unconditional return | Redress achieved; museum loses its main draw; possible physical change to the object through ritual use; economic strain on staff and local programs |
| Return with voluntary partnership | Redress achieved; some revenue and educational value may be retained through replicas, loans, or co-curated programming; depends on trust and good faith |

Your intuitive lean and the particularist lens (as in Dancy) fit well here because the same feature can point opposite ways. Preservation concern counts *for* caution when it reflects specific, assessable physical risk and the community is party to evaluating it. It counts *against* the museum's position when it functions as an outside veto over how sacred things are handled, or when ritual wear is itself part of the object's intended life. Likewise, the museum's long care counts for something when the community values it, and for little when it is offered as a reason to keep what was taken. No rule fixes these weights ahead of time. Your sense of how the particulars sit is legitimate evidence here, though it's worth testing against the facts below.

Your moderate collective lean raises the institutional question: what do a museum and a community owe each other after a historical taking? Plausibly, the museum owes repair and the community owes nothing in return, though a continuing relationship may be good for both.

## 3. What to watch for

- **"The community" is not one voice.** Descendants may disagree about ceremonial use, custody, or conditions. Confirm who holds recognized authority through the community's own processes, rather than choosing an interlocutor.
- **Paternalism disguised as care.** Ask who evaluates preservation risk. If only museum conservators do, the concern is not neutral. A joint assessment, with community-appointed conservators or ritual specialists, is fairer.
- **Conditions that become vetoes.** Terms attached to return can recreate the original asymmetry. Distinguish conditions the community proposes from those the museum imposes.
- **Treating economic loss as trivial or as decisive.** It is neither. It justifies a transition plan and a search for alternative funding, not retention.
- **Letting present innocence dilute the obligation.** The current staff did not commit the taking, but the institution inherited both the object and the responsibility. Cost-bearing is appropriate even though it is unfair to individuals, and it can be cushioned through grants, repatriation funds, or phased timelines.
- **Where genuine disagreement remains:** how much present cost an institution should absorb for a historical wrong is contestable, and reasonable people weigh it differently. The case for return is strong, not unanimous.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a one-page list of **open factual questions** and send a request to the community's recognized representatives, offering to meet on their terms. The list should ask, not assert:

1. Who does the community want to speak for it in this process?
2. What does ceremonial use actually involve, and what do they want the museum to know about it?
3. What would the community consider a respectful process, including timing and the role of any outside conservators?

In parallel, ask your own staff for two numbers: what share of revenue depends on this single object, and what a realistic replica or alternative exhibition would cost. That gives you the real consequences to weigh, which is where your outcome-oriented judgment will have the most to work with.
