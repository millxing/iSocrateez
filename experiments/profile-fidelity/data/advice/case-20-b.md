## Core advice

This is a real conflict between goods, not a puzzle with a hidden answer. Strict adherence protects fairness and consistency. Discretion honors the particular lives at stake. Whichever she chooses, she will carry a cost, and she should accept that openly.

**Strict policy** protects consistency, legal safety, and the trust of the six remaining team members, who will learn what the organization values. It also spares her from partiality. Its risk is that it can become moral abdication. If she treats the rule as absolving her of responsibility for two people's serious harm, she has chosen without owning the choice.

**Discretionary weighing** honors the specific stakes of need and long service. Its risks are bias, inconsistency, unequal treatment of the newer employee, legal exposure, and rationalization that looks like compassion.

Her best course is probably neither pure option. She should first try to widen the options: ask whether the performance data is complete and fairly measured, and whether the policy allows any room for voluntary exits, severance enhancements, pension bridging, or redeployment. If the policy truly leaves no room, she should raise the tension with HR or leadership rather than resolve it alone or quietly work around the rule.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

You likely weigh consequences for actual people and trust what your intuitions register. That is a strength here, because a rule that ignores the single father's dependents or the colleague's pension cliff is incomplete. But intuition also tracks what is vivid, familiar, or sympathetic, not only what is most vulnerable. The question is whether your pull toward one person reflects their situation or your closeness to them.

Existentialist thinkers like Sartre and de Beauvoir would press a simpler point: she cannot hide behind a role. Following the policy is a choice she answers for, and so is departing from it. Neither is automatically cold or warm.

## What to watch for

- **The three factors carry different weight.** *Loyalty* and *need* are the substance of the moral question. *Likelihood of staying* is instrumental and speculative. The two leavers may change their minds, or others may leave. Retention matters to the organization's survival and so to the remaining team's welfare, but it is a weak basis for deciding whom to lay off.
- **Loyalty is real but not decisive.** Twenty years creates a claim of reciprocity. Yet performance rules exist partly to protect everyone, including newer staff who have earned their place on the same terms.
- **Need is not easily ranked.** Both candidates face severe but different harms. Avoid implying that one suffering is measurably greater than the other.
- **Protected characteristics are not criteria.** Age, family status, and tenure-linked factors may be legally sensitive in how they are used. She should not let them shape the decision directly, and should check with HR or legal counsel before relying on any reasoning that touches them.
- **Test the metrics.** "By the numbers" may hide context: whether the newer hire is still ramping up, whether the colleague's recent performance reflects circumstances, or whether the role itself is harder to measure.
- **Avoid secret workarounds.** Quietly bending the process, or deceiving the company, would make her discretion unaccountable.
