## 1. Core advice

The stakes are large on both sides. Cheap clean energy could reduce climate harm, energy poverty, and the deaths linked to fossil fuel pollution. A technology that can be adapted into a destructive weapon could cause harm that is catastrophic and irreversible. Neither concern is a rounding error.

The choice is also not limited to publish or suppress. The realistic options include:

- full publication
- permanent suppression
- staged or delayed release
- confidential disclosure to a small group of independent experts or oversight bodies
- publishing the energy-relevant principles while withholding the details that matter for weaponization
- building verification or defensive measures before any release

Each option has real costs. Leaks can happen, powerful actors can capture the technology, delay has human costs, and beneficial and dangerous knowledge may not separate cleanly. A middle path is not a way out of the dilemma.

Under a consequence-focused, individually responsible, intuition-led profile, a staged and controlled approach is probably best supported. It keeps options open, avoids irreversible steps, and sets a defined point for reassessment as facts become clearer. Even so, this is a provisional judgment, not a certainty.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your emphasis on outcomes means the key questions are empirical. How likely is misuse, and how severe would it be? How hard would weaponization be in practice? How likely is independent discovery, and would others do it under worse safeguards? Are defenses feasible, and how many lives would a delay cost? Irreversible tail risks can reasonably weigh more than a simple average of expected outcomes. But suppression is not a neutral default. It has its own victims, so it needs the same scrutiny as publication.

Your reliance on intuition is useful, but it is evidence to examine, not a verdict. Notice what the gut says, then ask what it might be tracking: fear of catastrophe, excitement about the discovery, grief over climate harm, or the appeal of being the one who decides. Ask what evidence would change it. If nothing could, that is worth knowing.

Your focus on individual responsibility points in two directions. She cannot hand the decision to someone else. But seeking independent, informed counsel is part of exercising responsibility, not abdicating it. Her unilateral power over a choice affecting everyone is also morally uncomfortable, and acknowledging that discomfort is part of acting well.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Overconfidence:** assuming that because she understands the science, she also understands the social, political, and security consequences.
- **Vivid pictures distorting judgment:** either the image of catastrophe or the image of utopia can crowd out a clear assessment of probabilities.
- **Slogans as cover:** "science must be open" or "I'm only preventing harm" can both be ways of avoiding ownership of a choice that no rule settles.
- **Assuming delay is free or staging is safe:** both carry risks that need to be named and weighed.
- **Indefinite secrecy:** a temporary hold can quietly become permanent without any deliberate decision.
- **Personal motives:** ambition, recognition, or fear of losing control over her work can shape the judgment without being acknowledged.

No option here is clean. She may be wrong. Waiting has victims, and so does releasing too early. The decision remains hers to own.
