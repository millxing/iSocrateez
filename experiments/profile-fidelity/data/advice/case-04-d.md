## Core advice

Take the migrants on board. Notify the coast guard and the company immediately, and document everything as you go. A boat that is damaged, in rough seas, with sixty people aboard cannot safely wait six hours for help that is not yet at hand. The duty to render assistance is among the oldest and least contested norms of the sea, and here it falls on the one person who is actually able to act.

The tensions deserve to be named rather than resolved by fiat:

- **Legal duty vs. institutional instruction.** Maritime law (SOLAS, UNCLOS) binds the master personally. The company's instruction is a policy, not a legitimate override. But the company's fear is not baseless: the captain's choice carries real costs for the crew.
- **Duty to the stranger vs. duty to the crew.** Three weeks in port and a smuggling investigation fall on people who did not choose this risk. The captain is accountable for them too.
- **Certain harm vs. probable harm.** Sixty lives at imminent risk are weighed against costs that are serious but survivable and largely procedural.
- **Rule-following vs. practical judgment.** Rescue is required by law, yet the law's application (where to land people, who investigates) is shaped by institutions that may punish compliance. Good character must work inside that gap.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

You lean moderately toward principles and toward the collective, with reason and intuition in balance. The principle here is clear and well established, and the collective at stake includes the people in the water as much as the crew and company. Your intuition, the immediate recognition that you do not sail past a sinking boat, and your reasoning, that the legal and moral weight both point the same way, converge.

In Aristotelian terms, the question is what a person of practical wisdom would do. Such a person would not be reckless, nor would they hide behind the company memo. Courage here is not heroism for its own sake but the mean between abandoning people out of fear and acting without regard for the crew's safety and the ship's capacity. Practical wisdom shows in how the rescue is carried out: transparently, within the ship's capability, and with the crew informed.

## What to watch for

- **Capacity and safety.** Sixty people may exceed what the ship can safely carry or manage in rough seas. Assess the recovery method, the risk to your crew, and the stability of the vessel. If a safe on-board rescue is truly impossible, the duty shifts toward standing by, providing what aid you can, and keeping the coast guard engaged until help arrives. This should be a genuine assessment, not a convenient exit.
- **Rationalizing the memo.** "Report and continue" is tempting because it feels procedural. Notice whether reporting is serving the people in distress or primarily serving the company's risk profile.
- **Burden on the crew.** Tell them what is happening and why. They share the consequences, so they deserve candor and, where possible, a voice in how it is carried out.
- **Smuggling exposure.** Contemporaneous records are your best protection: positions, times, communications with the coast guard (MRCC), photographs, and logs showing a distress response and not facilitation.
- **Disembarkation.** Rescue does not end at the pickup. Coordinate with the coast guard on a place of safety rather than improvising a destination.
- **Moral residue.** Even the right choice leaves costs, including to the crew and your career. Acknowledging that honestly is part of acting well, not evidence you chose wrongly.
