## 1. Core advice

This is a real conflict of goods, and each path carries a cost you will have to own. Neither strict policy adherence nor discretionary weighing is clean, and neither is automatically the principled or the evasive option.

**Strict adherence** protects fairness, consistency, legal safety, and the trust of the six who remain. It spares you from partiality. The price is potentially severe harm to two people, and a risk of moral abdication if you treat the rule as absolving you of the choice. Applying a policy is still something you do and answer for.

**Discretion** honors the particularity of these lives and the real weight of need and long service. It risks bias, inconsistency, unfair treatment of the newer employee, legal exposure, and rationalization dressed as compassion.

Your three considerations carry different weight:

- **Likelihood of staying** is the weakest ground. It is speculative, and it treats people as instruments of team continuity. It matters to the organization's health and so to the remaining team, but it should not decide who loses a job.
- **Loyalty** is a real moral consideration rooted in reciprocity. Set against it is the fact that performance rules exist to protect everyone, including newer staff, who deserve the same fairness.
- **Need** is weighty, but both people have serious need of different kinds: dependents on one side, a pension cliff on the other. Resist ranking their suffering as though it were measurable.

Before deciding, ask whether the question is really binary. You can follow the performance rule while widening the options: voluntary departures, early-retirement or pension bridging, severance enhancements, internal transfers, reduced hours across the team. Raise the case with HR or leadership rather than deciding alone or quietly working around the policy. These don't dissolve the tension, but they may shrink the harm.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

You lean toward consequences, individual persons, and intuition, so you will likely care most about what actually happens to these two people and distrust a rule that ignores their lives. That orientation is a strength here. Taking the pension and the child seriously is not sentimentality.

It also calls for testing your intuitions. Which person makes you flinch more, and why? Does that track their vulnerability, or your closeness to a twenty-year colleague, or the vividness of a child? Sympathy can follow familiarity rather than need.

An existentialist lens helps lightly: hiding behind the rule and bending the rule are both choices you answer for. What matters is that you can say plainly why you chose as you did, to both people and to yourself.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Protected characteristics.** Age, family status, and proximity to pension can be legally protected or fraught as decision criteria. Using them as straightforward factors creates fairness and legal risk. Consult HR or legal counsel before letting them shape the decision.
- **Retention as rationalization.** The two strong performers' plans are a prediction, not a fact. Notice if it becomes a convenient justification for a preference you already hold.
- **Metrics that mislead.** Check whether the performance data is fair and complete. A one-year employee may reflect onboarding effects, role difficulty, or circumstances that distort the numbers.
- **Quiet circumvention.** Any departure from policy should be transparent and sanctioned, not hidden. Discretion that can't be explained openly is probably bias.
- **The signal to the six.** The remaining team will read this decision as a statement of what the organization values.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Request a conversation with HR or your own manager. Before it, write a short note covering three things: what the performance data shows and any context that might change its reading; whether the policy leaves any legitimate room for judgment; and which harm-reduction options (severance, pension accommodation, transfers, voluntary exits) you want to ask about. Then ask directly whether any are available. Whatever you ultimately decide, this makes your discretion accountable and widens the choices before you.
