# Advice on the hardware store

## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a choice among several options, not a binary of "sell" or "keep going." Both your promise to your father and your obligations to your children carry real moral weight. Feeling pulled in two directions is an accurate reading of the situation, not confusion.

Three steps, in order:

1. **Clarify the promise.** Ask what your father actually asked for. Was it "never sell," or "don't let the store die, and look after the people who depend on it"? Recall his exact words and the circumstances in which he said them. If he spoke before the losses became structural, his purpose may be better served by a carefully conducted sale than by a slow collapse that drains the family and ends in closure anyway.
2. **Get the facts that decide this.** You haven't shared the size of your debts, the annual loss, your runway, or the developer's terms. Whether the store can be made viable, even in a smaller form, is a factual question, separate from how much the legacy matters to you.
3. **Test intermediate paths against the promise's purpose.** These include:
   - selling with negotiated terms (employee retention periods, severance, transition support, a lease-back or continued retail use of the building);
   - an employee buyout or cooperative model;
   - community investment;
   - restructuring toward a smaller or different business (services, contractor accounts, specialty lines);
   - continuing with a defined review date and a hard financial limit.

"As long as I can" is only a principled stance if it has a boundary. Without one, it can postpone the decision while savings and goodwill drain, which would harm your employees and children as well.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

You lean toward principles over pure outcomes and toward reasoning over intuition, so the decision should rest on duties you can articulate and criteria you can state in advance, not on guilt or projected totals alone. Here, that means identifying which duties are in play (the promise, parental responsibility, fair treatment of employees) and asking how each is best discharged, not which one wins outright.

Your balance between individual and collective concerns means neither your family's security nor the town's fabric should be dismissed quickly. Your children's education is a real claim, not selfishness. The employees are stakeholders with their own interests, not a cost line.

The Stoic frame also helps. You control your judgment about the promise, your honesty with employees, and how you conduct a sale or wind-down. You don't control the town's future, the developer's intentions, or the retail market. Putting your effort into the first group is both more effective and more defensible.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Literalism versus purpose.** Honoring the words while defeating their point (the store dies anyway, and the family is poorer) is a hollow fulfillment. Equally, don't use "purpose" to rationalize what is merely convenient. Test your reading by asking whether your father, seeing the numbers, would recognize it as faithful.
- **Drift.** Open-ended continuation tends to erode the resources your children and your own future depend on. Set the limit while your judgment is clear, not when you're out of cash.
- **Optimism and pessimism bias.** Projections about the store's turnaround and about the sale's consequences are both uncertain. Be skeptical of confident stories in either direction.
- **The developer's terms.** Don't assume the sale is purely harmful or purely beneficial. Terms on employees and on the building's use could change the moral picture considerably.
- **Employee circumstances.** Some staff may be near retirement, others more mobile. Early, honest disclosure matters more than a polished plan, and concealment will cost trust either way.
- **Your own strain.** Your financial and psychological pressure is a legitimate factor, not a weakness to override.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Spend an hour writing two things on paper:

1. **The promise, verbatim.** Your father's words as best you remember them, when he said them, what he knew about the finances then, and what you think he was most afraid of losing.
2. **The numbers you don't yet have.** Total debt, trailing three-year losses, months of runway, and what you'd need to know from the developer (employee provisions, timeline, building use, room to negotiate).

Then identify one person to call in the next few days, such as an accountant or a small-business advisor, to help you assess viability and the intermediate options. That turns an open-ended dilemma into a decision with defined criteria.
