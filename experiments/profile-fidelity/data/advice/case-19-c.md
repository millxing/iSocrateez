# Core advice

Treat this as three separate questions: what your parent wanted, what each sibling is owed, and what you, as the person entrusted with the task, should do. They blur easily, and the note tempts you to answer all three at once.

The note is real evidence, but weak evidence. It has no amount, it contradicts what your parent told you directly, and you don't know when it was written or in what frame of mind. It could be a settled intention, a passing pang of gratitude or guilt, or a thought your parent chose not to act on. Treat it as a reason to take the question seriously, not as the parent's true will.

Of the main paths, I'd lean toward one that is defensible to all three siblings, because how you decide may matter as much as the split itself:

1. **Follow the spoken instruction and set the note aside.** This honors your direct commission and avoids acting on a guess. It costs you the possibility that you are knowingly ignoring your parent's deeper intent, and it leaves a secret you alone hold.
2. **Adjust toward the caregiver on your own.** This honors the note and the claim of desert. Any amount you choose would be arbitrary, and you'd be overriding the instruction you were given on your own interpretation.
3. **Disclose the note and deliberate together.** This honors honesty and the siblings' shared standing as your parent's children. It risks conflict and could pressure the caregiver into the awkward position of advocating for themselves.
4. **Divide equally, paired with a voluntary, transparent gesture of recognition toward the caregiver.** This keeps fidelity to the spoken wish while answering the note's spirit. It depends on goodwill you can't compel, and "voluntary" can feel hollow if the pressure is obvious.

Paths 3 and 4 can be combined: share the note, keep the equal split as the default, and invite the siblings to decide together whether and how to recognize the caregiving. I'd offer this as a live option, not the answer. A reasonable person could land on 1 or 2 depending on how they rank fidelity to words, fidelity to intent, and fairness.

On desert versus need: the caregiver's years of unpaid labor ground a real claim, and the sibling who paid for professional help also made a real sacrifice. The ill sibling's limited contribution is not a failure of contribution, and their need is acute and not their fault. None of these claims cleanly outranks the others, and care labor and money are hard to compare. Beware any formula or percentage here, since without more context it would only look precise.

# Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans toward principled reasoning, the wellbeing of the family as a group, and deliberate analysis over gut reaction. Together these suggest you'll want a rationale you can defend consistently, not a sympathetic improvisation.

- **Principle:** Your duty of fidelity is real, but the principles in play (obey the parent, reward care, protect the vulnerable) conflict, so no single one settles the matter. A transparent process is a principled way to proceed when the principles themselves collide.
- **Collective orientation:** The sibling bond is a value in itself, not just a means. This favors paths that keep all three able to look at one another afterward.
- **Reason over sympathy:** Examining the note's date, context, and ambiguity respects your analytic lean better than simply siding with the caregiver.

The open tension is that "fidelity to the parent" can mean the literal instruction or the deeper intent, and your profile alone doesn't say which ranks higher. That judgment is yours.

# What to watch for

- **Overreading the note.** Resist reading it as certain proof of what your parent "really" wanted. Look for any date, surrounding papers, or context.
- **Secrecy costs.** Concealing the note makes you the sole arbiter of a secret. If it surfaces later, the damage to trust may exceed the damage of disclosure now.
- **Framing the caregiver.** Avoid casting them as either simply entitled or as grasping. The same goes for the other two siblings.
- **Role drift.** Acting mechanically can abdicate responsibility, while acting unilaterally on interpretation can overstep. Know which risk you are more prone to.
- **Legal variation.** A private note may carry no legal force, and rules differ by jurisdiction. If there is a will or formal estate, check it and consult an appropriate professional before distributing anything.
- **Process pressure.** If you disclose, how and when you do it matters. Avoid an ambush, and avoid framing that makes anyone defend their worth.
