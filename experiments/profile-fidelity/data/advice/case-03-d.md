## Core advice

Don't treat this as "sell" versus "keep." Treat it as a question about which of your obligations can actually be met, and at what cost. Several real duties conflict here, and none of them cancels the others:

- **The promise to your father** is a genuine obligation. It is also ambiguous. Did you promise to keep the store open *whatever happens*, or to not abandon what your family built *if it can be sustained*? Reason can examine that distinction. Guilt can't.
- **Your duty to your children** is not selfish. Debt relief and their education are obligations you hold as a parent, and a store that drains the family indefinitely may fail them.
- **Your obligations to eleven employees and the town** are real, but they are not owed in the form of "never close." They are owed as fairness, notice, and good-faith effort. How a sale or closure happens matters as much as whether it happens.
- **Loyalty to a legacy** pulls against **honesty about the numbers.** A store that loses money every year may close anyway, and a forced, late closure usually harms employees more than a planned transition.

My advice is to test the promise against reality before breaking or honoring it blindly:

1. Give the store one honest, time-bound viability test (6-12 months) with a defined threshold, such as a credible path to break-even, a lower-cost model, or a buyer who would keep it running.
2. If no such path exists, selling is defensible. Make it a *principled* sale: negotiate employee retention or severance, longer notice, and possibly a local-use or employee-priority clause.
3. Treat the broken or reinterpreted promise as a moral cost you acknowledge, not one you explain away.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

You lean strongly toward principle and reason, so the promise should weigh heavily in your thinking. A Stoic reading, in the spirit of Epictetus, asks you to act well within your *roles*: son, parent, employer, citizen. When roles conflict, you rank them by reason rather than by the loudest emotion. It also asks you to separate what is in your control (the quality of your decision, the terms you negotiate, how you treat people) from what is not (whether the town's retail landscape survives, whether the store is profitable in a changing market). Your balance between individual and collective concerns fits here: neither your family's security nor the town's interest should automatically win.

## What to watch for

- **Rationalizing in either direction.** Guilt can make you cling to the store past the point of sense. Relief at an exit can make you read the promise too loosely.
- **Treating the promise as all-or-nothing.** If your father meant "don't give up easily," a serious, honest effort may honor it. If he meant "never sell," you'll have to decide whether that promise can bind you against your children's welfare.
- **Underweighting the employees' position.** Keeping the store open at a loss may only postpone their job loss. Delay without a real plan can leave them with less notice and fewer options.
- **Taking the developer's offer as the only one.** A single bid sets the terms. Other buyers, or one who keeps the store running, might ease the tension.
- **Moral residue.** Even a justified sale may leave grief or a sense of having broken something. That doesn't mean you chose wrongly.
