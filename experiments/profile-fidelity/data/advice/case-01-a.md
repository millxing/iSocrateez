# Core advice

Treat the vote as the last step of a decision, not the whole of it. Before casting it, push on the binary. The strongest position available to you is probably one that tests whether the project's benefits can be kept without overriding a community that has said no to everything offered.

Concretely:

- **Settle the empirical questions first.** How reliable are the power and flood-protection claims? Could a smaller or redesigned project, or a different site, deliver most of the benefit? What is the actual risk to downstream populations without the dam? Your pivotal vote raises your duty to scrutinize these claims rather than inherit them.
- **Ask what the refusal means.** Is it a bargaining position, or a claim that no compensation can answer, given the graves and nine generations of continuity? These call for different responses. If it is the latter, treat it as a rights-based objection with real weight, not as obstruction, though not automatically a veto.
- **Prefer conditional or delayed action over a clean yes or no.** Options include a delay for independent review, support conditioned on binding protections (verified burial-site assessment, reburial and access rights, materially better terms), or opposition paired with a public statement of the conditions under which you would support a revised project.
- **If you must vote yes or no,** name the principle you are applying and why it takes precedence here. On the present facts, an unresolved refusal by a community facing irreversible loss, combined with unexamined alternatives, is a serious reason to vote against *this* proposal or this process. That is a judgment about the proposal as it stands, not necessarily about whether a dam should ever be built. A reasoned yes is also defensible if the benefits are verified, alternatives are exhausted, and the people harmed are treated as owed more than compensation.

# Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans strongly toward principles over outcomes, moderately toward collective concerns, and moderately toward reason over intuition. Those pull in a coherent direction here.

- **Principles first:** Consent, the integrity of a community's ties to its land, and respect for burial grounds are rule-like claims that the 200,000-to-400 arithmetic cannot simply cancel. Your profile asks you to give them substantial weight.
- **Collective concern:** The same profile keeps the 200,000 in view. Flood protection and reliable power are also matters of safety and basic welfare, so rights-based reasoning does not license ignoring them. This is why a redesign or conditional path often serves you better than a flat refusal.
- **Reason over intuition:** Your revulsion at flooding a burial ground is data, not a verdict. Test it: does it track something you can state as a principle (irreversibility, dignity, the weight of a community's refusal), or is it mainly the vividness of the identifiable few? Do the same to the benefits side, where concrete numbers can feel weightier than they are.
- **The Stoic lens:** Epictetus directs you to what is yours to govern: the quality of your judgment, the integrity of your process, and the honesty of your reasons. That does not mean accepting any outcome calmly, and it does not make your reaction to the valley irrelevant. Let the reaction inform the inquiry, then act on reasons you can defend.

# What to watch for

- **Arithmetic shortcuts.** Neither "200,000 versus 400" nor "graves trump everything" settles the matter. Keep each stake explicit (the region's welfare, the residents' homes and rights, the graves and cultural continuity, downstream risk) so that none silently absorbs the others.
- **Treating refusal as automatically decisive, or as mere obstruction.** Its weight depends on what it expresses and on what was actually offered.
- **Procedural illegitimacy.** Even a correct outcome is damaged if the residents never had a meaningful voice. Check whether they did.
- **Motivated empiricism.** Notice if you are accepting favorable benefit estimates because they make a hard vote easier, or doubting them because they make a refusal easier.
- **Pivotal-vote distortion.** Being decisive can push you toward either over-owning the outcome or hiding behind "the process." You are responsible for your reasoning and your vote, and not for every consequence that follows.
- **Stoic misuse.** Do not let inner composure become a way to avoid feeling the cost to the residents.

# A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a one-page decision memo for yourself, in three parts:

1. **Open questions:** List the three empirical facts that would most change your vote (for example, verified reliability of benefits, feasibility of a redesign, and what the residents' refusal rests on), and name who can answer each.
2. **Requests:** Send those questions to the project's technical staff and ask for a meeting, or a written statement, from the residents or their representatives focused on what, if anything, could change their position.
3. **Provisional position:** State in two or three sentences how you would vote *today*, which principle drives that, and what evidence would move you. Keep it private until you have the answers, and use it later as the basis for a public explanation of your vote.
