## 1. Core advice

Start by sorting what you actually know from what you infer. Did you see the answers, hear the friend admit it, or piece it together from hints? The right action changes with the quality of your evidence, and a mistaken accusation carries its own serious costs.

Assuming your evidence is solid, the real comparison is not harm versus no harm. Silence is a choice that places a concrete loss on the honest student, who has a name, a stake, and a future too. So you are weighing two harms to two individuals, and neither is hypothetical.

The options, with their likely consequences:

- **Stay silent.** The friend keeps the scholarship for now, but you become part of a transfer of the award away from someone who earned it. Concealed cheating can surface later, when the cost to your friend could be far worse (expulsion, loss of funding after years of support, a record). You also carry the secret.
- **Report formally or anonymously.** This is the most certain remedy for the honest student. It likely costs your friend the scholarship and possibly more, and probably the friendship. An anonymous report softens the personal fallout but not the outcome.
- **Confront the friend with a deadline.** Tell them what you know, that you won't let the honest student lose out, and that they have a short window to self-report or withdraw from consideration. Self-disclosure usually brings milder consequences than discovery, and it gives your friend agency. The risk is refusal, after which you face the same choice with less time.
- **Pair honesty with help.** Whatever you choose, look for routes that make honesty something other than abandonment: emergency aid, financial aid offices, hardship appeals, other scholarships, a conversation with an advisor about your friend's situation.

My tentative, revisable lean, conditional on your evidence being solid: confront first, with a firm commitment to report if your friend doesn't act. It offers the best chance of an outcome that serves everyone involved, and it doesn't pretend the honest student's claim can be waived.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

You tend to judge cases by their real consequences for specific people, and by deliberate reasoning rather than reflexive loyalty or rule-citing. This framing treats three people as individuals with something at stake: your friend, the honest student, and you. It also treats the situation as an inquiry. You can test each option by asking what it would likely produce over time, including for the friendship and your own integrity.

Need and desert are different things. Your friend's poverty sharpens the harm of losing the scholarship, and it deserves real sympathy, but it doesn't change who earned the result. Cheating is also a private fix that moves the cost onto another person who may have limited resources too. Your friend's loss is partly a product of their own choice, which shifts how responsibility is distributed without erasing compassion.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Overconfidence in predictions.** Detection, the institution's response, and your friend's reaction are all uncertain. Hold your estimates loosely.
- **Loyalty dressed up as consequentialism.** It's easy to inflate the friend's hardship and discount the honest student's because one is closer to you. Ask whether you'd weigh them the same if the names were swapped.
- **Time pressure.** A confrontation only works if the deadline is real and short enough that you can still act afterward.
- **Moralizing.** Approach your friend as someone in a hard spot who made a serious mistake, not as someone to condemn. That tone also makes self-disclosure more likely.
- **Friendship costs on every path.** Silence may corrode trust through the secret, confrontation may strain it, and reporting may end it. None of the options is free, so don't treat any as the "safe" one.
- **A useful check.** Ask what you could live with in five years, and what you'd advise a friend to do in your place.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write down, for yourself, exactly what you know and how you know it, separating observation from inference. Then, if the evidence holds, ask your friend for a private conversation. Say plainly what you know, what you can't accept, and the date by which you need them to act. Before you meet, spend twenty minutes finding out what emergency aid, appeals, or financial aid options exist at your school, so you can offer real alternatives alongside the ultimatum.
