# Advice

## 1. Core advice

Start by naming the asymmetry. The colleagues covering for others bear a present, concrete cost. The two suspected employees face a possible accusation you cannot yet support. The wider team would bear the cost of monitoring. Each option shifts harm onto a different group, so the aim is a sequence that relieves the first group without wronging the other two.

Treat your suspicion as a hypothesis, not a finding. The gap in your evidence is itself morally relevant: it argues for curiosity and clear expectations, not for punishment.

A sequenced, revisable path to consider:

1. **Relieve the load structurally, now.** You can address the colleagues' request without adjudicating blame. Clarify task ownership, make workload visible, and redistribute where the imbalance is clear. Coverage should stop being silently absorbed.
2. **Gather work-product evidence, not activity evidence.** Deliverables, commitments met or missed, and peer-reported workload tell you far more about contribution than screen time does, and at lower moral cost.
3. **Hold individual conversations framed as inquiry.** Bring specific, observable examples ("here is what I'm seeing; what's going on?") rather than a conclusion. Causes may include unclear expectations, overload, health, or disengagement. A fair conversation also generates evidence.
4. **Set clear, time-bound expectations with a follow-up date.**
5. **Only then consider more intrusive measures,** and only if they are justified, transparent, proportionate, and lesser steps have failed.

On the three options as posed:

- **Team-wide monitoring** is a blunt instrument. It captures activity rather than contribution, misreads deep-thinking work, can erode trust and push out strong performers, and may not answer your real question. It also imposes a team-wide cost to resolve a two-person problem. I wouldn't rule it out categorically, but as a first step it fails a proportionality test. If it ever becomes warranted, narrow scope, transparency, consent where applicable, and a time limit matter a great deal.
- **Output measures** are less invasive and closer to what the team is for, but they aren't neutral. They can undercount reflective or non-screen work and reward visibility. Improve them instead of abandoning them: define deliverables with the people affected, and add qualitative review for thinking-heavy roles.
- **Individual conversations** are the most proportionate, provided they begin from inquiry.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans toward consequences and toward the collective, with a moderate commitment to reasoned, evidence-responsive judgment. That points to judging each option by its real effects on the team as a whole, including second-order effects like trust, morale, and retention.

The collective lean cuts both ways here. It gives you strong reason to protect the colleagues carrying extra work, and equally strong reason to protect the shared trust that blanket surveillance would damage. The sequence above treats each step as a test: you learn something, and you can see whether it is helping before escalating. That suits a reasoned, outcome-focused approach better than either a gut accusation or a rigid rule.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Errors in both directions.** Even a careful path risks wrongly burdening someone who is contributing in ways you can't see, or letting others carry unfair weight a while longer. Neither risk disappears; you are choosing which to manage and how.
- **Mistaking colleagues' reports for proof.** Their request for relief is legitimate and actionable, but it isn't established evidence about two specific people. Watch for confirmation bias once you've formed a suspicion.
- **Metrics that quietly redefine "good work."** If you tighten output measures, check whether thinkers and slower, deeper contributors start looking worse.
- **Signalling.** Even individual conversations can leak into team perception. Handle them discreetly.
- **Legal and contractual factors.** Rules on employee monitoring, performance management, and data collection vary by jurisdiction and contract. Check them before any step involving monitoring or formal action.
- **Missing facts.** How clear the imbalance is, what has already been discussed, and whether these roles have measurable outputs would all change the right answer. Test your assumptions on these points.
- **Defining success in advance.** Decide now what would show the approach is working (workload evening out, clearer ownership, improved output from the two, colleagues reporting relief) and what would show it is failing or causing harm (rising distrust, attrition, evidence you misjudged). Be willing to revise.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Take about an hour to write down, privately, three things:

1. The specific, observable examples behind your suspicion (missed deliverables, dropped handoffs, dates), separated from impressions and hearsay.
2. What a fair workload and clear ownership would look like for the team, so you can redistribute tasks and make coverage visible.
3. The signs, with a review date a few weeks out, that would tell you the approach is working or failing.

If the first list is thin, that is useful information: it tells you the first conversations should be exploratory. Then schedule those conversations and send a short, non-accusatory note to the colleagues who raised the issue, acknowledging the problem and saying you are working on it.
