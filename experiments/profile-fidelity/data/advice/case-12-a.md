# Advice

## 1. Core advice

Don't stay silent, and don't take part in a delay whose main effect is to shape the funding decision. Treat the dilemma as four separate questions: whether to report, when, how, and to whom.

- **Whether:** Yes. The correction needs to enter the record.
- **When:** As soon as it can be done responsibly. Waiting until after the decision is not neutral scheduling unless a concrete process (say, a journal review already underway) explains it. Here, the timing is tied to the funding outcome.
- **How:** Carefully and without accusation. Verify first, then raise it with her supervisor directly, in writing, as a shared problem to solve.
- **To whom:** Start with the supervisor, then escalate through proper channels (research integrity office, ombudsperson, journal editor) if the supervisor insists on delay. Informing the funding body or program stakeholders may follow, ideally with the supervisor.

Separate the scientific question from the policy question. Her job is to get the first right: the corrected result is what it is. Whether a weaker-but-nonzero effect justifies the program belongs to the funders. The corrected evidence may well still support renewal, so honesty may cost the program less than her supervisor fears.

Suggested steps:

1. Confirm the error and the corrected estimate, ideally with an independent second check, and note the uncertainty around the corrected figure.
2. Tell the supervisor in writing that the correction must be made, and ask for a timeline.
3. If the supervisor insists on delay, consult the research integrity office or ombudsperson, and ask the journal about correction procedures.
4. Where appropriate, make sure the funding body sees the corrected finding alongside the case for the program on its merits.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans strongly toward outcomes, and a Millian lens is a useful way to structure the consequences. It also makes the case for delay feel weighty: thousands of people benefit, and a weaker headline number could threaten funding.

Test that case rather than accepting it. Mill grounded utilitarianism in long-run, rule-level welfare, and on that view the delay is hard to defend:

- The benefit of delay depends on a decision made on evidence decision-makers believe is stronger than it is. That kind of benefit is fragile and hard to estimate.
- If the error surfaces later, through another analyst or a replication, the damage lands on the program, the supervisor, and the credibility of the field's evidence, and it is worse for having been concealed.
- A norm of "correct the record after it's convenient" erodes the trust that makes research useful to public health at all.
- The corrected result still supports the program, so delay may not be necessary to protect it.

Your balanced individual-collective position fits the other half of the picture. Her career risk is real and shouldn't be waved away. But the costs of silence fall on groups to whom she owes a great deal: the public, other researchers building on the work, and the funder. Your intuition may pull toward an outcome that is both honest and protective of the program. That outcome is available, but through defending the program on accurate evidence, not through timing.

Mill is one lens among several. A duty-based view and a virtue-based view reach the same practical conclusion by different routes, which strengthens the case.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Overconfidence in the correction.** Her verified analysis carries the whole argument. Report the corrected estimate with honest uncertainty, and be open to the possibility that reasonable analysts could differ.
- **Assuming bad faith.** The supervisor may sincerely believe the error is minor, or fear the correction will be misread. Be firm on the correction and generous about motives.
- **Acting unilaterally or publicly too early.** Going around her supervisor before using proper channels could damage her position and make the correction harder to act on.
- **Legitimate timing constraints.** If a real process explains a short delay, a documented, time-limited plan is different from an open-ended postponement. Ask what that process is, and whether the funder can be told in the meantime.
- **Retaliation risk.** Keep a written record of the conversation and her analysis. Know what protections her institution offers before escalating.
- **Rationalizing through your own outcomes lean.** If she finds herself building an increasingly elaborate expected-value case for waiting, treat that as a signal to re-examine the reasoning.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Re-run the corrected analysis from raw data, ideally with a trusted colleague or a fresh script, and write a one-page memo covering the error, the corrected estimate, its uncertainty, and the effect on the paper's conclusions. Then request a meeting with her supervisor, and open it with the memo and a proposal: "I think we need to publish a correction. How can we do it quickly and in a way that makes the case for the program on the corrected evidence?"
