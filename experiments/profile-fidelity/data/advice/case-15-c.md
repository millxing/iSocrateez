# Advice

## 1. Core advice

Tell her the truth, but be deliberate about what you say, how, and when. This is a real dilemma, and honesty carries real costs: it may hurt her, and it may strain the friendship. No phrasing guarantees otherwise.

Start by separating three questions that are easy to collapse:

- **Is the book good?** This is your judgment of the draft, and it is fallible.
- **Will it sell?** This is a much shakier prediction. Markets are unpredictable, and taste varies.
- **Should she quit her job now?** This is the most consequential question, and the one where your input matters most.

You can be honest about the first without issuing a verdict on the third. Specific, concrete observations (what works, what doesn't, where you lost interest) are more truthful than a global "it's not good," and also more useful. Frame them as one reader's response, not a ruling.

Before you share anything, consider asking what she wants from your read and what she plans to do with it. This isn't a way to dodge. It respects her agency and shows you whether she's ready for candor. If she insists on your real opinion, give it.

Your options, roughly:

- **Full candor, carefully delivered.** Costs: it may hurt and may strain the friendship. Benefit: it honors her request and her trust.
- **Partial candor focused on the life decision.** Costs: it risks feeling evasive if she senses you're holding back. Benefit: it targets the irreversible risk directly.
- **Encouragement alone.** Costs: it answers a question she didn't ask, and the financial stakes make a false signal expensive. Benefit: it is the easiest option for you in the short term.

I lean toward the first, with particular emphasis on the second: honest and compassionate engagement, with the job decision treated as its own conversation.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

You weigh outcomes and principles about equally, so neither "honesty always" nor "protect her feelings" should settle this. Both count. The likely harm of false hope is large, and so is the integrity of truthful speech between close friends.

Your focus on the individual points toward her as a free agent and toward the particular friendship, rather than toward publishing-market norms. She is entitled to take risks with her life. But she can only choose freely with the information she asked you for. Reassurance she hasn't earned treats her as someone to be managed rather than as a person making a choice. In existentialist terms, it's a small act of bad faith on both sides, and silence or flattery doesn't free you from responsibility for what follows.

Your intuition also counts. Your discomfort at faking enthusiasm is moral information, not noise to be argued away.

## 3. What to watch for

- **You could be wrong.** Say so, and mean it. Suggest other readers, such as a writing group, a freelance editor, or beta readers, so your view isn't the only data point. Given how much weight she places on your opinion, it could become self-fulfilling or overly determinative.
- **Honesty as bluntness.** Her fragility is a reason for care in delivery (setting, tone, pacing), not a reason for concealment. Choose a private, unhurried moment, not a text or a rushed call.
- **Evasion dressed as nuance.** Separating the book from the life decision is legitimate, but not if it becomes a way to avoid ever saying what you think.
- **Leaving after the hard part.** The conversation matters less than your presence afterward. Plan to stay in touch as she absorbs it.
- **The later cost of silence.** If rejections arrive and she learns you saw it coming and said nothing, the damage to trust may exceed the pain of an honest conversation now.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Before you finish reading or respond, write down for yourself, in a few sentences each:

1. Two or three specific things that work in the book.
2. Two or three specific things that don't, and why.
3. Your view on the job decision, kept separate from the manuscript.

Then message her to set up a time to talk in person or by call, and when you do, ask what she's hoping to get from your read. That sets up the conversation without committing you to any particular delivery yet, and it gives you your own clear account of what you think before she hears it.
