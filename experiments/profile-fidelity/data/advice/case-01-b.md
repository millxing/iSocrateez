## 1. Core advice

You are weighing two defensible principles against each other. One is that a community should not be forcibly removed from its homes and burial ground against its refusal. The other is that you owe real protection to 200,000 people facing real flood and power risks. Neither principle is a mere preference, so the task is to find which one you can stand behind on reflection, and why.

Because your vote is likely decisive, you cannot treat the outcome as the responsibility of the process or of others. Before deciding, test the binary itself:

- **Option space.** Are there alternative designs, a lower dam height, a different site, or flood and energy measures that reduce the harm? Can you attach binding conditions, or demand a delay for independent review?
- **Strength of the claims.** How severe and irreversible is the flood risk, and is it existential or substantial? How certain are the power benefits? How irreversible is the loss to the valley, and can compensation reach what is lost there?
- **Consent.** Is the residents' refusal a veto, a very strong presumption against forced removal, or one claim among many? Decide which you hold before you decide the vote.
- **Control.** You control your vote, your reasons, the conditions you insist on, and how you treat the affected residents. You do not control whether the flood comes, whether other officials cooperate, or how the project is later executed.

If the answers show that the flood threat is modest and alternatives are workable, your principles point toward voting against this version of the project. A vote for it would need a justification beyond aggregate benefit. If the threat is severe and no alternative exists, the weight shifts toward the public. Even then, a vote for the dam should be paired with extraordinary obligations to the displaced community, such as genuine consultation, fair terms, and remedies for what cannot be replaced, like graves and continuity.

Whichever way you vote, you leave moral residue. A vote for the dam leaves a community that was overridden. A vote against leaves preventable risk borne by people who also have rightful claims on you. Own that cost rather than dissolving it through a neat argument.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your strong orientation toward principle suggests you want a decision you could state as a universal rule. The test is reversal: would you accept the rule "a community may be displaced against its refusal when the benefit to a larger group is sufficient" if you were a member of a small community facing that decision elsewhere? If you would not accept it in reverse, the principle is suspect. If you would accept it, you should also be willing to accept the flood risk to the 200,000 as a principled cost of your own rule.

Your moderate collective lean means you will feel the weight of the larger population and of your duty as their representative. Do not dismiss that duty as mere convenience. Your moderate reason lean suggests you will want an explicit argument, while still treating the intuition against uprooting a people from their dead as a check. That intuition may be tracking something real, namely that some losses cannot be fully compensated and do not sit on the same scale as economic or safety gains.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Headcount drift.** Do not let 200,000 versus 400 settle the question. The kind, certainty, and irreversibility of each harm matter as much as their size.
- **Treating refusal as bargaining.** The residents may be refusing a bad offer, not negotiating a price. Ask whether a better offer would be a genuine change in their situation, or only a higher number.
- **Minimizing either side.** Do not treat the valley's loss as sentimental, and do not treat flood protection as mere convenience, especially if lives are at stake.
- **Premature certainty.** You have not been told whether the dam is necessary, whether alternatives exist, or whether expropriation is legally permitted. These facts should shape your judgment, and you should verify them rather than assume them.
- **Confusing a decision with an ending.** Voting does not discharge your obligations to whichever group loses. Plan what you owe them afterward.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Before casting your vote, put these three written questions to the project's engineers, the regional authority, and the residents' representatives, and ask for written answers:

1. What is the flood risk without this dam, stated with its uncertainty, and what alternatives were evaluated and why were they rejected?
2. Which conditions (lower height, phased construction, independent review, binding compensation terms, or a delay) could be attached to approval, and are they legally available?
3. Is the residents' refusal legally binding under the relevant framework, and what remedies exist for them if the project proceeds?

Then write one paragraph stating the principle you would vote on and whether you could apply it in reverse. Your vote should follow from that paragraph, not from the pressure of the moment.
