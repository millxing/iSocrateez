# Advice on the ICU bed dilemma

## 1. Core advice

The choice is between allocating by medical prognosis, which favors the 25-year-old at roughly 85%, and letting moral history count, which would favor the 72-year-old teacher at roughly 40%. Both figures are estimates, not certainties, but the gap is large enough to matter.

My provisional leaning: let prognosis govern, while being honest that the pull of desert (what people morally deserve, given what they have done) is real and that the outcome leaves a moral cost.

**The case for prognosis.** It treats both people as having equal standing. It spares the doctor from acting as a judge of lives. It saves more expected life and more expected life-years. It also fits the epidemic context, where the bed is a scarce public resource.

**The case for desert or contribution.** The teacher's decades of service are a real moral good. The murder is a grave wrong. Ignoring every moral fact can feel like a failure of moral seriousness, not neutrality.

**Testing the desert view.**
- A conviction means the justice system has dealt, or will deal, with the offense. Withholding lifesaving care would be a punishment no court imposed.
- Ranking patients by social worth is unreliable and open to abuse. Once it is allowed here, it is hard to confine to cases where the verdict seems obvious.
- The teacher's goodness is real, but it is not a medical criterion, and it does not close a gap this size.
- Age does not rescue the desert view. A "fair innings" argument (the idea that those who have lived long have had their fair share) cuts against the teacher, since the younger patient has far more expected life ahead.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

You tend to treat this as a concrete encounter between a doctor and two particular people, and you take moral perception seriously. You will likely feel the teacher's goodness as a real pull, and that pull deserves to be treated as evidence, not noise.

You also lean, moderately, toward principle over outcome-counting. That side resists letting reputation or conviction status decide who receives care. Equal dignity and need act as constraints on what intuition is allowed to do.

Intuition is a signal and a possible distortion at once. Perceiving sustained goodness and grievous wrong is not mere bias. But a vivid story about a beloved teacher against a bare label of "murderer" is exactly the kind of asymmetry that skews judgment. We know one person's story richly and the other's barely at all, including any circumstances, remorse, or capacity for change.

There is also a loose existentialist resonance. The doctor cannot hand all responsibility to a protocol, because choosing to follow a rule is itself a choice. It can be made in good faith, as a commitment to equality, or in bad faith, as an escape from owning the decision.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Treating a rule as an alibi.** Prognosis-based allocation is defensible, but it should be owned, not used to avoid feeling the weight of the choice.
- **Smuggling desert in through the back door.** Be wary of nudging prognosis estimates, even unconsciously, toward the patient you favor.
- **False precision.** 85% and 40% are estimates. If the real gap were much smaller, tie-breaking by other factors becomes more debatable, and the argument for prognosis alone loses some force.
- **Institutional constraints.** Real triage guidelines generally exclude social worth and criminal history, and a doctor bound by them has additional reasons to follow them. That does not end the moral discussion, but it changes what is the doctor's decision to make.
- **Moral residue.** Whichever patient is not treated, something real is lost. Regret here is not a sign of having chosen wrongly.
- **Caricature.** The patient with the conviction is still a person with a full claim to care. The teacher's goodness is real even though it does not decide the matter.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write two short paragraphs. In the first, state your decision and justify it without mentioning either patient's story, using only medical need, prognosis, and fairness. In the second, rewrite the case with the stories swapped (the teacher is the 25-year-old with the 85% chance, the convicted patient is 72 with 40%) or hidden entirely, and note whether your reasoning holds.

Then answer one more question in a sentence or two: what would you owe the patient who does not receive the bed? Honest answers to these three checks will show whether your position rests on principle or on the narrative.
