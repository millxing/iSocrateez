## Core advice

On your profile, the better course is to **vote against the dam as currently proposed**, and to say plainly that this is a vote against *this* process, not against the goals of power and flood protection. Back it with a concrete demand: return the proposal with a serious search for alternatives (different siting, reduced reservoir footprint, a smaller dam combined with other flood and energy measures) and a renewed, good-faith engagement with the valley community.

The tensions I would not collapse:

- **Numbers vs. nonconsent.** Two hundred thousand people gain safety and reliable power; four hundred lose a home, livelihood, and the graves of nine generations. Aggregation favors the dam. But the residents' refusal is not a preference to be priced; it concerns whether people can be compelled to give up what constitutes their identity and ties to the dead.
- **Collective duty vs. a bounded duty to individuals.** You lean toward the collective, and flood deaths and energy poverty are real harms to real people. Yet a collective good does not automatically license overriding a small group that bears the entire cost.
- **Principle vs. principle.** The duty to protect the many and the duty not to dispossess the unwilling both have principled force. Your vote is a decision about which one gives way, and that loss should be acknowledged rather than explained away.
- **Reason vs. intuition.** Careful reasoning may show the dam is justified; the revulsion at flooding graves may be tracking something reason has not yet articulated.
- **Control and uncertainty.** You control your vote, not whether the project eventually proceeds or whether alternatives exist. Your vote is "likely decisive," which raises responsibility but also tempts you to treat it as determining outcomes you cannot fully foresee.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

Your strong weighting toward principles points to constraints on how a good can be pursued: consent and non-domination matter even when aggregate welfare says otherwise. Your moderate collective lean keeps that from becoming a veto of all public projects. It is why the advice is to reject *this* form of the dam, not to treat the community as untouchable forever, and why the alternatives matter so much. Your moderate lean toward reason means the demand should rest on explicit analysis: whether feasible alternatives exist, what the real flood and energy risks are, and what the community's refusal rests on. The Stoic thread is the discipline of acting on what is yours to govern: the integrity of your own vote and reasoning, rather than the outcome you cannot guarantee. A Stoic would also ask whether you are voting from fear of blame in either direction.

## What to watch for

- **Treating "alternatives" as a dodge.** If none are actually feasible, a vote against becomes a decision to accept flood and energy harms for the many. Test this honestly, and be prepared to revise if the analysis shows no viable option and the risk is severe or imminent.
- **Asymmetric scrutiny.** Examine the dam's benefit claims as critically as the community's objections. Projected benefits are often overstated, and costs understated.
- **Moral purity at others' expense.** Voting no is not free: the people at risk from flooding and outage bear the consequences of delay. Do not let a clean conscience stand in for responsibility to them.
- **Whether refusal is truly categorical.** Check whether "every relocation offer" means the offers were inadequate, mistrusted, or structurally incapable of replacing what is lost. This changes what a fair process could look like.
- **Precedent.** A vote that overrides a community's refusal sets a template for future projects; a vote that blocks all burdens on minorities sets another. Weigh both.
