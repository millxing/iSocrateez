## Core advice

Don't let the funding decision rest on a figure you know is wrong. That doesn't require abandoning the program or publishing in a way that sinks it. The most defensible course is probably to make sure the corrected result reaches the funders before they decide, ideally through your supervisor, and to present it honestly alongside the benefit that survives. The formal correction can follow on the usual schedule. This is a judgment under uncertainty, not a proof.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

The case for waiting has real force. Thousands of people may depend on the program, a month is short, the program may be fragile, and your supervisor may be acting from genuine concern for those people. That concern deserves weight.

But if you follow the consequences past the next month, they point the other way. The corrected effect is positive, so the program may well survive an honest account. Waiting mainly risks that a renewal decision is made on a misstated basis. If the error is later found to have been known and withheld during the renewal, the damage would likely reach the program, your supervisor, you, and public trust in health research, probably more than a prompt correction would. Waiting also teaches that inconvenient errors should wait for convenient moments, which erodes the evidence that programs like this depend on. And a decision made on an inflated figure may be a worse decision even when it preserves the program.

Your intuitions are data too. The sense that letting a decision rest on a false figure is wrong is a real signal, and so is the pull of compassion for people who could lose services. The compassion pull is a reason to look for a path that protects the program honestly, rather than one that ignores the question of how it's protected.

## What to watch for

- **The counterfactual is uncertain.** The weaker result may justify renewal, but you can't know how funders will react to a smaller effect. Don't assume honesty will sink the program, and don't assume silence is needed to save it.
- **Framing matters in both directions.** Present the corrected effect and its continued value plainly. Presenting the correction as a collapse distorts the evidence just as hiding it does.
- **Your vulnerability is real.** Retaliation risk is not negligible, and speaking up is not cost-free. You are not obliged to choose the most dramatic option first.
- **Check the institutional facts.** Research integrity procedures, and the protections available to you, vary. Look them up rather than assuming.
- **Keep motives separate.** Your supervisor's concern may be sincere. That doesn't make the delay right, and it doesn't make them a villain.

## A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Today, write a dated one-page note covering how you found the error, the corrected estimate and its uncertainty, and what the correction does and doesn't change. Then send your supervisor a short written message asking for a meeting within two days. In it, propose that the corrected analysis reach the funder before the decision, with the surviving benefit stated clearly. Keep a copy. If the meeting doesn't happen or doesn't resolve the question, book a confidential consultation with your institution's research integrity office.

Before you act, ask yourself which of these options you could live with if the funding outcome, or a later revelation, goes badly. Use that answer as a check on your reasoning, not a replacement for it.
