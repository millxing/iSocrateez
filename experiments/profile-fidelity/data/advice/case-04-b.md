## 1. Core advice

The captain should rescue, provided the facts are roughly as described: the boat is in real danger, the ship can recover people without grave risk to itself, and no nearer or faster help will arrive in time. The captain should report to the coast guard immediately, but reporting does not complete the duty. Report-and-continue is defensible only if it actually brings timely help. Six hours in rough seas with a damaged boat usually means it does not.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

The duty to rescue is more than a legal technicality. Maritime law and seafaring custom bind the master personally, and a company instruction cannot discharge that duty on the captain's behalf. The norm also works only if people keep it. Every captain who sails past a boat in distress makes the next rescue less likely, and every captain who rescues despite pressure helps sustain a shared expectation that protects people at sea, including the crew one day.

The company's concern is not frivolous. A prior detention and a smuggling investigation were real harms to real people, and the captain does not decide only for themselves. The crew did not consent to legal jeopardy, and their interests deserve direct weight. But the company's policy responds to a legal climate that punishes rescue. Much of the moral fault for that climate lies with the institutions that created it, not with the captain who is asked to absorb its costs.

Intuition deserves a place here too. The discomfort of sailing away from a boat in distress is evidence about what is owed, not merely a feeling to be overridden by argument. Reason tells you the duty is strong and the risks are real. Intuition tells you what it would mean to see those sixty people fall behind the ship's wake. Both point in the same direction here, and both should be taken seriously.

Consider what kind of captain each choice builds. One who rescues under pressure, accepts costs, and protects the crew through transparency becomes someone whose word on duty can be trusted. One who reports and continues to protect the company becomes someone who has learned to treat a clear obligation as negotiable when it is inconvenient. Test the conclusion against your own sense of what you could live with afterward.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Crew costs are real.** Detention, lost wages, and legal exposure fall on people who did not choose the risk. Consult senior crew where time allows, and record the decision and its reasons. Do not impose the choice silently.
- **Report-and-continue can become moral laundering.** Ask honestly whether the coast guard, or any nearer vessel, will reach the boat in time. If not, reporting discharges a procedural step while leaving the danger in place.
- **Intermediate options are only real if they keep you engaged.** Staying on scene, relaying the urgency of the distress, lighting the area, dropping life rafts, water, or supplies, and summoning nearer assets all address the danger. Options that let the ship sail on while appearing to help are evasions.
- **Unknown facts could change the answer.** The condition of the boat, the sea state, the ship's freeboard and capacity to recover people, and the presence of other vessels all matter. The lean shifts if a nearer, capable rescuer is already responding, or if the ship cannot safely recover people. In that case, stay on scene, provide what you can, and press the coast guard hard for a rescue that can be carried out.
- **Do not minimize the personal risk.** Defying the company may cost the captain their job and expose them to legal action. Those costs are real, and the captain should not be left to carry them alone. Seek legal advice and insist on the company's written support.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

If the boat is in sight now, contact the rescue coordination center by radio immediately. State the position and the ship's intention to proceed to assist, then log the time, the decision, and the reasons. Within the next 24 hours, ask the company in writing to confirm the legal basis of its rescue obligations and the support it will provide to the crew if the ship is detained.
