## Core advice

Don't treat this as a choice among three options. Start with a direct, respectful conversation with your neighbor, paired with a concrete offer to reduce how much he needs to drive. Make the goal fewer solo trips, not just a promise to stop. Decide now what would make you report, so that if it comes to that you are acting on a judgment you made earlier and not drifting into it.

Staying silent is the hardest option to defend now that you know. It isn't monstrous given how little he seems to drive, but it is still a choice, and it leaves the risk unaddressed.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

Weigh the outcomes on both sides. Driving without a license after a failed vision test is a real risk to pedestrians and other drivers. But the harm of reporting is not speculative: it could mean losing his only way to reach his wife and buy food, in a place with no bus route and no family nearby. Enforcement would likely cause severe, certain harm to him. A response that reduces the driving while keeping him mobile and connected to his wife serves both sets of outcomes better than either extreme.

Center the particular person. His marriage, his independence, and his dignity deserve real weight, not just as obstacles to public safety. Your own integrity matters too. Ask what kind of neighbor you want to be, and whether you can live with the choice you make.

Let reason and intuition check each other. Notice when your calculation says one thing and your sympathy says another, and find out which is tracking the facts.

Lightly, Sartre and Beauvoir are useful here. You cannot step outside the choice, because not acting is also acting. And Beauvoir's point that our freedom is bound up with others' concrete freedom applies to both him and the people who share the road. Responsibility here means taking both seriously, not deferring to a rule or to inertia.

## What to watch for

- **Don't dismiss the risk because he has never had an accident.** A clean record is weak evidence. A failed vision test is a direct measure of the capacity that matters, and short, infrequent trips reduce exposure without eliminating danger.
- **A confrontation can humiliate him.** Frame it as concern and curiosity, not accusation. Ask how he's managing before you say what you've noticed.
- **Don't let alternatives become an evasion.** Offers of help may be slow, partial, or refused. If they fail, you will still face the underlying choice, and you should be honest with yourself about that.
- **You don't know several key facts:** how impaired his vision is, how you learned of this, whether local reporting is anonymous, and which senior or volunteer transport services exist where you live. Find these out before deciding.
- **Reporting rules vary by jurisdiction.** Check your local procedures rather than relying on general assumptions.
- **Helping has a cost.** It means time, awkwardness, and ongoing involvement, such as regular rides or check-ins. Be honest about whether you can sustain that before you offer it.

## A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write down two things:

1. **Your alternatives.** Find one specific, realistic thing you could offer, such as a regular ride to the care home on certain days, or the phone number of a local senior services office or volunteer driver program. Vague offers are easy to make and hard to keep.
2. **Your reporting trigger.** Name the conditions that would change your mind: he refuses all help, you see him driving unsafely, his vision is clearly worse than you assumed, or he keeps driving after alternatives are in place.

Then, within the day, look up the senior services or volunteer transport information. Once you have it, plan a short, warm conversation with him that opens with a question about how he is managing and includes the specific help you can offer.
