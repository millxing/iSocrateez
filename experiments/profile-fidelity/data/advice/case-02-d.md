## Core advice

Lean toward **returning the object, with the descendant community, not the museum, setting the terms of any continuing relationship.** Treat "unconditional return" and "shared custody" as points on a spectrum the community chooses along, not as rival policies the museum picks between.

The museum can offer things like conservation expertise, a high-quality replica, documentation, a co-curated exhibit about the object's history and return, and transition funding. But these should be offers, not conditions. If the community wants shared custody, honor it. If it wants full return, don't make the return contingent on museum oversight.

**Tensions to keep in view:**
- **Redress vs. local harm.** The taking was wrongful and the descendants' claim is live, since the object is needed for current worship. But the museum's revenue funds jobs and free school visits, and those losses fall on people who didn't commit the original wrong.
- **Preservation vs. self-determination.** Ceremonial use may wear or change the object. But a living religious object may not be best understood as an artifact to freeze. Insisting on preservation on the museum's terms can repeat the original presumption that outsiders know best.
- **Two publics.** Schoolchildren and local visitors have a real interest in access. The descendants' interest is not just access but standing, meaning authority over something sacred to them. These interests differ in kind, not just in size.
- **Compromise as virtue or evasion.** Shared custody sounds balanced, but if the museum keeps the final say, it quietly preserves the original power imbalance.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

You weigh consequences heavily, trust intuition over derivation, and lean moderately toward the collective. Together, these point toward a particularist reading:

- **Outcomes:** Return directly serves the community's ongoing religious life, a high-stakes and irreplaceable good. The museum's losses are real but mitigable through replicas, new programming, and grants. The community's loss from continued retention is not mitigable in the same way.
- **Intuition:** The sense that holding a sacred object taken without consent is *wrong*, and that "we know how to care for it better" rings hollow, is evidence worth trusting. A particularist would treat it as a reason that carries special weight in this case, without needing to derive it from a rule like "all colonial acquisitions must be reversed."
- **Collective:** You're weighing two collectives, the local public and the descendant community. The latter's claim is stronger because it is tied to identity, worship, and the history of the wrong.
- **Particularism:** No blanket principle settles this. The details decide it: the object's fragility, what the ceremonies actually involve, the museum's financial fragility, and whether the community wants a continuing partnership.

## What to watch for

- **Paternalism disguised as stewardship.** If preservation concerns are the main reason to hold on, ask whether the community has been consulted on conservation, or merely told about risks.
- **Overstating the economic argument.** Check whether the museum's financial danger is as dire as feared. One object being the "main attraction" may reflect a lack of other investment, which return could prompt the museum to fix.
- **Pressure on the community.** Don't make the descendants justify their religious practice or prove its legitimacy. Demanding proof can be another form of control.
- **False consensus.** "The community" is rarely monolithic. Make sure you're negotiating with those legitimately authorized to speak, by their own processes.
- **Intuition's blind spots.** A strong moral feeling can overlook practical facts, such as storage conditions, security, or legal obligations. Let it guide the direction, not replace due diligence.
