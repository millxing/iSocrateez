# Core advice

Treat this as a conflict between two defensible obligations, not a choice between "report" and "look away." The pharmacist owes something to the rule and its purposes (patient safety, controlled stock, fairness to others in need) and something to the particular person standing at the counter. Neither is trivial, and theft remains theft even if the motive is sympathetic.

The binary is also probably false. Before deciding, the pharmacist can look for a third path that takes the violation seriously without making the child's treatment collateral damage:

- Speak with the mother openly, rather than letting her leave in silence, and name what happened.
- Check whether the pharmacy's policy or the law gives any discretion, and whether reporting is mandatory in this jurisdiction or for this class of drug.
- Consult a supervisor, compliance officer, or ethics resource, so the decision is not made in isolation and is documented properly.
- Point the mother to routes that may actually solve the problem: the prescriber, hospital social work, the manufacturer's patient-assistance program, an insurance appeal or external review, charitable foundations.

If none of these are possible and the pharmacist must pick, that choice should be made knowingly, with a clear account of the reasons.

The existentialist lens (one among several) adds a useful point: silence is not a neutral refuge. Letting her leave is a choice the pharmacist authors, and so is reporting. Whichever path you favor, you are responsible for its consequences.

The strongest case for reporting: the pharmacist holds a role others rely on, can't verify the story, and quiet exceptions erode the norm and expose the pharmacist and pharmacy to liability. The strongest case for discretion: the harm of prosecution falls on a critically ill child, the theft was an act of last resort, and rigid enforcement may serve the letter of the rule while defeating its purpose. Ask which of these you find weightier, and why.

# What to watch for

- **Treating the story as established fact.** Compassion that skips verification may be misplaced. Dismissing the story without checking is equally an unexamined choice.
- **Assuming the child's outcome hinges on this one decision.** Other sources of help may exist, and both reporting and silence may be less decisive than they feel.
- **Rationalizing in either direction.** "I'm just following the rules" can be a way to avoid ownership. "I'm just being compassionate" can be a way to avoid accountability.
- **Role and legal exposure.** Whether you are an employee, owner, or licensed professional with mandatory reporting duties changes what is actually available to you.
- **Precedent.** Ask whether you could defend the same choice if the next person's story were less sympathetic or less true.
- **Intuition without testing.** A strong feeling is data, not a conclusion. Ask whether it survives scrutiny.
