## 1. Core advice

Keep three questions separate: what your parent wanted, what each sibling is owed, and what you, as the person entrusted with the request, should do. Don't let them blur.

The note is weak evidence of your parent's final will. It gives no amount, contradicts the spoken instruction, and its date and circumstances are unknown. It may reflect a passing mood, guilt, or an unsent intention. It should not override the equal-split request on its own, but it should not be dismissed either. It is a real signal that your parent may have worried about the caregiver.

Your duty is fidelity to your parent, and fidelity can mean literal compliance or faithful service to deeper intent. Here the two diverge. The most defensible default is to honor the spoken instruction while bringing the note into the open so the three siblings can decide together whether any recognition for the caregiver is appropriate. Acting alone on a guessed amount is the weakest option.

The main paths:

1. **Follow the equal split and treat the note as uncertain.** This honors the explicit, communicated word and the parent's authority over their own estate. The cost is that the caregiver may experience it as a final injustice, and the note may have reflected a real wish.
2. **Adjust toward the caregiver on your own judgment.** This honors apparent intent and the desert of care. The cost is that any amount is arbitrary, you become the sole arbiter, and you may betray the trust the parent placed in the equal arrangement.
3. **Disclose the note to all three and deliberate together.** This honors honesty and the shared bond and keeps the decision collective. The cost is that it may reopen old grievances, pressure the ill sibling, and create conflict that is hard to undo.
4. **Equal division plus a voluntary, transparent gesture from the siblings toward the caregiver.** This honors the parent's word and recognizes care without you imposing a verdict. The cost is that it depends on goodwill: a gift can feel like a consolation prize, and the ill sibling's present need still requires its own answer.

On desert and need: the caregiver's years of unpaid labor are a real claim of desert. The sibling who paid for professional care also sacrificed. The ill sibling's claim is one of need, and illness is not a failure of contribution. Low contribution should not be read as low worth. Because these claims are of different kinds, no formula settles them, and any fixed percentage would be arbitrary without more information.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your preference for principled, reasoned decisions suggests you want a rationale you could state to all three siblings without embarrassment. Paths 3 and 4 do most of that work because they keep the decision within the family rather than resting it on your private interpretation. Your collective orientation suggests the siblings' continued bond is itself something to protect, not merely a means to a fair outcome. Here the procedure may matter as much as the split. A decision everyone understood and reached openly is more likely to survive than a number that seems correct but was handed down.

The difficulty is that no option is costless. Fidelity to words, fidelity to intent, and fairness can pull apart here, and reasonable people rank them differently. Notice which of these you would be least able to live with violating. That answer will often show which path fits your convictions.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Overstating the note.** Don't present it as your parent's true will. Its vagueness is part of its meaning.
- **Discounting the ill sibling.** Their need is acute and not their fault.
- **Casting people as villains.** Avoid framing the caregiver as entitled or the paying sibling as selfish.
- **Concealment.** Keeping the note private is possible, but it makes you the sole keeper of a secret and risks a serious breach of trust if it surfaces later. If you consider it, weigh that cost honestly.
- **Legal limits.** Legal treatment varies by jurisdiction, and a private note may have no legal force. If there is a will or estate, read the actual document and consult an appropriate professional before distributing anything.
- **Your role.** Acting unilaterally may overstep your mandate, while acting mechanically may abdicate responsibility you still hold.
