# Advice on the Captain's Dilemma

## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a real conflict between legitimate considerations, but not a symmetrical one. The duty to rescue persons in distress at sea is among the oldest and most widely recognized obligations in maritime practice, and sixty people in rough seas, with help six hours away, face a danger that may not wait. That gives the duty a strong presumption. The company's directive and the crew's risks are real, but they are mainly costs of acting rightly, not reasons that cancel the duty.

The binary of "rescue" versus "report and continue" is probably too narrow. The captain's options include:

- **Full rescue:** takes everyone aboard. It best serves the duty, but carries real risks of overcrowding, medical strain, and legal exposure.
- **Partial or staged rescue:** takes the most vulnerable aboard, or transfers people as conditions allow. It may be necessary if capacity or safety is limited, but it raises hard questions about who is chosen.
- **Standing by and assisting:** stays near the boat, provides flotation, water, and medical help, and waits for the coast guard or another vessel. It may meet the duty if the boat is stable enough to survive six hours, which the captain cannot assume.
- **Report and continue:** gets help on the way, but leaves people alone in rough seas. It is defensible only if reporting plus a nearby rescuer would truly be safer, and the facts given do not suggest that.

Whichever path is taken, the captain should alert the coast guard immediately, document everything, and notify the company. The aim is to meet the duty while shrinking the legal and operational risks, not to pick one side of a false choice.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans moderately toward principle and toward collective considerations, with reason and intuition in balance. Each of these bears on the case:

- **Principle:** The wrongness of abandonment carries weight here. A duty owed to persons in danger is not simply a factor to be traded against convenience. The company's instruction does not obviously have the standing to override it.
- **Collective concern:** The policy matters beyond this boat. If captains learn that rescue means weeks of detention and suspicion, rescues become rarer for everyone at sea. That is a systemic harm, and it is also a reason to think the company's incentive structure is itself an ethical problem, not just a constraint to be obeyed.
- **Reason and intuition:** Argument and gut response may converge here: the sense that you cannot sail past sixty people is backed by both law and principle. The harder gap is between that urgency and the sober recognition that the crew's risks are real. It is worth noticing which feels heavier to you and asking why.

In the spirit of virtue ethics, one useful question is what practical wisdom looks like in this role. It would likely mean neither reckless heroism nor defensive compliance, but a captain who sees the situation accurately, acts proportionately, and can stand behind the decision afterward.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Assuming facts not given.** We don't know the boat's condition, the ship's capacity, the migrants' medical state, or the exact legal text. Obligations vary by flag state and jurisdiction, so legal conclusions should be checked with maritime authorities rather than assumed.
- **Treating the company as a villain or an authority.** Its fear is grounded in a real event, but its instruction cannot be assumed to override legal duty. Its motives and effects both deserve scrutiny.
- **Reporting as moral cover.** "I told the coast guard" can feel like action while functioning as avoidance. The test is whether it actually makes survival more likely.
- **Seeing the migrants only as victims or as suspects.** They are persons in danger with their own standing, and the earlier smuggling investigation says little about this particular boat.
- **Underweighting the crew.** Overcrowding, unrest, and medical demands are not trivial. A rescue carried out carelessly can endanger everyone, including those rescued.
- **Letting consequences and principle blur.** They may converge here, since rescue is both dutiful and likely to save lives, but they diverge on whether the policy's future harms matter more than this boat. Be honest about which reasoning is doing the work.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a short, private account of how you would decide as the captain: which option you would choose, the single fact that would most change your mind, and what you would want to be true of yourself afterward. Then identify one resource, such as a maritime law summary for your flag state or the International Maritime Organization's guidance on rescue at sea, and read how it frames the duty. Compare it with your own account and note where your intuition and the stated rule agree or part ways.
