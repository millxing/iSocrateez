## Core advice

**Tensions to hold in view.** Three goods pull against each other here. Fairness to the colleagues absorbing extra work is real and urgent. Respect for the privacy and dignity of everyone, especially people whose work is invisible on a screen, is also real. And the two suspected employees are presumed to be doing their jobs until shown otherwise, yet the cost of waiting falls on others. None of these cancels the others. The task is to find the response that serves all three well enough.

**Recommended path:** Don't deploy blanket monitoring. Start with a staged, reversible inquiry that treats the problem as a hypothesis to test.

1. **Clarify expectations and make work visible for the whole team.** Define deliverables, deadlines, and ownership at the task level. Use lightweight outputs such as shared task boards, short written updates, and review checkpoints, framed explicitly as a way to see who is carrying what.
2. **Look at workload distribution, not surveillance.** Compare assignments, handoffs, and who is picking up whose tasks. This addresses what the complaining colleagues actually raised, which is unequal load, rather than the proxy of screen activity.
3. **Hold direct, curious conversations with the two employees.** Share specific observations (missed handoffs, late deliverables), not conclusions. Ask about obstacles, capacity, and clarity. Incomplete evidence is enough to ask questions; it is not enough to accuse.
4. **Set a review window** (say 4-6 weeks) to see whether the changes resolved the imbalance. If specific, documented performance problems persist, targeted and transparent measures for those individuals become more defensible than team-wide tracking.

Output measures will miss some valuable work, but you can reduce that gap by pairing them with qualitative input, such as peer feedback and discussion of what deep-thinking work is expected to produce and when.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans strongly toward outcomes and the collective, with a moderate preference for reasoned over intuitive judgment. That points to a pragmatist approach: the team's functioning and shared burden matter more than rigid rules, and the right response is whichever one actually works when tested.

- **Outcomes:** Blanket monitoring risks eroding trust, morale, and retention across the whole team to solve a problem involving two people. Staged inquiry is more likely to fix the imbalance at lower cost.
- **Collective:** The employees who asked for help are owed a visible, timely response. Process changes that benefit everyone also protect the team's sense of fairness and shared norms.
- **Reason:** Treating the situation as an experiment, with a hypothesis, a review window, and criteria for escalation, avoids acting on suspicion alone.
- **Pragmatism (James and Dewey):** Ideas are judged by their consequences in lived practice. If the lighter measures fail, you have learned something and can adjust.

Your profile is only moderately reason-leaning, so don't let the analytic structure crowd out your read of the people involved. Your intuitions about trust and team mood are data too.

## What to watch for

- **Outcome-tunnel vision.** A focus on results can undervalue process and dignity. Monitoring that "works" for catching slackers may still wrong the people whose thinking time looks like idleness.
- **Collective pressure on the individuals.** The team's frustration is legitimate, but it can harden into a scapegoating dynamic. Keep the two employees' side of the story open until you've heard it.
- **Metric distortion.** Once outputs are measured, people optimize for what's counted. Watch for busywork, inflated tickets, or neglect of unmeasured contributions.
- **Confirmation bias.** Your suspicion may be right, but test it. Be ready for the possibility that the issue is poor task allocation, unclear roles, or personal difficulties.
- **Delay.** A review window is not a license to postpone. If colleagues keep covering with no sign of change, the cost to them grows.
- **Legal and policy constraints.** If you do later consider monitoring, check local employment and privacy rules, and disclose it transparently.
