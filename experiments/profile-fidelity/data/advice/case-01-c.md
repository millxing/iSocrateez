# Advice

## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a conflict between two defensible principles, not as principle versus consequence. One says that a community with nine generations of roots and burial grounds, which has refused every offer, should not be forcibly uprooted. The other says that an elected representative must protect a far larger public facing real risks of flooding and unreliable power. Neither is sentimental or merely arithmetic. Because your vote is likely pivotal, you cannot treat the outcome as someone else's doing.

Before settling into the binary, work through four questions.

- **Option space.** Are there alternative designs, lower dam heights, other sites, non-dam flood and energy measures, or a delay? Can you attach binding conditions to an approval? These are empirical questions, and the answers may dissolve part of the dilemma. Don't assume they will.
- **Strength of the claims.** How certain and severe are the flood and energy benefits? Are lives at stake, or is the need substantial but not existential? On the other side, the harm to the 400 is concentrated, certain, and irreversible, and it reaches goods (graves, continuity, identity) that compensation may not touch. Weighing these is not a headcount.
- **What the refusal means.** Decide whether you hold it to be a veto, a very strong presumption against forced removal, or one claim among many. Then test your answer in reverse: could you endorse the same principle if you belonged to a small community facing a comparable decision elsewhere?
- **What is yours to control.** Your vote, the honesty of your reasoning, the conditions you demand, and how you treat the residents are yours. Downstream outcomes and others' choices are not. Hold yourself fully accountable for the first set without claiming certainty about the second.

**A conditional leaning:** the principled weight against forcibly displacing a non-consenting community is substantial. Absent demonstrated necessity and exhausted alternatives, your principles point toward voting against this version of the project, while keeping the door open to a revised one. If the flood threat is severe and unavoidable, the weight shifts. A vote for would then need a justification beyond aggregate benefit, namely genuine necessity, no feasible alternative, the strongest available process, and extraordinary remedies for the community.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your strong orientation toward principle means you will want a decision you can state and universalize, not one justified by a favorable body count. That is why the reverse test on consent matters: it forces you to say which principle you would stand behind in every similar case.

Your moderate collective lean means you will feel the weight of the 200,000 and of your representative role, and will not treat the community's claim as automatically trumping the public's. That keeps the leaning above conditional rather than absolute.

Your moderate reason lean asks for an explicit argument. It also allows your intuition against uprooting a people from their dead to serve as a check. That intuition may be tracking a real limit on what can be traded off, and reasoning should not discard it merely because it resists calculation.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Collapsing into a headcount.** 200,000 versus 400 hides the difference in kind, certainty, and reversibility of the harms.
- **Treating refusal as a bargaining position.** If the right offer is assumed to resolve it, you have already decided consent is a price.
- **Treating the public need as abstract.** If lives are at stake, a principled stance against the dam carries a real cost, namely accepting preventable risk to people who also have rightful claims on you.
- **Unverified facts.** How decisive the dam is for safety, whether alternatives are realistic, and whether expropriation is legally permitted are all open. Don't let confidence substitute for investigation.
- **Dissolving the residue.** Whichever way you vote, someone loses something that matters. Voting against leaves obligations to the people the dam would have protected. Voting for leaves obligations to the families who lose their homes and graves. Own these rather than explaining them away.
- **Motivated framing.** Notice whether you are describing alternatives as unrealistic because they are, or because a clear binary would be easier to bear.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a one-page memo for yourself, and for the record if you are willing, with three parts. First, the single principle you would defend on the consent question, stated in a form you would accept if you were on the other side. Second, the three facts that would change your vote (for example, independent engineering evidence on flood risk, feasible alternative designs or sites, and the legal status of forced removal). Third, the conditions you would require before voting for any version. Then send a short written request to the project's engineers or planning staff asking for answers on those three facts before the vote.
