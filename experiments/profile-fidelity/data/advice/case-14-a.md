# Core advice

Treat this as two separate obligations that have become tangled: what you owe your friend, and what, if anything, you owe his son. Paying the son does not discharge the debt, and repaying the debt does not settle the son's situation. Keeping them apart is the first step toward acting honestly.

Your three options are also not the full menu. The one that best fits the facts is probably a fourth: **talk to your friend directly.** An honest conversation lets you raise the old loan, learn what "forget about it" meant to him, and, if it seems appropriate, find out whether he is aware of his son's difficulty. It spares you from deciding alone what he intended, and it avoids both silent repayment and covert rescue.

On "forget about it": treat it as evidence, not a verdict. People say it in the moment both as a real release and as a kindness that eases the borrower's shame. Twenty years of silence from him is some evidence for the first reading. But your own comfort with that reading deserves scrutiny. Ask what he would say if you put the question to him today.

On the son: you barely know him, and how you learned of his trouble matters. If it came in confidence, acting on it may breach that confidence. A quiet gift is a defensible act of generosity, but it carries concealment costs, and it should not stand in for facing your friend.

# Why this fits your ethical profile

You lean toward principle: keeping one's word, fairness, and the integrity of obligations. That makes the unresolved debt likely to keep bothering you, and it makes a purely "convenient" reading of forgiveness hard to rest on. The principled question is not "was the debt legally cancelled?" but "what does honoring a gift of this kind require of me?" Sometimes the answer is repayment. Sometimes it is accepting the gift gracefully and expressing gratitude in a way he would recognize.

Your moderate collective lean means the family web matters here: you are a debtor, a friend, and now someone aware of a trouble inside his family. Confucian role ethics, which is one useful lens among several, would ask how to fulfill those roles well, with sincerity and propriety, rather than seeking one rule. On that view, a sincere and direct acknowledgment between friends is usually more fitting than a transaction conducted behind his back.

# What to watch for

- **Self-serving interpretation in either direction.** Using his words to avoid repayment is one risk. Using repayment or a secret gift mainly to relieve your guilt is the other. Ask whose discomfort the action chiefly eases.
- **Insulting a gift by refusing it.** A large repayment to a wealthy friend may feel to him like a rejection of what he gave. Gratitude and repayment are not the same thing.
- **Humiliating the son.** If he learns that a near stranger quietly stepped in, or that his father was bypassed, the help may wound more than it relieves.
- **Humiliating the friend.** He may feel his family's affairs were managed around him.
- **Unverified facts.** Confirm the original sum, what it is worth to you now, and what you actually know about the son's situation and whether his father already knows.
- **Proportion.** The right option may change with the size of the sum relative to your means. For large amounts, a financial or legal adviser is worth consulting on the practical side.

# A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Take twenty minutes to write down, privately, three things: (1) what you honestly believe your friend meant by "forget about it," and what evidence supports that; (2) how you learned of the son's trouble, and whether it was shared in confidence; (3) what you would want to say to your friend if there were no money involved. Then use the third answer to draft a short, sincere message or invitation to see him, one that opens the door to the conversation without yet deciding what it will conclude.
