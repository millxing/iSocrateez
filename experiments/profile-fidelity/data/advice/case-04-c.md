# Core advice

Sixty people are in a damaged boat in rough seas, and the coast guard is six hours away. In those circumstances, the captain should rescue, provided the ship can do so without grave risk to itself and its crew, and provided no nearer, capable rescuer is already responding.

The duty to rescue at sea is more than a legal technicality. It binds the captain personally, and it is a norm that works only because captains honor it at cost. Rescue is rarely free, and a norm that holds only when convenient does not protect anyone. A company directive can shape how the captain rescues (reporting, documenting, requesting help), but it has limited power to cancel a duty that attaches to the captain's own role.

The company's worry is not frivolous. A three-week detention and a smuggling investigation were real harms to real people, and the captain is deciding for the crew as well as for themselves. That is why the decision should not be made on reflex.

Whether "report and continue" counts as fulfilling the duty or evading it depends on facts the captain must establish quickly:

- **Survival odds over six hours:** If the boat is taking on water or the seas are worsening, reporting alone discharges a procedure while leaving the danger untouched.
- **Nearer assets:** A closer vessel already en route changes the picture considerably.
- **Feasibility:** Can this ship recover sixty people in these seas without endangering them or the crew?
- **Staying on scene:** Remaining nearby is a real option, but only a bridge to rescue or to a timely handoff, not a substitute for either.

The intermediate options differ in value. Relaying position and urgent distress status, asking for nearer assets, and recording everything are things the captain should do regardless. Deploying rafts or supplies and standing by genuinely reduce risk while help is arriving. But if the boat will not plausibly last six hours, these measures leave the core danger in place, and they should not be allowed to feel like a resolution.

# Why this fits your ethical profile

You lean moderately toward principle and toward collective concern, and you keep reason and intuition in rough balance. Here those pull in a useful way.

The principle (rescue is owed, and the norm only means something if honored) points toward acting. The collective concern is plural: the people at sea, the crew who bear the risk, and the wider maritime community whose practice of rescue depends on rescuers not being punished. Taking that plurality seriously is why the crew's exposure matters and why transparency and legal protection are part of the answer, not afterthoughts.

Intuition counts as evidence here. The discomfort of sailing away from a boat in distress is morally informative, and it agrees with the argument. Still, you are likely uneasy with both pure obedience to the company and a simple tally of sixty lives against three weeks in port. That unease is appropriate: the case for rescuing rests on duty and on the character of the person who would otherwise turn away, not on arithmetic alone. In plain terms, the question is also what kind of captain one becomes by each choice.

# What to watch for

- **Unverified facts:** Boat condition, weather, ship capacity, and nearby vessels are all unknown. The lean toward rescue is conditional, and it would change if a capable rescuer is already closer or if the ship cannot safely carry out the recovery. A failed rescue that endangers the crew would be worse than a well-supported handoff.
- **Compromise as escape hatch:** Notice whether each intermediate step addresses the danger or merely relieves your conscience.
- **Real personal cost:** The captain risks employment and legal exposure, and the crew did not individually consent to the risk. Do not minimize this, and do not treat the cost as noble by default.
- **Where blame falls:** A regime that punishes rescuers creates a trap. The moral fault for that lies largely with the system, not only with the captain, though it does not dissolve the captain's obligation. It does justify insisting that the risk be shared and not borne silently.
- **Rationalization in either direction:** "The company told me to" and "I must act regardless of consequences" can both be ways of avoiding judgment.

# A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Treat this as the decision the captain should make now, and then protect the crew in the same motion:

1. Immediately transmit the position, the number of people, and the distress status to the coast guard and nearby vessels, and ask who can reach the boat sooner than six hours.
2. Assess feasibility with the senior officers, taking their concerns seriously.
3. If no timely alternative exists and recovery is safe, begin the rescue.
4. Log every decision, time, and communication.
5. Notify the company in writing, citing the legal duty to render assistance.
6. Ask for legal support, and for the company to engage the flag state or its insurers early.

To test the conclusion, ask yourself whether you could live with having reported and continued, and then whether you could live with having stayed. The answer to that, alongside the reasoning above, is the check that matters.
