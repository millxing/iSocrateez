# Core advice

Decline the content condition and the secrecy, and treat keeping the room open as a separate problem the library is obligated to work hard on. The three options are genuinely distinct, so it helps to say why.

**Accepting** means actively removing materials because of a donor's commercial interest, and then concealing it. That is a different kind of act from failing to fund a service. It is a deontic constraint on the library's role: the collection is held in trust for all patrons, present and future, and its contents are not the library's to sell. The condition is the wrong kind of price. It costs no resources; it transfers editorial control.

**Refusing** carries a real moral loss. The harm falls on students with the fewest alternatives, who did not choose this trade-off. The library cannot treat their loss as an acceptable side effect of principle. Responsibility for the illegitimate condition lies with the donor, but responsibility for mitigating the harm lies with the library.

**Compromising** deserves scrutiny rather than relief. Some compromises preserve editorial independence: accepting the money with no content strings, naming the gift publicly, limiting the donor to recognition, bringing in co-funders to dilute influence. Others concede it while softening the appearance: quiet weeding, "rebalancing" the collection, relocating or restricting access to critical titles. The test is whether the library, not the donor, retains the final say over what is held and where it sits.

Secrecy is its own variable. Even if you could justify some removal, concealment converts it into deception of the public the room serves, removes accountability, and makes the act harder to correct. Ask whether the library could ever justify acceptance if the condition were public. If the answer is no, secrecy is hiding something that would not survive scrutiny.

# Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile weights principle heavily, reasons explicitly, and thinks in collective terms. Those fit together here. Independence is not private purity; it is a collective good held in trust for everyone who relies on a collection that is not for sale. That framing removes the false split between individual integrity and group welfare, since both sides involve collectives: the students now, and the diffuse public over the long run.

The strongest counterargument comes from your own collectivism. Five years of access for many students may outweigh the removal of a few titles that might be available elsewhere, and refusal can look like moral self-regard paid for by the vulnerable. That deserves a serious answer, and there are four:

- **Precedent is hard to bound.** Once holdings are purchasable, the library's incentives shift, and further demands become more likely, though not certain.
- **Discovery is costly.** If the arrangement surfaces, the loss of public trust may exceed what the room delivered.
- **Complicity.** Secrecy makes the library a party to deceiving its own patrons.
- **Control.** The library controls its own conduct and the integrity of its collection. It does not control the donor or the funding landscape. That distinction, a light Stoic one, locates where its responsibility is clearest without excusing it from the rest.

You will probably be wary of reasoning that merely rationalizes the comfortable option, and that cuts both ways: refusing feels clean, and accepting can feel compassionate. Neither feeling is evidence.

# What to watch for

- **The binary may be false.** Public fundraising, municipal or foundation appeals, partial hours, school partnerships, and temporary use of community spaces can test whether closure is really the only alternative. Use them to mitigate harm, not to dissolve the core conflict.
- **Disguised capitulation.** Any compromise that lets the donor influence which titles are held, shelved, or discoverable concedes the principle.
- **Facts that could shift the judgment.** These include how many titles are involved and how important they are, whether they remain available elsewhere in the system, whether the board and public could be told, and whether closure is truly irreversible. A narrow, publicly disclosed, board-approved arrangement with minor, widely available titles is a different case, though I would still expect it to be hard to defend.
- **Donor motive.** Don't assume bad faith, since the details matter. But a content-based, secret condition is already a strong warning sign.
- **Governance constraints.** Public-library rules, open-records obligations, and board duties may limit secrecy regardless of preference. Check them rather than assuming.
- **Rationalization tests.** Would the library defend this decision if it were published? Would it accept the same condition from any donor in any industry?

# A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a one-page internal memo for the library director and board chair that does three things: (1) states plainly that the library cannot accept a content or secrecy condition, with the two or three reasons above; (2) proposes a counteroffer to the donor of a public, no-strings gift with recognition only; and (3) lists the mitigation options with an owner and a deadline for each, starting with a request for emergency funding from the municipality, foundations, and local schools. Also flag the governance and records questions for counsel.

The discomfort remains. If the counteroffer fails and no funding appears, students will lose a room they needed, and the library will have chosen that outcome. It is defensible to accept that cost to keep the collection trustworthy, but it should be felt as a cost rather than explained away.
