## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a genuine conflict rather than a choice between "report" and "stay silent." Both options carry real costs, and the strongest path probably lies in a sequence: speak to your friend first, give her a short, real chance to correct this herself, and be prepared to report if she doesn't.

The reasoning, laid out in terms of outcomes:

- **Silence** protects your friend in the short term, but the cost lands on a specific person: the honest student, who loses a four-year opportunity through no fault of her own. Silence also leaves your friend holding a secret that could unravel later, with worse consequences, and it makes you a knowing party to the outcome.
- **Reporting immediately** upholds fairness and removes your complicity, but it may cost your friend her education, which could be disproportionate to a single act, and it ends the friendship's trust with no chance for repair.
- **Confronting your friend first** keeps more options open. She may disclose voluntarily, which usually changes how institutions respond. It also tells you whether she sees this as a mistake or as something she would defend.
- **Self-disclosure** is the best outcome if it happens: fairness is restored, the friend takes ownership, and the harm to her may be reduced. It depends on her cooperation and on a deadline you set.
- **Reporting with context** (her financial situation, her willingness or unwillingness to come forward) lets you honor fairness while advocating for a proportionate response.

Your friend's hardship explains the pressure she was under, but it doesn't make the honest student's loss disappear. And the cheating doesn't make your friend's precarity irrelevant to how you respond. Both can be true at once.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile weighs consequences over rigid rules, favors concrete obligations to particular people over abstract institutional duty, and prefers explicit reasons to intuition. That shapes the analysis in three ways:

- **Consequences:** "Cheating is against the rules" doesn't settle the matter for you, and it shouldn't. What matters is what each path does to real people: your friend, the honest student, and the trust other students place in the process.
- **Particular people:** Your obligations here are to two specific individuals, not just to "the institution." The honest student has a personal stake you can name, which makes her claim as weighty as your friend's, not weaker because she is less close to you.
- **Reasons over pull:** Your loyalty to your friend is a real consideration, not a bias to suppress. But test it: would you feel the same pull if the beneficiary were a stranger in the same financial position? If the pull is mostly about closeness, that's worth knowing when you weigh it against the honest student's claim.

This resembles a pragmatist approach, treating the situation as an inquiry whose options you test against how they would likely play out. It's a descriptive fit, not a framework you need to adopt.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Unknown facts that could shift the picture.** I'm assuming the evidence is solid, that the honest student is a real and identifiable competitor, and that the scholarship goes to the top scorer. Check these where you can:
  - Is your knowledge firsthand or secondhand?
  - Does the university have a retake, appeal, or non-expulsion remedy? Penalties may range from a grade penalty to expulsion, and you don't know which applies.
  - Is the honest student also in financial need? Don't assume she is better off.
- **Rationalizing delay.** "Giving her time" can quietly become indefinite silence. Set a deadline before you talk to her, ideally before the scholarship is awarded.
- **Bargaining from a position of threat.** Confronting your friend is different from pressuring her. Aim for an honest conversation, not an ultimatum that makes her defensive.
- **Asymmetric imagination.** It's easy to picture your friend's loss vividly because you know her. Make an equal effort to picture the honest student's situation.
- **Overestimating the harm of reporting and underestimating the harm of silence, or the reverse.** Neither "reporting is betrayal" nor "silence is complicity" is automatically true. Each needs an argument, and the arguments depend on the facts above.
- **Structural help.** Emergency aid, other scholarships, or financial counseling could address the root of your friend's situation. These don't excuse the cheating, but they matter for what a proportionate outcome looks like.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Do two things:

1. **Find out the facts you can.** Read your university's academic integrity policy and the scholarship rules, noting the range of penalties, whether voluntary disclosure is treated differently, and when the award is decided. Don't discuss the situation with others yet.
2. **Ask your friend to talk privately.** Tell her plainly what you know and that you can't pretend you don't. Ask for her account, then say you want her to come forward herself within a specific window (for example, before the scholarship is finalized), and that you'll need to act on your own if she doesn't. Offer to help her find financial alternatives and to go with her to disclose if she wants that.

Decide beforehand what you will do if she refuses, so the conversation doesn't turn into drift.
