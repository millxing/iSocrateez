# Advice on the layoff decision

**Moral tensions at stake**

- **Policy vs. consequences.** The performance-only rule exists to protect fairness and shield managers from favoritism. But applying it mechanically can produce outcomes (a pension forfeited, a child's sole provider unemployed) that the rule's authors may not have weighed.
- **Desert vs. need.** Performance data speaks to what people have contributed, not to what losing a job will cost them. Both are morally relevant, and they point in different directions.
- **Loyalty vs. fairness.** Twenty years of service creates a real claim, but honoring it at the expense of the newer colleague treats tenure as a trump card. The single father's vulnerability is just as real, though his claim rests on need rather than history.
- **Present team vs. future team.** Retention considerations (the two top performers are leaving) tempt her to optimize for who stays. But using "likely to stay" as a criterion punishes people for honesty, and it quietly treats colleagues as assets.
- **Her own responsibility.** She cannot hand this choice entirely to the policy or entirely to her sympathies. Either move is a way of avoiding ownership.

## 1. Core advice

Don't treat this as a choice between "follow the policy" and "override it." Start from the policy as the default, since it is the fairest shared baseline, and then ask whether there is a *specific, defensible reason* to deviate that she would be willing to state openly.

Practically:

- **Check the numbers first.** How large is the performance gap between the bottom two and the third-weakest? If it's slim, the "weakest" label is weaker than it looks, and room for judgment opens legitimately.
- **Look for ways to reduce the harm before choosing who bears it.** Early retirement or a bridge arrangement for the colleague near her pension, severance weighted to need, redeployment, or a longer notice period can change what each outcome actually costs. Her duty is partly to expand the option space, not just pick within it.
- **Exclude the retention factor.** The top performers' intention to leave is a business risk to plan for separately. It shouldn't determine who loses a job.
- **Be wary of ranking suffering.** Who "needs" the job more is hard to know and invites assumptions. Weigh need only where she has concrete, verified information, and consider whether she'd be comfortable explaining that reasoning to everyone affected.

If the gap is real and no mitigation is possible, following the policy while doing everything possible to cushion the impact is defensible. If she deviates, she should do so transparently and with the company's knowledge, not quietly.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

You weigh outcomes heavily, focus on the individuals involved rather than abstract institutional claims, and trust intuition over formal derivation. That leans toward attending to concrete human consequences for these particular people, not toward rule-worship.

The existentialist strand matters here: she is *choosing*, and the policy doesn't absolve her. Sartre would call hiding behind "the rule says so" a form of bad faith, but the same logic condemns hiding behind "I was just being compassionate" if it avoids confronting what the choice does to others. Your intuition that the pension and the dependent child matter is a signal worth honoring, but it should be tested against fairness to the other six, who also face the consequences of whatever precedent she sets.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Intuition as rationalization.** Sympathy for the veteran colleague may be partly about her own relationship and discomfort. Ask whether she'd feel the same toward an equally long-tenured stranger.
- **Hidden bias in "need."** Assumptions about a single father's situation, or about whether a near-pension employee is "fine," may be inaccurate.
- **Policy exposure.** Deviating from a stated rule can create legal or discrimination risk (age, family status) and can undermine trust if it comes to light.
- **The remaining team.** Colleagues will read the decision as a signal about what the organization values. A visibly principled process matters as much as the result.
- **Moral residue.** Even a well-reasoned choice leaves a real loss. Expecting it to feel clean is a sign something is being avoided.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a one-page memo to herself with three parts: (1) the actual performance gaps among the bottom three or four, (2) any mitigation options she can ask HR about, such as early retirement, severance adjustments, or redeployment, and (3) the single reason, stated in a sentence, that she would give to each affected person for her decision. Then request a short conversation with HR to ask what flexibility the policy genuinely allows before she decides anything.
