## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a tension to hold, not a puzzle with a hidden right answer. The doctor cannot avoid choosing. Deferring to a protocol, flipping a coin, or going by arrival order are also choices, and the doctor owns them.

The central question is whether past conduct and social contribution should bear on a life-saving allocation at all. Separate that from the clinical question. Clinically, 85% versus 40% is a large gap, though both figures are estimates with real uncertainty. Morally, the question is whether anything about who these people have been should shift the balance.

A defensible position is to let prognosis carry the decision. Equal consideration of persons, and a refusal to rank lives by worth, are principles you could accept for any patient. Another reasonable doctor might weigh things differently, for instance by treating a 40% chance as close enough to justify other considerations. What matters is that the reasons are open, consistent, and ones the doctor can defend publicly.

Real hospitals use institutional triage protocols for this situation. Understand them rather than inventing criteria from scratch.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans toward individual-centered reasoning and intuition, with a moderate lean toward principle.

- **Individual focus:** Each patient has an immediate, equal claim to the bed. Neither claim is erased by a biography. This supports not letting the murder or the teaching decide.
- **Intuition:** Your gut response to each patient is evidence, but it is likely pulled toward the sympathetic teacher and away from the killer. Test it by asking whether you would accept the same rule if the roles were reversed: a beloved figure with the better odds, or a despised one with the worse odds.
- **Principle:** Your moderate lean toward principle suggests that a rule such as "past conduct does not rank claims to life-saving care" should weigh more than raw outcome maximizing, while still letting the survival difference count.
- **Existentialist spirit:** Following Sartre and de Beauvoir, the aim is authentic choice. The doctor owns a decision made openly, rather than hiding behind a rule or chance to avoid responsibility.

You may underweight the collective side. The teacher's decades of service are a real good, but treating them as credit that buys priority risks letting social usefulness decide who deserves to live. Consider that argument rather than dismissing it by reflex.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Sentimentalizing and demonizing.** Both distort. The crime is a matter for the legal system. It is unclear that an ICU bed is a fitting place to continue punishment.
- **False precision.** Treat 85% and 40% as estimates. Ask how confident the clinicians are, and whether the gap holds up under scrutiny.
- **Smuggling in worth.** "Life-years" or "social value" arguments can quietly turn into ranking persons. Check whether you would apply the same criterion universally.
- **Hiding in procedure.** A protocol can be the right answer, but invoking it to avoid owning the outcome is evasion. Accept the responsibility that comes with whichever route you choose.
- **Costs on each side.** Prioritizing prognosis may feel to many like rewarding a killer and failing a respected teacher. Weighing desert risks making life-and-death access depend on judgments of worth, with likely bias and inconsistency. Neither option is free.
