## 1. Core advice

Treat the problem as two problems. The colleagues covering the work need relief now, and the suspicion about the two employees is a hypothesis you haven't tested. Don't let the first push you into acting on the second.

Start by changing the workload structure: make coverage visible, reassign or clarify ownership of open work, and stop absorbing the overflow silently. Then gather evidence from work products, such as deliverables, commitments, and missed or met deadlines. Only after that, hold specific, inquiry-framed conversations with the two employees. Treat team-wide monitoring as a later option, justified only if these steps fail and it is narrow, transparent, and proportionate.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile weights consequences for the whole team, so the costs of each option matter more than their principles alone. The covering colleagues bear a concrete, ongoing cost. The two suspected employees face an accusation that is unconfirmed. The wider team would bear the cost of surveillance to resolve a problem created by a few. Monitoring everyone to catch two people spends the team's trust, and trust is hard to rebuild once spent.

The evidence gap is itself morally relevant. Acting as if you know more than you do risks wronging someone, and the sequenced approach limits that risk while still responding to the people who asked for help.

The sequence also works as an experiment. Each step tells you something: whether the workload shifts, whether work products show a pattern, and how the two employees respond when given clear expectations. You can judge each step by what it reveals and what it costs.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Monitoring measures activity, not contribution.** Keystrokes and screen time misread people who do their best thinking away from the screen. They can also reward visible busyness, and they may push out strong performers who feel distrusted.
- **Output metrics aren't neutral.** They can undervalue slow, reflective, or coordinating work. If you use them, design them with the people they will measure, and include qualitative review for thinking-heavy roles.
- **Conversations can become accusations.** Open with specific, observable examples and a question, not a conclusion. Watch for causes you may not have considered: unclear expectations, overload, health, or disengagement. Also consider that the colleagues' picture may be incomplete. They may not see the full range of work each person does.
- **Legal and contractual limits.** Employee monitoring is regulated in many places, and rules vary by jurisdiction and contract. Check with HR or legal counsel before any monitoring, and before formal action on performance concerns.
- **Errors in both directions.** You could wrongly burden an innocent person, or let others keep carrying unfair weight. No path removes both risks. Choose the one whose errors are easiest to notice and correct.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a one-page list of the team's open work. For each item, record the stated owner, who has actually been doing it, and roughly how long it has taken. Keep it factual and avoid conclusions about individuals.

Then send a short message to the colleagues who raised concerns. Thank them, say you're taking the workload issue seriously, and ask one question: which specific tasks have been falling to them, and since when? Tell them you will confirm a redistribution decision by a set date, such as the end of next week.

Before you act, define what success and failure would look like. For example, success might mean the covering colleagues' load drops, key deliverables are met, and ownership is clear. Failure might mean the load stays uneven after two weeks, or the team's trust visibly declines. Agree with yourself now on what would make you change course.
