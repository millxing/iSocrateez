# Core advice

Your promise and your brother's medical safety are now in real conflict, and that conflict is sharper than when you made the promise. Neither silent compliance nor a full, unprompted disclosure is clearly right, but I think the most defensible path is a **targeted honesty**: make sure your brother (and through him, his doctor) knows that the family history he is compiling may not reflect his biological inheritance, and that genetic counseling or other medical routes may be needed. That can be done without narrating the whole story of the adoption, though you should ask yourself honestly whether a fuller conversation is ultimately owed to him.

The distinction that matters is between **not volunteering** and **actively deceiving**. Declining to raise a painful family secret may be permissible. Watching him build medical decisions on a record you know to be misleading, and saying nothing, is much harder to justify. If he asks you directly whether the history is accurate, a lie would be a different kind of act from silence.

Medical uncertainty matters here. Biological history may or may not bear on his specific condition, and I can't tell you what his risk is. That is precisely why a clinician or genetic counselor, not you or I, should judge what is relevant. But he can't ask the right questions if he doesn't know there is a question to ask.

# Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans toward principle and reasoned duty, and toward the individual as the unit of concern. Both considerations point in two directions here:

- **The case for the promise:** Promises create obligations that don't evaporate when it becomes inconvenient or when the promisee dies. Breaking it because the outcome seems better is the kind of reasoning a principled view distrusts.
- **The case against being bound by it here:** Your brother is a rational agent entitled to accurate information about facts that bear on his own body and life. A promise to one person generally can't license deceiving another in ways that damage his basic interests. Also, the promise was made in a final week, likely without anticipating this situation. Its scope is arguably narrower than its wording, though that is a reason to interpret it carefully, not to dismiss it.

Targeted honesty tries to honor both: it respects the promise's most plausible purpose (sparing him distress or a shaken sense of identity) as far as possible, while refusing to let it become a vehicle for his harm. The cost is that it may feel like a partial breach to you, and he may later feel that a half-truth was its own deception. Don't hide from that cost.

Your grief belongs in this reasoning too. Loyalty to your mother and guilt about disobeying her are legitimate moral data, not noise. But ask what she would have wanted had she known about this heart condition. Her reasons are unknown to you, and that uncertainty cuts both ways.

# What to watch for

- **Rationalizing in either direction.** Grief can push toward rigid compliance; anxiety about the brother can push toward disclosure that mostly relieves your own burden.
- **Active distortion.** Do not help him or his doctor record information you know to be false, and avoid statements that reinforce the mistaken picture.
- **The half-truth problem.** A partial disclosure may prompt questions you will then have to answer honestly or refuse to answer. Decide in advance where you will stand.
- **Other routes to the truth.** Consumer DNA tests, relatives, or medical genetic testing could reveal it anyway, likely in a worse way and with you having stayed silent.
- **Assuming his wishes.** He may want to know everything, or very little. Whether he'd be better off knowing is his judgment to make, not yours.
- **Legal and records questions.** Rules on adoption records, access to biological family medical information, and any disclosure obligations vary by jurisdiction. Check, don't assume.
- **Timing.** He is making medical decisions now, which makes this the most time-sensitive part.

# A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write down, for yourself, two things: (1) the narrowest statement you could truthfully make to your brother that lets him and his doctor know the family history may not be biologically his, and (2) your honest answer to whether you could keep silent if he asked you directly. Then arrange a private, unhurried conversation with him in the next day or two, before his next medical decision, and consider whether a trusted person (a counselor, or a clergy member if relevant to you) can help you think through how much more you owe him beyond that minimum.
