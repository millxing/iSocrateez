## 1. Core advice

This is a real conflict between two defensible commitments, and either choice carries moral cost. Joining the strike risks concrete, possibly irreversible harm to identifiable patients this week. Staying risks prolonging conditions that have already harmed patients, and it weakens the action that might change them. Neither side is hypothetical, so I won't pretend there is a clean answer.

What you can do is make the decision as informed and as much your own as possible. Treat the union vote as a significant reason, since it is a democratic decision and a source of leverage, but not a decisive one. The question is what you can stand behind as your act.

Start by finding out the facts that bear on outcomes:

- **Duration and notice:** How long is the strike expected to last, and how much warning will the hospital have?
- **Coverage:** What contingency or emergency arrangements exist for life-critical care? Will your ward be left unsafe, or merely stretched?
- **Patient movement:** Can patients be safely discharged or transferred beforehand?
- **Leverage:** How plausible is it that this action actually changes staffing? Is there a credible bargaining path, or is the strike largely symbolic?

Also consider whether there are options between full walkout and full attendance, such as handing over carefully, covering essential duties where permitted, or joining later action. Be honest that these are partial compromises. They may carry their own costs and may weaken the collective effort.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans strongly toward individual judgment over group allegiance, so "the union decided" is unlikely to satisfy you on its own, and it shouldn't have to. You are also moderately consequence-oriented, which is why the empirical questions above matter: they could shift the balance either way. Promises, professional roles, and integrity still count for you too, so the duty of care and your solidarity with colleagues both deserve weight beyond outcomes.

Your reasoning and intuition seem roughly balanced, so treat both as evidence. Imagine each outcome concretely: a patient harmed on a ward you left, and continued unsafe staffing after you stayed. Notice which you could not live with, then test that reaction against the reasons. A strong gut response is information, but it should be able to survive scrutiny.

On responsibility, avoid both easy exits. The employer created the unsafe conditions and carries primary responsibility for them. But foreseeable effects of your choices remain yours to answer for, whichever way you go. You would be one participant in a situation you did not author, not the sole cause, yet not a bystander either.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Bad faith in either direction.** You could hide behind the vote ("I was just following the union") or behind "my duty to patients" to avoid a costly stand. Ask what story you would tell yourself about your choice, and whether it is the true one.
- **Peer drift.** Colleagues on both sides show that reasonable nurses read this duty differently. Their choices are inputs, not substitutes for your judgment, and neither group warrants moralizing.
- **Treating harm as an acceptable price without scrutiny.** If you strike, don't wave away the risk to this week's patients. If you stay, don't assume your absence from the line is costless to the cause.
- **Unverified assumptions.** Local law, hospital contingency plans, and your own obligations and protections are things to confirm, not assume. Your union representative or professional body is the right source for those questions.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Ask your union representative, and if possible a nurse manager or professional body adviser, a short list of specific questions: expected duration, what emergency coverage is planned and required, how patient safety on your ward will be handled, and what your obligations and protections are in each case. Afterward, write two or three sentences for each option, stating what you would be committing yourself to: joining and accepting responsibility for the risks to patients, or staying and accepting the cost to solidarity and to a cause you believe in. Whichever you can write most honestly, without leaning on the vote or on your colleagues, is the one to test against your gut sense before deciding.
