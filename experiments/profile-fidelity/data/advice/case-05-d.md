# Advice

**Moral tensions I'm weighing**

- **Loyalty vs. fairness:** Her friendship and her sympathy for the friend's poverty pull against the claim of an honest student who stands to lose a fairly earned outcome.
- **Compassion vs. desert:** Need is a real moral consideration, but it doesn't make the scholarship the friend's by right. The scholarship is explicitly allocated by exam performance.
- **Consequences for one vs. consequences for many:** Beyond the two students, a scholarship system that rewards cheating loses its integrity, and future honest applicants bear that cost.
- **Certainty vs. uncertainty:** She "discovered" the cheating. How solid is her evidence? Acting on a mistaken belief would harm an innocent friend.

## 1. Core advice

Don't treat this as a binary between exposing and protecting. Start by confirming what she actually knows, then talk to her friend directly: tell her what you know, and give her a short, firm chance to self-report or withdraw from scholarship consideration. If the friend refuses, report it to the relevant authority (the instructor or academic integrity office), framing it as a factual disclosure and, where possible, asking that the friend's financial need be considered in other aid channels.

The pragmatist test: which course leaves the fewest irreparable harms? Silence permanently harms an honest student, and it leaves the friend exposed to later discovery under worse circumstances. A direct conversation first preserves the most options and the most trust.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans strongly toward outcomes, individual-level concern, and reasoned analysis. That fits an approach that:

- **Counts consequences honestly,** including the less visible ones: the honest student's lost four years, the friend's risk of far harsher penalties if caught later, and the erosion of trust in the scholarship process.
- **Keeps the focus on specific people,** not abstractions. The honest student and the friend are both real individuals with real stakes, and neither is only a symbol of "the system."
- **Treats the situation as inquiry.** In the spirit of Dewey and James, you test options against how they'd likely play out in lived experience. A private conversation is an experiment with low cost and high information value, and it may reveal context she doesn't have.

The outcomes-first view cuts against the instinct to protect the friend. Even on purely consequentialist terms, the harm of concealment (a stolen opportunity, a corrupted award, a compromised friendship built on a secret) likely outweighs the harm of disclosure, especially when disclosure can be paired with advocacy for the friend's need.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Rationalizing from sympathy.** Need can quietly turn into "she deserves it more," which isn't hers to decide for the selection committee.
- **Overconfidence in the evidence.** Check what she saw and whether there's an innocent explanation before confronting anyone.
- **Moralizing in the conversation.** If it comes across as an ultimatum from a judge, the friend may become defensive, not accountable.
- **Delay.** The longer she waits, the more she becomes complicit, and the harder reporting gets.
- **Underestimating the cost to herself.** The friendship may not survive this, and she should accept that possibility instead of hoping it won't happen.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write down, privately, exactly what she saw or learned, how she knows it, and what alternative explanations exist. Then ask her friend for a private conversation, and prepare one plain opening sentence, such as: "I know about the exam answers, and I want to talk about what we do before this gets decided." Also find out the policy and deadline for reporting, so the friend's window to self-report is real and not open-ended.
