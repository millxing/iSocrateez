# Advice

## 1. Core advice

Start by naming the two claims as claims of particular people, not as "justice versus safety."

- **The prisoner** has a claim not to be held for a crime he did not commit. The harm is ongoing, imposed by the state, and each year is unrecoverable.
- **The informant and their family** have a claim not to be sacrificed through a disclosure they did not consent to and cannot control. The informant accepted some risk, but probably not exposure through a court filing. The family accepted none.

Now test candidate principles by asking who could reasonably reject them.

- *"Never risk an informant, whatever the cost."* The prisoner could reasonably reject this. It makes him bear a lifetime of unjust confinement to protect an investigative practice.
- *"Always disclose exculpatory evidence, regardless of danger."* The informant and family could reasonably reject this. It treats them as costs to be absorbed, without consent or protection.

A more defensible principle: the state owes the prisoner correction of the wrong, and owes the informant and family protection and a real voice. So the detective's first duty is to **exhaust options that satisfy both claims** before accepting any forced tradeoff. In practice:

1. **Test the evidence.** "Compelling" is not certain. Look for independent corroboration (records, forensics, other witnesses) that could reach the same facts without the informant.
2. **Ask the informant.** Their informed wishes are the missing input. Don't assume consent or refusal.
3. **Secure protection first.** Relocation or witness protection before any disclosure, while being honest that such programs are not reliably safe.
4. **Use protective legal mechanisms.** In camera review, sealed filings, or a conviction integrity unit may let a court act on the evidence without public exposure.
5. **Escalate rather than act alone.** Bring in a supervisor, prosecutor, or review unit. A decision this heavy should not rest on one person's judgment if it can be avoided.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile balances outcomes and principles, leans toward the individual, and favors explicit reasoning. Each shapes the advice above.

- **Individual focus:** Neither side is an aggregate. Deterrence effects and institutional trust matter, but they are secondary to the concrete persons here, especially a family who chose nothing.
- **Reason over reaction:** Horror at injustice and loyalty to a source are both strong pulls, and neither should settle the matter unexamined. The rejectability test is a tool for that scrutiny, not a school you must adopt.
- **Balance of outcomes and principles:** The intermediate steps respect both the duty to correct a wrong and the consequences of exposure.

The doing/allowing distinction deserves skepticism here. Disclosure is the detective's act, but the detective now *knows*, and has a role within the system holding the prisoner. Silence is therefore not a clean omission. It may be a form of complicity, even if it is less direct than exposure.

## 3. What to watch for

- **False binaries.** The steps above are reasoned moves, not escapes, but they may not be available. Don't use them to postpone the decision indefinitely.
- **Silence treated as costless or permanent.** Waiting for a safer path is defensible only if you are actively working toward one.
- **Uncertainty on both sides.** The evidence is compelling, not conclusive, and exposure is almost certain, not certain. Ask which uncertainty you are weighing most heavily, and why.
- **Unilateral action.** Leaking, or quietly burying the evidence, bypasses the informant's voice and institutional checks.
- **Residual hard case.** If no safe path exists, the claims are not symmetrical. The prisoner suffers a certain, ongoing, severe harm. The informant and family face a probable, grave, possibly irreversible one, and the family did not consent. If the informant gives informed consent, the analysis shifts significantly toward disclosure. Without it, the detective should be very reluctant to expose a family to lethal risk unilaterally, while still not treating the prisoner's confinement as something that can wait. Either path leaves moral residue, and that residue is not a sign you reasoned badly.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Document precisely what the evidence shows and where it could be independently corroborated. Then request a confidential consultation with a conviction integrity unit or a trusted senior prosecutor, without naming the source, to learn what protective legal mechanisms exist in your jurisdiction. Do this before approaching the informant, so that when you do, you can offer them real options rather than only a risk.
