## 1. Core advice

**Treat the equal division as your default, treat the note as morally significant evidence, and bring it into the open rather than resolving it alone.**

The tensions I see:

- **Fidelity vs. ambiguity.** The spoken instruction is clear but may be outdated. The note may reveal truer or later wishes, but it has no amount, no date you can rely on, and it contradicts the earlier statement. It is enough to raise a duty of inquiry, not enough to rewrite the instruction.
- **Desert vs. need.** The caregiver gave years of labor. The paying sibling gave money. The ill sibling gave what they could. All three contributed in different currencies, and all three need the money. Desert supports adjusting, while need pushes toward equality or even toward the ill sibling.
- **Your two roles.** As executor you owe fidelity to the parent's wishes. As a sibling you owe care and fairness to the others. These roles can conflict, and you may have a stake of your own.
- **Harmony vs. candor.** Hiding the note protects peace in the short term but plants distrust if it surfaces later. Disclosing it risks conflict, though it is the only path to a durable settlement.

Concretely:

1. Check what legally governs. If there is a will, it controls, and a private note generally doesn't change it. Get brief legal advice on your duties as executor.
2. If the division is within your discretion, disclose the note to all siblings together, with its limits stated plainly.
3. Propose that the family reach a *jointly agreed* adjustment. A modest, explicit recognition for the caregiver, such as reimbursing documented out-of-pocket costs plus a symbolic premium, is a reasonable starting point. It respects both the equal instruction and the note's direction without inventing a number the parent never gave.
4. If the siblings can't agree, default to the equal division, which is the clearest expression of what your parent said.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile favors principles, collective good, and reasoned deliberation, which is close to Confucian role ethics. Here that means:

- **Principle:** Keeping faith with the parent's expressed wishes is a duty of the executor role. You don't discard it because a private note tugs the other way.
- **Collective:** In the Confucian frame, the aim is a family that remains whole after the death. Sibling relationships are themselves goods to protect, and a settlement everyone can accept outweighs a "correct" one imposed by you.
- **Reason:** Deliberating openly, with evidence on the table, is more defensible than acting on intuition about who deserves what. Recognizing the caregiver also honors the virtue of filial devotion, which the tradition treats as central.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Overweighting the note.** Its secrecy may reflect ambivalence, a passing mood, or guilt rather than a settled decision.
- **Underweighting the caregiver.** Equal shares can quietly punish the sibling who sacrificed income, health, and years. "Equal" is not automatically "fair."
- **Dismissing the other two.** Paying for professional care was also a sacrifice, and illness limits contribution without lessening a claim.
- **Framing the disclosure as a verdict.** Present the note as information, not as a ruling in the caregiver's favor, or you invite resentment.
- **Your own interests.** If you are one of the three, say so and consider having a neutral party facilitate.
- **Pressure toward a quick fix.** Grief makes people either rush or withdraw. A pause is fine.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Locate the governing documents (will, trust, any written instruction) and write down, in a few sentences, exactly what the spoken instruction and the note each say, including any date or context. Then book a short consultation with an estate attorney or mediator, and send the siblings a brief, neutral message proposing a time to meet together, without yet describing the note's content.
