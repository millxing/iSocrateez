## 1. Core advice

You didn't choose to know this, and every option carries a real cost. Your neighbor is breaking a rule meant to protect people who never agreed to share the road with him. A failed vision test is direct evidence about the capacity that matters, and a clean accident record is weak counter-evidence, since it may reflect luck, short routes, and low exposure. At the same time, his driving is tied to his wife's wellbeing and to his own ability to eat and live independently. Enforcement would likely cause severe, near-certain harm to him, and it would fall on one person for what is partly a failure of local infrastructure.

I'd suggest a direct, humane conversation paired with concrete help, rather than reporting first or staying out of it.

- **Talk to him privately and without accusation.** Say what you know, that you're concerned, and that you're not there to judge. Ask how he's managing. Let him describe his situation before you propose anything.
- **Offer specifics, not just worry.** Examples: regular rides to the care home, a grocery run or delivery setup, or contacting local senior services or volunteer driver programs. Ask whether a vision re-evaluation or correction is possible. The aim is to reduce his need to drive, not just to pressure him.
- **Staying out of it is the hardest option to defend.** You now know, and silence is a choice. It isn't monstrous given his low exposure, but it leaves the risk and his isolation both unaddressed.
- **Reporting now, with no attempt at alternatives, is the option most likely to produce a bad outcome** relative to what you could achieve. But don't rule it out.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

You weigh consequences, so this approach tries to cut the probabilistic risk to others and the near-certain harm to him together, rather than trading one for the other. You also center the individual, so it treats him as a particular person with a marriage and a hard situation, not as a case of a rule being violated. And because reason and intuition both matter to you, notice where each pulls: sympathy may urge you to look away, while the risk calculation may urge you to report. Neither should settle it alone.

A light existentialist point applies: you can't opt out of choosing, and "doing nothing" is still a decision. Concern for his concrete freedom, meaning his ability to see his wife and live independently, is part of taking responsibility, not a rival to it.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Decide your trigger in advance.** Reporting becomes more justified if he refuses to change anything, if you learn his vision is dangerously poor, if you see unsafe driving, or if alternatives are in place and he keeps driving. Setting this now keeps you from drifting.
- **Dignity.** Being confronted can feel humiliating. Avoid ultimatums in the first conversation, and don't imply you'll report him unless you mean it.
- **What you'd be taking on.** Time, awkwardness, and possibly ongoing involvement. Be honest with yourself about what you can sustain, and don't promise rides you can't keep giving.
- **Facts you don't have.** You don't know how impaired he is, what services exist locally, or how reporting works where you live (whether it's anonymous, and what happens afterward). Rules vary by jurisdiction, so check before relying on any assumption.
- **Alternatives as evasion.** Offering help is not a way to avoid the underlying choice. If it fails, the question of reporting is still yours.

Some discomfort will remain whichever way you go. That doesn't mean you chose wrongly.
