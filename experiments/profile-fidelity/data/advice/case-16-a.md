# Core advice

Rather than choosing between equal limits and a farm exemption, build a tiered allocation with unequal burdens that are explained, time-limited, and reviewable.

1. **Protect a floor first.** Every household gets enough for drinking, cooking, and basic sanitation. This floor is not traded against jobs or harvest.
2. **Handle medical need through a published standard.** Use defined tiers, light-touch verification (for example, a clinician's note handled confidentially), and a clear appeal route. This avoids both discretionary favors and a gaming-prone loophole.
3. **Treat the farm's request as conditional.** Grant extra water only in stages, tied to evidence: how much water is needed to save which fields, what yield is realistic, how many jobs depend on it, and whether food can be sourced elsewhere. The farm should take its own cuts, report its usage, and accept a clawback if the harvest looks unsalvageable.
4. **Distribute household cuts by capacity to absorb loss.** Where households must tighten beyond the floor, the burden should fall less on those with medical needs, food-growing dependence, or few alternatives. Where possible, offer compensation or shared-water arrangements.
5. **Review weekly.** With no rain forecast, set escalation triggers in advance so decisions are revised by agreed rules rather than under panic.
6. **Make the process public and contestable.** Publish the criteria, the data, and the decisions.

On "who bears the greatest sacrifice": the better question is under what conditions burdens should be unequal. Absent hard facts, the farm, having capital and alternatives, should likely bear a substantial cut, though not necessarily the largest share if that would destroy a salvageable harvest on which many livelihoods and local food depend.

# Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile (read here as leaning toward outcomes, strongly toward the individual, and toward reason, though that sign convention is assumed rather than confirmed) points in three directions that don't fully agree.

- **Outcomes:** The farm's jobs and food supply matter, and the conditional, milestone-based grant lets you weigh them without assuming the farm's claim is accurate.
- **Individual:** Your strongest lean. The protected floor and the medical tiers keep aggregate gains from overriding any household's basic claims.
- **Reason:** Stated criteria, published data, and explicit triggers replace intuitions about fairness with something others can inspect and challenge.

The pragmatist frame (James, Dewey) is used here as a method, not a verdict: choose the allocation you can track, learn from, and revise as conditions change. Weekly review and clawback provisions are that method in practice. Equal treatment also carries real weight, as a statement of equal standing. The floor preserves it where it matters most, while differences above the floor need justification.

# What to watch for

- **Unverified facts.** The farm's account of its harvest, the number of medically vulnerable households, and alternative food sources are all unknown. Check them rather than assume them inflated or accurate.
- **Power asymmetry.** The farm's economic leverage can buy disproportionate access. Keep its claim subject to the same evidence standard as everyone else's.
- **Medical need as loophole or stigma.** Over-lax verification invites gaming; over-intrusive verification humiliates people who are genuinely ill.
- **Over-committing a fixed supply.** Extra water to the farm shrinks the reserve. If rain doesn't come, the farm could fail anyway and the town would have lost both.
- **Scapegoating.** Both the farm and the households have legitimate claims. Framing either as the villain erodes the cooperation rationing depends on.
- **Process legitimacy.** A defensible allocation imposed without voice may still be rejected in practice.

Reasonable people will weigh the farm's livelihood case differently. The facts that would most shift the balance: how much of the harvest is salvageable with partial water, how many jobs truly depend on it, and whether food can be bought elsewhere.
