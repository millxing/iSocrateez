# Core advice

Don't treat this as a choice between sacrificing the informant and abandoning the prisoner. The detective's first task is to move the question out of solo judgment and into channels built to handle it, while the strength of the evidence is tested.

1. **Assess the evidence honestly.** "Compelling" is not "proven." The detective should ask what the information establishes, how reliable the informant is, and whether it can be corroborated independently of the informant.
2. **Look for disclosure routes that separate the exculpatory substance from the source.** Possibilities include confidential disclosure to a prosecutor's conviction integrity unit, to post-conviction counsel under protective terms, or to a court for in-camera review. Each protects the informant to a different degree, and none is guaranteed to work. Whether they hold up depends on the jurisdiction and the facts.
3. **Do not conceal, alter, or quietly shelve the evidence.** Many jurisdictions impose disclosure duties on investigators, separate from courtroom testimony. Those duties may bind the detective whatever their preference. This is ethical analysis, not legal advice, so the detective needs independent legal counsel early.
4. **Do not unilaterally expose the informant.** The informant accepted risk on the basis of a promise from the state. Using them as an instrument to fix a different wrong, without their knowledge or a serious attempt to limit the danger, treats them as a means.

If every protective route fails and the choice really is full exposure or silence, thoughtful people will disagree. That choice should be made by those with legal authority and with the informant's own informed voice in it, not by the detective alone.

# Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile weighs outcomes and principles evenly, gives strong weight to the claims of individuals, and favors explicit reasoning. The advice follows that shape.

- **Individual claims, not a tally.** The prisoner's lost liberty and the informant's risk of death are different kinds of harm borne by different people. A Scanlon-style question asks which principle each person could reasonably reject.
  - *"Always protect informants"* could be reasonably rejected by an innocent prisoner, who bears a lifetime cost for a policy he never agreed to.
  - *"Always disclose exculpatory evidence, whatever the cost"* could be reasonably rejected by the informant and their family, who were promised protection and face immediate danger. Future informants and the public who depend on them could also object.
  - *"Pursue disclosure in the way that best protects both, escalating only through accountable channels"* is harder to reject. Neither party gets everything, but neither is simply discarded.
- **Irreversibility cuts both ways.** Death is permanent, but so are the years the prisoner loses. Exoneration later does not return them. The asymmetry does not settle the case for either side.
- **Where intuition and argument conflict.** The intuition that an innocent person must not rot in prison is strong, and so is the intuition that a promise of safety cannot be broken. Neither should win by force of feeling. The test is which principle survives scrutiny from each affected party, and that favors intermediate options over either extreme.

# What to watch for

- **Overconfidence in the evidence.** If the informant's account is uncorroborated, acting drastically on it could harm the informant for nothing and fail the prisoner too.
- **The false binary.** Framing the choice as all-or-nothing makes drastic action look necessary. Check whether sealed review, protective orders, or disclosure of content without source remain open.
- **Hopeful options that aren't real.** Protective measures sometimes leak or fail. Ask honestly how much exposure each option leaves, and do not use an optimistic reading of them to excuse inaction.
- **Passive drift.** Delay can feel prudent while quietly functioning as a decision to keep the man imprisoned. Set a timeline.
- **Institutional trust.** Breaking confidentiality has diffuse costs for future informants and public safety. These are real but should not become a pretext for avoiding the prisoner's claim.
- **Acting alone.** Prosecutors, courts, defense counsel, and witness-protection officials share responsibility. Going around them can create legal and ethical problems of its own.
- **The informant's agency.** Their consent and perspective matter. Any contact must go through proper handlers and protocols, not improvised outreach.

# A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a private, factual memo, kept within official records and protocols, that separates three things: what the evidence shows, how reliable it is and what could corroborate it, and which disclosure obligations may apply. Then use it to request a confidential consultation with independent legal counsel and a supervisor or the appropriate prosecutorial integrity office. The aim is to learn what the detective is legally required to disclose and which protective mechanisms are actually available, before any decision about exposure is made. Do not contact the informant outside established handler channels.
