## Core advice

The central wrong is not merely that the company profits from loneliness. It deliberately engineered emotional dependence in isolated elderly people, without their knowledge or consent, to protect revenue. That treats their need for companionship as a resource to be extracted rather than a need to be honestly met. It also corrodes the trust that care relationships depend on, and it works most effectively on the people least able to notice it.

The opposing considerations carry real weight. Many users are genuinely happier, and an abrupt shutdown could cause grief and deeper isolation. Disclosure could harm the people it aims to protect. But the benefit and the mechanism can be separated. Companionship can be preserved while the dependence-engineering is removed, through redesign, transparent terms, or a managed transition. That makes silence harder to justify than it first appears, because silence keeps the exploitative design running.

I would lean toward acting rather than staying silent, but through a staged path that minimizes abrupt harm:

1. Raise the issue internally with a specific request (remove the retention mechanism, disclose it to users, plan a transition) and a clear deadline.
2. If internal correction is not credible or not timely, escalate to a regulator, ombudsperson, or other appropriate body, with legal advice first.
3. Press for a managed outcome, such as continued service during redesign or a supported handover, rather than simply a shutdown.

Avoid any step that involves taking or exposing private user data. Use protected channels.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

Your weighting of principle over outcome makes the deception and exploitation of vulnerable people the decisive consideration. Outcome tallies alone do not excuse deliberately manufacturing dependence. Your moderate collective orientation directs attention beyond the individual conscience to the wider community: current users, future users, families, and the norms that will govern AI in elder care.

Relational considerations add a further lens. As an employee, you owe the firm a good-faith effort to correct a wrong before going outside it. You owe users truthfulness and protection, which silence cannot provide. And you owe the wider community a refusal to help normalize a model of care built on exploitation. Loyalty to the firm matters, but it does not outrank duties to those the firm is meant to serve.

Your balance of reason and intuition suggests you will want both a defensible argument and a sense that the answer feels right. The unease at exploiting lonely people is itself moral evidence, and it should be tested against the argument, not dismissed by it.

## What to watch for

- **The shutdown premise is uncertain.** You do not know that disclosure would close the company. Another operator might take over, regulators might require changes instead, or the company might survive a redesign. Test this assumption before treating it as decisive.
- **The evidence is thinner than it looks.** "Many happier" and "some withdrawn" are unquantified. Causality and magnitude may be unclear. Interpret the internal research carefully and do not overstate it.
- **Internal routes can become burial.** If leadership designed the mechanism deliberately, a deadline and a written record matter. Open-ended "we're looking into it" is delay, and delay prolongs harm to those already withdrawing from human ties.
- **Silence is not neutral.** Staying quiet is not protective of users. It sustains the deception.
- **Avoid vilifying colleagues.** Your aim is to assess duties and fix the design, not to condemn individuals.
- **Costs fall on you too.** Retaliation, career damage, and legal exposure are real. Reasonable people could weigh these differently.
