# Core advice

The conflict is real, and neither side should be dissolved. Five probable survivors weigh heavily against one person forcibly put into the water, a person who has not consented and who is being used, in effect, as the group's way out. Both pulls deserve respect.

Before accepting the binary, test it. "Will probably sink" is not "will sink." Bail hard, throw out gear, redistribute weight, and take a few minutes to see whether the water gain slows. Rotating people into the water while they hold the boat is also worth considering if conditions allow. Irreversible action under panic and probabilistic belief needs a stronger justification than the situation initially offers. Alternatives that spread the burden should be exhausted first.

If the choice truly becomes binary, because the boat is demonstrably going down, here is a tentative and provisional view: a shared, minimally coercive process is more defensible than either paralysis or arbitrary selection. Invite volunteers first, and say plainly that no one is obliged to. If no one steps forward, use a lottery that everyone agrees to in advance. Do not choose by pregnancy, age, or profession. With survival odds unknowable, those rankings are guesses about worth dressed up as judgment.

This view has costs. A lottery treats people as equal, but its loser is still forced, so it reduces the wrong without removing it. Refusing to act is not cleaner either. Doing versus allowing matters morally, but when a group could have averted six deaths and chose not to, "we only let it happen" is a comfort more than an argument. Reasonable people, you included, may land elsewhere.

# Why this fits your ethical profile

You lean collective and moderately outcome-sensitive, so the survival of the group as a whole carries real weight for you, and a sacrifice that saves five is not something you can wave off. Your strong intuitive and relational side resists reaching that conclusion coldly. The approach above tries to honor both: it accepts that one loss may be necessary in the worst case, but insists that the group reach it together, with voices heard and fairness visible, rather than by a few people imposing a ranking.

Your intuitions here are evidence, not verdicts. The urge to shield the pregnant woman and the boy may track real care for the vulnerable. It may also smuggle in a hierarchy of whose life counts more. Part of the care is asking whether sparing them means condemning someone else on grounds you could not defend to that person.

# What to watch for

- **Treating the premise as settled.** Panic narrows options. Ask whether "someone must go" is a fact or a conclusion reached too fast.
- **Intuition as bias.** Notice if you are ranking people by social value and calling it compassion.
- **Fairness as a cover.** A lottery can launder coercion. The loser still faces force, and the group should not pretend the procedure makes it painless.
- **Pressure on the vulnerable.** A "volunteer" who feels cornered by the group's silence or stares is not freely consenting.
- **Moral residue on every path.** Survivors who imposed a choice, who watched someone volunteer, or who refused to act will each carry something different. Weigh how you could live alongside the others afterward, not only the decision itself.
- **False cleanliness.** Neither the one who forces nor the one who refuses is a hero or a villain by default.
