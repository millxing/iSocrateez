## Core advice

You are facing a real conflict between two defensible commitments, and either choice will carry moral cost. Treat this as a decision to make on grounds you can own, not as a puzzle with a hidden correct answer. The union vote matters as a collective, democratic judgment and as a source of leverage, but it does not settle your individual responsibility. Your own reasoning has to carry the decision.

Before you choose, answer three sets of questions:

- **Outcomes:** How long is the strike expected to last? What emergency or life-critical cover is planned, and how much notice is required? Would your ward be left unsafe, or only stretched? Can patients be transferred or discharged safely? How plausible is it that this action changes staffing?
- **Responsibility:** Staffing levels were set by the employer, and the harm already done flows from those decisions. But foreseeable effects of your own action remain yours to account for, whichever way you go.
- **Ownership:** What would you be choosing, and what account would you give yourself afterward?

## Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile suggests you weigh consequences seriously but will not treat outcomes as the only test. You are also unlikely to be satisfied with "the union decided" as a reason. You want to act on something you can stand behind personally. That makes the outcome questions important, but it also makes your own judgment the final court.

Your felt sense also counts as evidence. Imagine two futures in concrete detail. In one, you walked out and a patient on your ward was harmed under thinner cover. In the other, you stayed and unsafe staffing continued, with the harm that has already occurred repeated. Notice which you could not live with, then test that reaction against your reasons. Neither the feeling nor the analysis should override the other automatically.

## What to watch for

- **Hiding behind the vote.** "The union decided" can become a way to avoid owning the choice. Ask whether you would still choose this if the vote had gone the other way.
- **Hiding behind duty of care.** "I can't leave my patients" can also be a convenient way to avoid a costly stand. Ask whether the concern is real and specific, or whether it is a shield against discomfort with conflict.
- **Treating the employer as the only author of harm.** Systemic blame does not erase your share of foreseeable consequences. Equally, do not carry the full weight of outcomes that stem from understaffing itself.
- **Assuming facts you have not checked.** Strike duration, contingency arrangements, local rules, and likely effectiveness are all unknown to you right now. Each could shift the balance.
- **Middle paths that quietly defeat the purpose.** Partial participation, such as handover arrangements or covering only essential duties, may be honest compromises, but they can also weaken the collective action. Be clear with yourself about which it is.
- **Judging colleagues.** Those who cross the line may read the duty differently, and so may those who strike. Reasonable nurses disagree here.

## A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Contact your union representative and ask specific questions: expected duration, emergency and life-critical cover, required notice, what staff are expected to do during the action, and whether partial or alternative forms of participation exist. Ask about your obligations and protections as well, and take questions about legal or employment matters to your union or professional body, since I cannot advise on those.

Then, before you talk to anyone else about it, write two short paragraphs: one describing what you would do if you walked out, and one describing what you would do if you stayed. For each, write what you would say to yourself about it a year from now. Read them back and note which one you could sign your name to.
