## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a question about what the promise required, not only whether to keep it. Your father asked you to keep the store open. The deeper aim was likely that the store survive, that the family not abandon what it stood for, and that the people who depend on it be looked after. If the store cannot break even, running it until the savings and the children's future are gone may defeat that aim while still technically honoring the words.

My tentative view is this: if your records show no credible path to break-even, a sale or an orderly wind-down that protects employees is likely the more faithful way to honor the promise. If the numbers show a realistic path to sustainability, keeping the store open is the stronger option. Your children's security is a more direct duty than a promise whose wording may not have anticipated this situation, so it should not be spent on a store that is only delaying its closure.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

You seem to reason from principles and want the argument to hold up. That means the promise should be examined, not explained away. It binds you, but you still need to ask what it commits you to. The duties here conflict: fidelity to your father, provision for your children, honesty and fairness to employees, and the town's stake. Settling this takes adjudication among principles, not a simple ranking of principles over outcomes. Your balanced position on individual versus collective concerns also means neither the family's interests nor the town's should automatically win.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Sunk-cost and legacy pressure.** Decades of family history can make a losing business feel like a verdict on your worth. Ask whether you would keep it open if you had just bought it today.
- **Optimistic numbers.** Losses often look smaller from inside than they are. Use actual cash flow, not hopes about a turnaround.
- **Unknown facts.** How fast the losses grow, what the debts actually are, and whether your father is living (and what he meant) are pivotal. Your judgment should change if these come out differently than you expect.
- **Middle options are uncertain.** A buyer who keeps the store, an employee buyout, a sale-leaseback, or negotiated severance may not be available. Do not build your decision on them until someone has committed in writing.
- **Employees' interests in a sale.** A buyer may not retain staff. Their loss is real whichever path you choose, and they may deserve early, honest notice regardless.
- **Grief and guilt.** Guilt over disappointing your father, or over the employees, can push you toward a choice that feels like penance rather than judgment. Be honest with yourself about which is happening.
