# Advice on the layoff decision

## 1. Core advice

Treat this as three separate questions rather than one: what the policy requires, what the metrics actually show, and what you are willing to be accountable for. Each can be answered on its own, and the dilemma tends to feel more intractable when they are fused.

**What the policy requires.** It asks for a performance-only decision. That is not a cold rule by default. It promises that the other six people are judged by a consistent standard and not by whom you happen to feel closest to. Departing from it commits you to a different claim: that the policy failed to anticipate something morally relevant here, and that you can say what that is.

**What the metrics show.** Before deciding between policy and discretion, test whether the numbers are sound. A person who joined a year ago may still be in ramp-up, and a score may reflect role changes, assignment mix, or a narrow review window. If the measure is shaky for either person, that is a performance-based reason to ask HR for a review, and it keeps you inside the policy.

**What you can own.** Each path carries a different commitment:

- *Following the policy strictly* means accepting that a fair process can still produce a painful result, and that you take responsibility for applying it without hiding behind it.
- *Weighing loyalty, need, and retention* means stating those reasons openly, to HR, to the affected people, and to the team. Need and loyalty cannot be quietly decisive, and pension proximity and parental status may be legally protected factors in your jurisdiction, so reliance on them can create real exposure.

Retention is the most defensible of the discretionary factors, because it concerns the team's future capacity and not anyone's personal circumstances. But it is only a reason if it is verified. Intent to leave "within a year" is not a resignation, and cutting the weakest two could leave a team weaker than the numbers suggest if the strongest two do depart. Treat it as an open question to investigate, not as a settled fact.

Also widen the option set beyond a strict two-of-eight choice: voluntary departures or early retirement, reduced hours, internal transfers, enhanced severance or pension bridging, a delayed timeline, or a conversation with HR about whether the target can be met differently.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans toward outcomes, toward the individual over the group, and toward intuition over explicit reasoning. That is a real strength here: it keeps the single father and your long-time colleague visible as persons and not as rows in a spreadsheet. It would be a mistake to suppress that.

The same tendencies carry a specific risk. An intuition that favors people you know well can track something morally real, or it can track familiarity. And a focus on the individuals in front of you can crowd out the six others, who also have a claim to a fair and stable process. The discipline that fits you is not to override your felt sense but to put it into words and test it: *what exactly is the reason, and would it survive being said aloud?*

The existentialist echo in your profile is only a loose descriptor, but one point in it is useful: no rule fully carries the weight of the decision for you. Whichever way you go, you own it.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Rationalizing a predetermined answer.** Both policy and compassion can be used as cover. If you notice you already know whom you want to keep, ask whether you are reasoning toward it.
- **Hidden reasons.** A quiet deviation from policy may spare individuals but erodes trust if discovered, and may expose you and them to scrutiny. An openly stated exception is harder but more defensible.
- **Mixing backward- and forward-looking logic.** The policy judges past performance, while retention is a prediction about the future. Blending them without saying so makes the decision incoherent.
- **Reducing people to their vulnerabilities.** Both individuals are employees whose work has been assessed. Their hardship matters, but they are not only their hardship.
- **Treating the strongest two's plans as decisive.** Verify what was said, how firm it is, and the timing before it influences anything.
- **Legal exposure.** Check the policy and local employment law before any factor other than performance enters the decision.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a one-page private memo with two columns, "Follow policy as written" and "Depart from policy," and under each, state the reason you would give in plain words to (a) each of the two affected people and (b) the remaining six colleagues. If a reason only works when left unsaid, mark it as a warning sign.

Then request a short, factual conversation with HR with three questions: whether the performance data and review window can be validated for these two people, what legal constraints apply to factors beyond performance, and whether alternatives such as voluntary exits, transfers, or enhanced severance are available.
