## Core advice

Continued silence is very hard to justify on welfare grounds. Exposure is ongoing, and the harm may compound while the evidence on causation is still being gathered. The reasonable course is to move toward disclosure, but the manner and sequence matter. How she proceeds can affect how much harm follows, to whom, and whether anyone can be held to account.

A workable sequence might look like this:

- **Document carefully.** Record what she knows, how she knows it, and what she is inferring. Keep observation separate from interpretation.
- **Get legal advice before acting.** A whistleblower attorney can explain what protections apply in her jurisdiction and what risks come with taking or copying company records.
- **Seek independent technical review.** An outside expert, not someone tied to the company or the town's economy, can assess the discharge data and the health evidence.
- **Consult the regulator confidentially, once she is prepared.** This may bring in remediation, enforcement, or a health investigation, rather than an immediate shutdown.
- **Push for remediation and a transition plan** alongside any enforcement, so that the response reduces exposure without causing sudden collapse.

Some of these steps may be unavailable or insufficient. If the company has concealed the discharges deliberately, internal escalation may be futile or dangerous. Intermediate routes are not guaranteed to work or to be costless, and she should not assume they will dissolve the dilemma.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

You weigh outcomes heavily but not exclusively, and you think in terms of shared welfare. Taking those seriously leads to an uncomfortable comparison.

**The health side** involves probable but unproven harm. The probability is uncertain, but the severity may be high, the harm may be irreversible for some residents, and it falls on people who did not consent to the risk. Each year of delay adds exposure.

**The economic side** involves harm that is more predictable in kind but uncertain in degree. Fines and closure are likely, though regulators often favor remediation. Even so, the loss of nearly half the working population's income would be severe and fall on the same families who are exposed to the contamination.

Your collectivist orientation cuts in more than one direction. "The community" is not one group. Residents with illnesses, downstream communities, future residents, and workers with families all belong to the collective, and their interests conflict. Those exposed to contamination are part of the collective too, not an external cost to be set aside.

Your reason-led orientation also means the epistemic situation matters. The lack of scientific establishment weakens the case for acting, but it also weakens any reassurance that the pollution is harmless. Uncertainty cuts both ways, and so does the cost of delay.

Rules and duties still count. Honesty, the engineering obligation to public safety, and fairness to people exposed without consent are not mere heuristics to discard. They should inform the weighing rather than be overridden by it.

## What to watch for

- **Conflict of interest.** Her family's livelihood is a legitimate interest, and it is also a bias. Treat it as something to manage, not as a reason to feel guilty. Test her reasoning by asking whether she would reach the same conclusion if her family had no connection to the factory. If not, seek review from people who have no stake in the outcome.
- **Overstating the health link.** Do not treat the illnesses as established as caused by the discharges. Equally, do not dismiss the possibility. Keep the uncertainty honest in both directions.
- **Overstating economic certainty.** Closure is probable but not inevitable. Do not assume the worst outcome, but do not minimize it either.
- **Escalating too fast or too slow.** Acting rashly may expose her to retaliation and weaken the case. Waiting indefinitely prolongs the exposure and deepens her own complicity over time.
- **Moral certainty that bypasses analysis.** Be wary of arguments, from either side, that claim the answer is obvious.

## The moral remainder

Even the best available course will likely leave real harm behind. Some residents may already be ill. Some workers may lose their jobs, even if the closure is avoided and remediation succeeds. Disclosure may be necessary and still leave something owed to those who were harmed and to those who will bear the cost of the response. Acknowledging that remainder is part of acting well, not evidence that the decision was wrong.

To test your own view, ask what evidence would change it. If strong independent data showed no link between the discharges and the illnesses, would your lean change? If regulators credibly committed to remediation without closure, would the balance shift? Identifying those conditions in advance will make your reasoning more reliable.
