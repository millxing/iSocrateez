## 1. Core advice

Don't start by asking which option is better in the abstract. Ask which option you can defend to each group of residents, given what you can honestly promise. Three pressures pull against each other:

- **Consent** matters because people have a strong claim to shape their own homes and lives. But the consent of residents facing unsellable houses and limited means is thinner than it looks. And future residents, including children not yet born, cannot consent at all.
- **Future risk** matters because you are a steward for people who will inherit the consequences of today's decision. But projected risk is uncertain, and uncertainty is a reason to investigate carefully, not a license to accept harm.
- **Obligations to those who cannot leave** are probably the most pressing. They have the least ability to protect themselves and the most to lose if the chosen option fails them.

Each option fails a different group. The barrier may leave the poorest and youngest exposed once it is overwhelmed, perhaps within your planning horizon. Relocation may uproot older residents and the community's bonds, and to them it may feel like abandonment, even if it is meant as protection.

So the question is less "barrier or relocation" than "which failure would be harder to justify, and to whom?" That depends on facts you don't yet have. Under these conditions, a balanced, moderately collective-minded judgment would lean in different directions:

- **Toward relocation**, designed as voluntary-first and well funded, if the barrier is likely to fail within the horizon and many vulnerable households have no realistic way out.
- **Toward the barrier**, paired with a staged and better-funded transition plan, if storm risk is moderate and the community can plan a managed move later without stranding anyone.

Whichever you choose, some loss is unavoidable. Naming that openly is part of acting responsibly.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile weighs outcomes and principles about equally, with a moderate lean toward the common good and a balance between systematic analysis and moral perception. That suggests:

- **Analysis** matters: barrier lifespan, storm trends, compensation terms, and cost are not optional details. A decision made without them is careless.
- **Perception** also matters: the grief of leaving ancestral ground is real and should not be reduced to a line item, and the image of someone stranded in a house nobody will buy is a warning that the numbers alone can't supply.
- **The collective** deserves weight, since a community that is repeatedly endangered or financially trapped cannot flourish. But the collective is made of individuals, and the people least able to speak for themselves should not disappear into the aggregate.

This framing lets you act as a council member, who must be accountable to the whole town, rather than as a detached calculator or a judge applying a single rule.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Decisive preference for staying.** Older residents' wishes are a weighty claim, but not an absolute one, especially where others bear the risk of the barrier failing.
- **Assuming what the poorest residents want.** Find out directly. Their circumstances and preferences should be sought through trusted local organizations, not inferred from who attends meetings.
- **False precision.** Storm projections and barrier performance are uncertain. Name the uncertainty and say which facts would change your view.
- **Relocation framed as logistics.** Moving people is a social and emotional event. Consider whether neighbors can move together, whether return visits or memorials are possible, and how the community's identity can survive.
- **Sunk-cost drift.** A barrier can delay a harder choice while property values fall and risk grows. Ask whether the barrier is buying time for a real plan or simply postponing one.
- **Dishonest reassurance.** Tell residents plainly what each option costs and whom it risks. A council that hides the tradeoff loses legitimacy, whatever it decides.
- **Unfunded promises.** Any commitment that nobody will be left with worthless property needs a specific funding mechanism, such as buyouts at pre-risk value or insurance support, or it is only words.

Today, send a written request to planning staff for three things:

1. The barrier's rated design life and the storm return period it is built to withstand, with the degradation assumptions behind them.
2. The relocation program's draft terms: per-household compensation, buyout valuation method, and whether neighbors can be moved together.
