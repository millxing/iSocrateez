## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a choice between two forms of care: protecting people where they are, or protecting them by changing where they live. Neither is plainly right, and the question as posed forces a choice. Within it, I'd lean toward whichever option you can make *fair to the people with the fewest options*, and be honest that this may not be the same as the option most people prefer today.

A few commitments should guide the decision:

- **Test the risk assumptions before choosing.** "Another decade" and "future storms could overwhelm it" are unquantified. Ask for the best available engineering and climate assessments, including what failure would look like (gradual overtopping or sudden breach) and who would be hurt first.
- **Examine the terms, not just the labels.** A "voluntary" relocation program can strand those without means if buyouts are priced below replacement cost, and can become coercive in effect if insurance or services withdraw. A barrier can quietly entrench exposure and depress property values. Judge each option by its realistic terms.
- **Hear the least mobile directly.** Consult residents who cannot afford to leave, in settings where they can speak candidly, rather than relying on majority sentiment or the loudest voices.
- **Look for conditional design within the constraint.** If you choose one path, build in what you can from the other: relocation with strong right-of-return or community-keeping provisions, or a barrier with defined review triggers and a funded exit option if conditions worsen.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile weighs consequences without making them the only test, and leans toward collective concerns. That points to a practical-wisdom question: what would a fair, caring, and responsible steward of this community do with what is known?

- **Consequences matter, but not as a bare cost calculation.** Expected harm from flooding counts, yet so does the loss of place, memory, and community, which money cannot fully repair.
- **Older residents' wish to stay is a real good**, not nostalgia. Younger families' fear of being trapped is a legitimate concern about security and fairness, not mere self-interest. Both are sincere and bear different costs.
- **The community as a whole includes people who are hard to hear.** Your collective orientation is best honored by attending to the distribution of risk and loss, not just the aggregate.
- **Character and trust are at stake.** How the council decides, with candor and accountability, shapes whether residents can trust it in the next crisis.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Consent that isn't informed or free.** Support for staying may reflect lack of alternatives; support for leaving may reflect fear of unsellable homes. Consent depends on information, affordability, and trust.
- **Hidden costs:** buyout prices, insurance availability, maintenance funding for a barrier, and who pays if it fails.
- **Overconfidence in either forecast.** Don't assume the barrier's protection is reliable, or that relocation will be fully funded, well-run, or truly voluntary.
- **Stranding.** Under both options, residents without means may end up most exposed: by losing housing and networks, or by living with lasting risk.
- **Irreversibility.** Relocation is harder to undo; barrier failure can be sudden and catastrophic. Weigh which error you could better recover from.
- **Moral residue.** Whichever you choose will impose real losses. Pretending otherwise would be a failure of honesty toward those who bear them.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Draft a one-page memo to your fellow council members listing the specific facts you need before any vote: the engineering assessment of the barrier (design life, failure mode), the proposed buyout and insurance terms, and a count of households that lack the means to move or to absorb a loss. Request these from staff, and ask to hold a small, separate listening session with the least-mobile residents before the decision. Write down, in a few sentences, the reasoning you'd be willing to defend publicly, and the losses you would acknowledge, so you can test your choice against it as information arrives.
