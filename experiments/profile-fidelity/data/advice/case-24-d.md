# Advice on the Lifeboat Dilemma

## Moral tensions

Several tensions pull against each other here, and none dissolves cleanly:

- **Aggregate survival vs. the wrong of choosing a victim.** Five living instead of zero is an overwhelming outcome consideration, yet it requires doing something coercive to a specific person who has done nothing to deserve it.
- **Collective welfare vs. the individual's claim.** The group's interest is real, but the one who goes overboard bears the entire cost, with no consent.
- **Vulnerability vs. impartiality.** The pregnant woman and the boy have strong claims on care, but ranking people by perceived worth risks treating others as expendable. The physician's usefulness after rescue is similarly a dangerous basis for judgment.
- **Intuition vs. its unreliability under terror.** Your gut says both "we cannot let everyone die" and "we cannot throw someone into freezing water." Both are morally serious, and panic distorts each.
- **Acting vs. refraining.** Refusing to choose is also a choice, and its consequences fall on everyone.

## 1. Core advice

Do not decide by unilateral force or by ranking people's worth. Treat the problem as a shared emergency that the group must carry together, in this order:

1. **Exhaust alternatives first:** bail continuously, shed all non-human weight, redistribute positions, and test whether the boat is actually unsafe before concluding someone must go.
2. **Seek a voluntary solution.** Name the situation aloud, acknowledge the fear, and give people time to speak. Volunteers sometimes emerge when the burden is shared openly rather than left as a silent threat.
3. **If no one volunteers and sinking is truly imminent, use a procedure that treats everyone as equal,** such as a lottery, rather than a judgment of who matters more. Because you cannot reliably estimate survival odds, chance is the fairest remaining tool. Vulnerable people may reasonably be excused, but only if the group agrees openly and the remaining pool is still genuine.
4. **Accept that this choice carries lasting moral weight.** If it comes to a lottery, the outcome is tragic rather than clean, and the group should carry it together, including its grief.

On balance, refusing to act while the boat sinks is hard to defend if the danger is real, since it sacrifices everyone, including those most dependent on others. But force is justified only after the gentler options have honestly failed.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

- **Moderately toward outcomes:** Five lives against none carries real weight, so passive refusal isn't a safe harbor.
- **Strongly collective:** Framing the problem as the group's shared burden, solved jointly and with shared accountability, matches your leaning.
- **Strongly intuitive, with care ethics nearby:** Your sense that this should be handled through attention, relationship, and voice rather than cold calculation shapes the advice. Speaking openly, noticing the fear, and protecting the vulnerable reflect Gilligan's emphasis on responsiveness to concrete people. A lottery is the one place where I lean on formal fairness, because care alone cannot pick among equals when no one consents.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Panic masquerading as intuition.** Gut reactions under terror often track fear or prejudice (age, status, gender) rather than moral insight. Check whether your impulse would survive being said aloud to everyone.
- **Quiet coercion by the strong.** "Voluntary" sacrifice can be pressured, especially on the elderly or the isolated. Real voluntariness means no one is shamed into it.
- **Premature certainty that someone must go.** Fear can inflate the danger. Verify before sacrificing.
- **Usefulness reasoning.** Valuing the physician's skills or the boy's years can slide into ranking human worth. Vulnerability justifies care, not disposability of others.
- **Moral residue.** Even a fair procedure leaves guilt and grief. Don't treat fairness as erasing the harm.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write down, in a few sentences, which of these you would be least willing to compromise on: a fair procedure, protection of the most vulnerable, or minimizing total deaths. Then identify one real situation in your life where a group faces a shared burden, and notice whether you tend to default to silence, to unilateral decision, or to open collective deliberation. Practice naming the burden aloud to the people it affects.
