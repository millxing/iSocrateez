# Advice

## 1. Core advice

Treat this as a decision you author, not a choice between two buttons. "Publish everything" and "suppress everything" are the endpoints of a wider range, and the strongest options probably lie between them.

**The case for sharing** rests on large, widely distributed benefits: cheap clean energy could reduce poverty, pollution, and climate harm for billions. Open work also allows verification and correction, and suppression may not hold. Others may reach the same result, and a secret held by one person or a small group concentrates power in whoever holds it.

**The case for restraint** rests on irreversibility and severity. Publication cannot be recalled, and a catastrophic downside may outweigh even very large benefits if its probability is not negligible. Restraint also acknowledges that she may not be the right sole decider, but also that waiting for no one is itself a unilateral act.

**Separate the two pathways.** The energy application and the weapons pathway are not the same knowledge. Ask whether they can be disclosed differently: for example, staged release, delayed publication, or sharing the beneficial application while withholding the specifics that most enable misuse. Each of these has its own risks, including leakage, a false sense of security, and the difficulty of cleanly dividing dual-use knowledge. But they deserve real examination before she accepts a binary.

**Seek counsel without outsourcing the decision.** Confidential consultation with people in security, governance, and ethics, along with trusted scientific peers, can improve her estimates and surface options she hasn't seen. The final judgment remains hers, and so does responsibility for how it is carried out.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans toward outcomes, toward individual judgment, and toward intuition (the sign convention in the scores is ambiguous, so this follows the stated labels).

- **Outcomes:** You'll want to weigh realistic consequences of each path, including the consequences of the discovery being made by others anyway. This keeps the focus on what actually happens rather than on rule-following alone.
- **Individual judgment:** You have standing to make a personal moral choice and don't need a collective procedure to legitimize it. Consulting others is then a way of informing your judgment, not replacing it.
- **Intuition:** Your sense of the stakes is real information. But intuition is strongest when tested. Ask whether your alarm, or your enthusiasm, tracks evidence or reflects fear, pride in the discovery, or a wish to be the hero or the guardian.

The existentialist emphasis on freedom and responsibility under ambiguity is a useful nearby lens: you cannot offload this choice onto a rule or onto probabilities you can't actually know, and you are answerable for it either way.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Treating gut feeling as a verdict.** Write down what you *know*, what you *estimate*, and what you *fear or hope*. Check whether your intuition survives that separation.
- **False binaries.** "Share or suppress" can hide middle options that are harder but better.
- **Overconfidence about secrecy.** Suppression may feel safe while only delaying an independent discovery, possibly by someone with less caution.
- **Overconfidence about control.** Staged or partial release may fail to contain what it is meant to contain.
- **Motivated reasoning.** Career, recognition, and the wish to be free of the burden can all quietly shape the analysis, in either direction.
- **Isolation.** Carrying this alone distorts judgment. The weight is real, and it's reasonable to want company in it, even if the decision stays yours.
- **Unrecoverable moves.** Prefer steps that preserve future options (secure documentation, delayed rather than abandoned publication) over those that close them.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a one-page private memo with three columns: **what I know**, **what I'm estimating (with rough confidence)**, and **what I fear or hope**. Then list three options other than "publish now" or "suppress permanently," and for each note its main risk. Finally, identify one or two people with relevant security or ethics expertise whom you could approach in confidence, and decide what minimal, non-technical framing you would use for a first conversation. Do not circulate technical details until you have decided how you will handle disclosure.
