# Core advice

On the principles you seem to hold, I think the stronger duty favors telling your brother, though the promise is real and breaking it costs something. This lean depends on a few premises I'll name below.

**The case for keeping the promise.** Promissory obligations don't lapse because the promisee has died. The practice of promising depends on people keeping their word when no one can check, and a deathbed promise is among the clearest cases. Your mother may also have acted from love, perhaps fear that your brother would feel less belonging. That deserves weight as a reason, though I'm speculating about her motives.

**The case for disclosure.** Your brother is making medical decisions on premises you know to be false. This differs from merely not volunteering information. He is relying, in part, on a family history you could correct, so your silence now helps sustain a deception that bears on his body and his choices. A promise also can't rightly commit you to wronging a third party. And a rule like "keep promises that require letting someone be seriously misled about their own health" is hard to will as universal.

**Did the promise have implicit limits?** Your mother plausibly meant to protect your brother from a painful revelation, not to endanger his health. Would she have wanted it kept in these circumstances? That is interpretive and can't be settled, but it makes the promise less clearly applicable to this situation than its words suggest. It does not dissolve the promise.

**Intermediate paths.** None satisfies both duties cleanly:
- *Telling him only that the family history may not apply to him*: this is alarming and evasive, and he will ask why.
- *Nudging him toward genetic testing*: it may reach the truth without breaking the letter of the promise, but it conceals your reason and arguably violates its spirit.
- *Telling his doctor*: this bypasses his agency over his own information.

These may sometimes serve as steps within honest disclosure, but not as substitutes for it.

# Why this fits your ethical profile

You tend to reason from duties rather than outcomes, and to prioritize the individual and reason over felt pull. So this is not a harm tally. It is a conflict between two principled duties: fidelity to a promise, and honesty and respect for your brother as someone entitled to accurate premises for his own decisions. The individual orientation points at both: your own promissory integrity and his claim to the truth about his identity and body. The emphasis on reason suggests examining the deathbed emotion rather than simply obeying or dismissing it. When duties conflict, the one protecting a person's standing as a rational agent, against a deception still actively operating, seems to carry more weight than a promise whose purpose was protective.

# What to watch for

- **Dissolving the residue.** Breaking the promise is a real wrong, not just a regret. Acknowledge it rather than rationalizing it away.
- **Overstating certainty.** The condition "may" be hereditary, and you don't know how much adoptive status matters medically. A doctor or genetic counselor could clarify that, though not by going around your brother.
- **Narrowing the disclosure as evasion.** Sharing only the medical fact while withholding the rest may feel safer but can itself become a half-truth.
- **Grief.** You are acting under strain, and the timing and manner of telling matter morally as well as practically.
- **The premises this lean rests on:** that the medical relevance is real, that no other honest route will surface the truth soon, and that your mother would not have wanted the promise kept at this cost. If any of these changes, the balance could shift.
