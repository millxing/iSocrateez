# Core advice

Treat this as a choice among several ways of acting, not just report, confront, or stay out. A proportionate path would be:

1. **Talk to him first, and lead with care rather than accusation.** Tell him what you know, that you aren't there to expose him, and that you're worried about both his safety and his ability to keep seeing his wife. Ask what the vision test actually found and whether he has looked into options.
2. **Bring alternatives, not just concern.** Volunteer driver programs, transport offered by the care home or local senior services, or your own offer to drive him some weeks can make "stop driving" a workable request instead of a cruel one.
3. **Encourage a formal reassessment or a conversation with his doctor.** Many places have routes to retest or to restore driving with corrective measures. Whether that applies to him depends on what the test found and on local rules, which vary.
4. **Keep reporting as a later option, not a first move.** If the impairment appears serious and he refuses to change anything, reporting becomes more defensible, and arguably more responsible. Check how your jurisdiction handles reports so you know what you'd be setting in motion.

Staying out is not a neutral default now that you know. It is a choice with consequences, though a defensible one if you judge the risk low and the cost of intervention high.

# Why this fits your ethical profile

You appear to weigh concrete outcomes and to give real weight to individual freedom and dignity, while staying open to wider concern for others. A direct, caring conversation honors his standing as an adult and may yield better results for him, his wife, and other road users than a report that cuts off his only link to her. It also lets you use both your reasoning (what is the actual risk, what are the alternatives?) and your intuition (your unease, your sympathy) rather than suppressing either.

There is also a question of ownership. Nobody has appointed you his guardian, but you now hold knowledge that others lack. Whatever you do, including nothing, is something you will have chosen. No option here is free of cost, and reasonable people could land in different places.

# What to watch for

- **The unknown severity.** A failed vision test could mean a marginal miss or a serious impairment. Your whole calculus shifts depending on which, so try to learn more before deciding.
- **Shame and defensiveness.** He may feel exposed. Keep the conversation private, unhurried, and free of ultimatums.
- **Rationalizing in either direction.** "He's never had an accident" is weak evidence about future risk, and "it's the law" doesn't settle what a proportionate response is. Check whether you're reaching for either because it spares you discomfort.
- **Signs the risk is growing.** Near-misses, damage to his car, getting lost, or him declining all help would be reasons to escalate.
- **The cost of reporting.** It could end his visits to his wife and deepen his isolation. If you do report, consider whether you can pair it with practical help so the consequences don't fall on him alone.
- **Your own limits.** You can offer help without becoming his permanent chauffeur. Be honest with yourself about what you can sustain.
