# Advice

## 1. Core advice

Two legitimate principles collide here. One is the duty to safeguard the integrity of dispensing: controlled inventory, safe practice, the trust that lets a pharmacy function for everyone. The other is the duty not to sacrifice a vulnerable person to a rule when you have the power to avoid it. Neither is a mere excuse for the other, and "report or look away" is too thin a framing.

Before leaning either way, take stock of what you don't know. The mother's account is unverified, though not implausible. You don't know the child's actual medical situation, whether this is a one-time act, or what your own legal and employer obligations are. Some jurisdictions and workplaces require inventory reconciliation or incident reporting regardless of your preference.

A tentative lean: take a path of **transparent, accountable intervention** rather than silent concealment. In practice, that could mean:

- Contacting the prescriber to confirm the situation and explore alternatives.
- Pursuing manufacturer assistance programs, charitable funds, or an insurance appeal.
- Speaking with a supervisor or pharmacist-in-charge, framing the matter as a problem to solve rather than only a crime to process.
- Covering the cost yourself, if feasible and permitted.

None of these is frictionless. Each costs you something, and none guarantees the child's care continues. This lean still compromises either strict rule-adherence or your full personal discretion.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

You tend to act from principle, to center the particular person over aggregates, and to trust moral intuition. Together this suggests a conscience-driven view in which the question is *which* principle you hold most deeply, and the answer isn't automatically "the rule."

- **Your pull toward the mother and child is evidence, not bias.** It tracks something real: a child with the strongest claim to protection, and a parent who acted out of desperation. But treat it as evidence to examine, not a verdict. Ask what it is responding to, and whether it survives the unknowns above.
- **Principle can point in either direction.** Fidelity to your professional commitments is a principle. So is refusing to abandon a person in acute need. Choosing between them is a real choice that you own.
- **Owning the decision matters.** Quietly letting her leave can look like mercy while functioning as a way to avoid responsibility. Reporting can likewise be a way to hide behind the rule. Either can be self-deception if it spares you from standing behind what you did. Transparent intervention keeps the decision visible and accountable.

## 3. What to watch for

- **The universalizability test.** Would you endorse the policy your choice implies? If you'd be comfortable with "pharmacists should quietly overlook theft when the story is sympathetic," ask whether you'd accept it when the story is false, or when you can't tell. Your intuition may resist reducing this case to a policy, but the test shows what you're committing to beyond this one encounter.
- **Intuition that can be exploited or mistaken.** Compassion is a reliable signal about stakes, but not about facts. Verification, done without cynicism or accusation, protects both the mother and the integrity of your judgment.
- **Risks to you and others.** Concealment, personal payment, or workaround arrangements can carry legal, licensing, and employment consequences, and may affect coworkers. Know your mandatory reporting and reconciliation obligations before acting.
- **The child as a person.** The child isn't a variable in the mother's fate. The child has independent claims, and the most exposed position if prosecution disrupts care. Any path should be tested against what actually keeps the child treated, not just what feels merciful.
- **Escalation as evasion.** Handing the matter to a supervisor or program can be a genuine solution or a way of offloading the decision. Stay involved in the outcome.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Do two things, in this order:

1. **Clarify your obligations.** Quietly check your pharmacy's policy and your jurisdiction's rules on theft, incident reporting, and inventory discrepancies, or consult your pharmacist-in-charge or professional board's guidance line in general terms.
2. **Contact the prescriber's office** to confirm the treatment need and ask about manufacturer assistance, patient-support programs, or an expedited insurance appeal. This addresses the child's continued care and gives you facts about the story.

Then write down, for yourself, which principle you're prioritizing and what you're giving up by doing so.

**The residual cost remains whichever way you go.** The mother stays exposed to legal risk, you may have breached your role or bent it, and a precedent is set that you'll have to be able to defend. Carrying that tension honestly is part of making the choice your own.
