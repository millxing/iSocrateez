## Core advice

Tell her what you honestly think, but separate the three questions she is bundling together: whether the book is good, whether it will sell, and whether she should quit her job now. You can be candid about the first and the second and still be careful about the third, where the stakes are highest and your judgment is least decisive.

Be specific rather than global. "It's poor" gives her a verdict to absorb. "The middle third loses its tension because the protagonist stops making choices, and the dialogue in chapter six repeats what the reader already understands" gives her something to work with. Name what works, too, if anything does. Frame your view as your reading, not as a fact about the world. Publishing outcomes depend on agents, timing, and luck, and you can say so without retreating from your assessment.

On the quitting question, you can be more direct. Encourage her to keep her job while she submits, to get a second or third reader (ideally an editor or someone who reads widely in her genre), and to treat this draft as part of a longer apprenticeship. This doesn't tell her not to write. It asks her not to bet her savings on a single unseen outcome.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

Her request is for judgment. If you answer with empty praise, you respond to a different question, "Do you still support me?", and leave unanswered the one she asked, "Is this good enough to stake my life on?" Flattery here also treats her as someone to be managed rather than as an adult making a real decision. That is a quiet kind of bad faith, toward her and toward the relationship.

Your discomfort at the thought of faking enthusiasm is worth taking seriously. It is a signal about what the friendship requires, not just a feeling to argue away.

At the same time, your judgment could be wrong, and she is entitled to make her own choices with the information you give her. Honesty that respects her agency means offering your view and leaving the decision with her.

## What to watch for

- **Overreach.** Your verdict on the book is not a verdict on her life. Avoid telling her not to write or implying she has no talent.
- **Bluntness passing for honesty.** A harsh delivery can satisfy your sense of candor while mainly serving your own relief. Ask whether your framing would help her see the book more clearly, or only let you feel you said it.
- **Fragility.** This argues for care in timing, setting, and tone, and for staying present afterward. It does not argue for concealment. If she gets rejection letters later and learns you saw the problems and said nothing, the damage to trust could be greater than an honest disappointment now.
- **Your own uncertainty.** Consider getting another reader's view before you deliver a strong verdict, or present yours explicitly as one reader's opinion.
- **Honesty may still hurt.** No phrasing guarantees that she won't be wounded or that the friendship won't be strained for a while.

## A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Before you share your opinion, ask her a question: "What do you want from this read? Are you looking for a proofreader's eye, a reader's reaction, or a judgment on whether to send it out now?" Her answer will show you whether she is ready for candor and what she needs from you. If she says she wants your honest view, give it, focusing on two or three specific strengths and two or three specific problems, and separate the book from the decision to quit. If she says she mainly wants reassurance, tell her that you want to be useful to her, and that you would rather give her your real reaction than a reaction she can't trust.
