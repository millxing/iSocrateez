## 1. Core advice

The tensions here do not reduce to one another, so name them before choosing:

- **Consent vs. safety.** Older residents' present wishes are morally weighty, yet the barrier's protection is temporary and the risk falls on those who live longest with the consequences.
- **Community continuity vs. long-term survival.** The town is a good in itself (shared memory, ancestral ties, mutual care), and it may not survive the very thing meant to save it.
- **Equal respect vs. unequal exposure.** Those who can afford to leave have options; those who cannot are the ones a failed barrier harms most.
- **Honesty vs. hope.** Funding a barrier can feel like care while deferring a harder decision to a future council and to the least mobile residents.

My advice is to lean toward the relocation program, but only in a form that treats it as voluntary, staged, and community-preserving rather than a one-time scattering. That means priority for residents who cannot afford to leave, buyout terms that make younger families whole rather than stranding them, and options for collective resettlement so neighbors can move together. Residents who wish to stay should not be forced out, but the town should be candid that public protection will not be indefinite. If you judge that a relocation program cannot be designed with these protections, the barrier becomes defensible only as an explicit bridge, paired with a funded commitment to revisit relocation within the decade.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile balances outcomes and principles, leans moderately toward the collective, and weighs reason and intuition evenly. Read through Aristotle, the question is what a practically wise steward of a shared place would do.

- **Outcomes and principles:** Long-term safety counts heavily, but so do consent and fairness, which is why the voluntary, protective design matters as much as the choice itself.
- **Collective lean:** The town's survival as a community, not just individual houses, is the object of concern. Collective resettlement honors that.
- **Reason and intuition:** The pull toward ancestral homes is not sentiment to be overridden; it signals a real good. Reasoned risk assessment tells you the good cannot be protected by denial.
- **Virtue lens:** Courage means telling people a hard truth early. Justice means centering those with the fewest exits. Compassion means easing the loss rather than denying it.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Paternalism:** Don't treat older residents' attachment as an error to be corrected. Their consent should shape the pace and terms.
- **The comfort of the barrier:** It is the option that avoids immediate conflict, which is a reason to scrutinize your preference for it.
- **Relocation in name only:** If funding covers moving costs but not equivalent housing, jobs, and social ties, the program just disperses vulnerability.
- **Uncertain risk estimates:** Ask whether the "decade" figure is a best case, and what the failure scenario looks like for people who cannot evacuate quickly.
- **Moral residue:** Even the best choice involves loss. Acknowledge it publicly rather than claiming a clean resolution.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Request from staff or an independent engineer a short written brief with two items: the realistic failure probability and consequences of the barrier over 10 to 20 years, and a count of households that lack the means to relocate without assistance. Then schedule a listening session specifically with those households and with older residents, so the design of whichever option you pursue starts from the people with the most at stake.
