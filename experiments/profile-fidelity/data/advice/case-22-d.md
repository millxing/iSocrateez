## 1. Core advice

**Don't treat this as a binary between exposing the informant and staying silent.** The detective's real task is to pursue every route that corrects the injustice without making the informant's identity the price of admission. In practice, that means working to *re-derive* the exculpatory evidence from sources that don't trace back to the informant: re-testing physical evidence, locating independent witnesses, re-examining the original investigation's weak points, or bringing the matter confidentially to a conviction integrity unit, a prosecutor, or a judge for in camera review.

Several tensions should stay visible while doing this:

- **Two innocents with claims against you.** The prisoner has a claim not to be punished for what he did not do. The informant and their family have a claim not to be exposed to lethal risk, one they accepted only on terms the detective would now be breaking. Neither claim is plainly stronger, and the family members never consented to any risk.
- **Certain harm versus probable harm.** The prisoner's injustice is ongoing and certain. The informant's danger is "almost certain" if exposed in open court, but the degree depends on the disclosure route, which is why route matters so much.
- **Promise versus rescue.** The detective made an implicit or explicit promise of protection. Breaking it for a good cause still damages the trust that informant systems, and future informants, depend on.
- **Institutional role versus personal conscience.** The detective is bound by duties of confidentiality and disclosure (including obligations to turn over exculpatory material), and those duties can pull in opposite directions.

If every alternative fails, I would not say the detective may simply bury the evidence. But the decision to expose should belong, as far as possible, to the informant, who can be offered relocation, protection, and the chance to consent or refuse. Overriding that choice unilaterally should be a last resort.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile is balanced between outcomes and principles, strongly individual-focused, and strongly reason-driven, and Scanlonian contractualism sits close to it. Ask which principle no one affected could reasonably reject. A rule like "Police may sacrifice a confidential informant's safety whenever it serves a conviction review" is one informants could reasonably reject. So is "Police may leave a person they know to be innocent in prison to protect an operation." The principle that survives scrutiny is something like: *exhaust the means that respect both parties' claims, and when a real conflict remains, give weight to the party facing the more severe, less avoidable burden, with the consent of the one who would bear the risk wherever possible.*

This respects your individual focus: both the prisoner and the informant are treated as persons with separate claims rather than as inputs to an aggregate. It also honors your preference for reasoned justification, since the aim is a principle the detective could defend to each of them face to face.

## 3. What to watch for

- **False dichotomy as an excuse.** "It's impossible without exposure" can become a convenient way to avoid the harder work of finding alternatives, or to avoid the career and legal risks of acting.
- **Overestimating or underestimating the danger.** Fear can inflate it; institutional comfort can minimize it. Get an independent threat assessment rather than relying on instinct.
- **Reliability of the evidence.** "Compelling" should be tested. An informant inside a criminal organization may have motives, and acting on flawed evidence could harm everyone.
- **Delay disguised as caution.** Every month is real time lost for the prisoner. Set a deadline for the alternative routes.
- **Unilateral heroics.** Leaking or going public would bypass the safeguards (and the informant's consent) that make the decision legitimate.
- **Legal exposure.** Disclosure duties vary by jurisdiction, so check them before acting.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a private memo (kept off shared systems) with two columns: what the informant's information establishes, and what independent evidence could establish the same facts without it. Then request a confidential consultation with a trusted supervisor, a conviction integrity unit, or legal counsel, framing it as "how can we pursue this exculpatory lead while protecting a source?" Do not reveal the informant's identity in that first conversation unless it is strictly unavoidable.
