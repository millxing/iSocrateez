## Core advice

Decline the condition as stated, and do not accept a secret removal of books. Then actively look for funding and for a donor arrangement that leaves collection decisions entirely with the library. If the donor will not move on that point, accept the service reduction and treat it as a cost borne for a principle, with a plan to mitigate it.

The tensions are real and should stay in view:

- **Access vs. integrity.** The study room serves students with the fewest alternatives. But a library that removes material at a funder's request is no longer the same kind of institution. Its function is to provide access to information on terms the public can trust.
- **Concrete harm vs. diffuse harm.** The loss of the room is visible and falls on identifiable people. The harm of censorship is spread out, partly invisible, and compounding, since a precedent for one donor invites others.
- **Secrecy.** Privacy in the arrangement is what makes it corrosive. Patrons would be relying on a collection they believe is independent, so the deception adds a second wrong beyond the removal itself.
- **The compromise option.** Middle paths (for example, "rebalancing" the collection, or accepting a nominal restriction) feel like a way to avoid a tragic choice, but they can carry the same dependency with less honesty. A compromise is only acceptable if it is a funding structure, not a content concession.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans strongly toward principle, collective good, and reasoned judgment, and the Stoic emphasis on what is within one's control fits this case well. The library cannot control whether funding appears. It can control whether it acts with integrity in how it responds. Reasoning from the library's role, as a trusted public institution serving all current and future patrons, favors the collective interest in an uncorrupted collection over the immediate collective interest in one room. A principle-first stance does not require ignoring the students. It requires that you honor their needs through means that do not compromise the institution on which they also depend.

## What to watch for

- **Rationalizing through outcomes.** "Only a few books" and "five years of access" are tempting arguments. Notice whether the number is doing the moral work, or whether the real issue is who decides what the public can read.
- **Moral self-congratulation.** Refusing is easy for those who do not bear the cost. Students pay for this principle, so the library owes them a serious effort to soften the loss.
- **False binary.** Treating this as a single yes or no can obscure options such as partial funding, shorter hours, partner spaces, or crowd-based support.
- **Disguised conditions.** Watch for softer language ("alignment," "balance," "review") that restores the same control in a more deniable form.
- **Transparency risk.** If the offer becomes public, the donor's behavior is itself relevant information to the community, and your response should be one you can defend openly.

## A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write a brief, candid reply to the donor declining any condition on collection content, while inviting an unrestricted gift or one tied only to the study room's operation. In parallel, contact two or three local institutions (schools, community centers, universities, or churches) to ask whether they could host or co-fund evening study hours if the room closes. Record the donor's offer and your response in a written note for the library board, so the decision is made openly and not by one person alone.
