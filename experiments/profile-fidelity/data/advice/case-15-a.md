# Core advice

Tell her the truth, but don't treat "what I really think" as a single verdict to deliver. Two decisions are tangled together here: whether the book is good, and whether she should quit her job. Pull them apart.

- **Read the book as an honest reader.** Identify specific strengths and specific problems, in concrete terms (voice, structure, pacing, character), not a general "it's not working."
- **Give your assessment of the book without pretending to certainty about the market.** You can say what you found weak. You cannot know whether it will sell, and you should say so.
- **Don't say you believe in it if you don't.** Encouragement is fine where it's true: praise what actually works, and affirm the effort and the commitment. Warmth built on real observations is not deception. Warmth built on a feigned verdict is.
- **Treat quitting as a separate, reversible-in-stages question.** The manuscript can be revised and resubmitted. Spent savings and a left job are much harder to recover. Encourage her to gather more evidence before the irreversible step: other readers, agent responses, a clear financial runway.
- **Leave the decision with her.** Your job is to make sure she chooses with accurate information, not to steer her toward or away from quitting.

If she asks you directly, "Do you think it will sell?", answer plainly and with humility: "I have doubts, and here's why, but I could be wrong, and I'm one reader."

# Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile balances outcomes and principles, so neither can be dropped. The consequences are real (her savings, her job, her fragile state), and so is the principle that she deserves not to be managed with a curated picture. A false endorsement from the person she trusts most would corrupt the very information she is relying on.

The lean toward the individual points you to this particular friend, her circumstances, and her request, rather than a general rule about honesty. That is why timing, tone, and checking what she actually wants matter as much as the content.

The Sartrean and Beauvoirian thread fits too. Both stress that treating someone as free means giving her the truth of her situation so she can own her choice. Shielding her from your opinion, however kindly meant, quietly decides for her. Beauvoir's emphasis on mutual freedom also suggests that honesty offered with care expresses respect rather than threatens the friendship.

Your intuitive unease at cheering on a decision you think is unwise deserves weight. Test it, though, against the possibility that part of you is also dreading conflict.

# What to watch for

- **Your own motives.** Are you inclined toward praise to avoid a hard conversation, or toward criticism to feel vindicated or to protect her from disappointment? Either can masquerade as care.
- **Being wrong.** Literary quality is contested and publishing outcomes are unpredictable. Hold your view firmly enough to share it and loosely enough to revise it.
- **What she's asking for.** "Read it before I submit" might mean "tell me the truth" or "tell me I'm ready." Ask her, rather than guessing.
- **Her fragility.** Choose a calm moment, not one rushed by a deadline. Candor is compatible with gentleness, but if you see signs of serious distress, her wellbeing comes before the feedback.
- **The weight of your words.** Because she values your opinion above anyone's, she may treat it as final. Remind her that it isn't, and that others' readings matter.
- **Overreach.** Don't tell her to quit writing, and don't tell her to quit her job. Offer information, not instructions.
