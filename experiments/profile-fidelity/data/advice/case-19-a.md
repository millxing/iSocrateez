# Advice on dividing the inheritance

## 1. Core advice

**Treat yourself as a steward of your parent's intentions, not an arbiter of who deserves what.** Three steps follow from that.

1. **Establish the note's status first.** Before weighing its content, find out what it is: a legal instrument, a draft, a passing sentiment, or something else. A lawyer or estate professional can tell you whether it has any force and what you are permitted to do as executor. The legal question (what you may do) and the moral question (what is fair) are separate, and the first may narrow the second.

2. **Don't act on the note unilaterally.** It says the caregiver "should receive more" but gives no amount. Acting on it means inventing the figure your parent didn't specify, and you would be putting your own judgment of desert in your parent's place. Ignoring it is also a choice: you would be treating a clear earlier instruction as the whole of your parent's wishes when you have evidence it wasn't.

3. **Bring the siblings into a transparent, shared process.** Disclose the note and the earlier instruction to all three. Then aim for a principle they can jointly endorse. One defensible shape is an equal base with an openly acknowledged adjustment for the caregiver, reached by agreement. Another is equal division, with the caregiver's sacrifice recognized in other ways. The goal is an outcome each sibling can accept as reasoned rather than imposed.

The competing grounds are genuine:

- **Fidelity:** the equal-division instruction was explicit, and the note is ambiguous.
- **Desert:** years of unpaid care is a real contribution, and it carried costs (income, health, time) that the sibling who paid for professional help did not bear in the same way.
- **Reciprocity:** the sibling who funded professional care also gave something substantial, though in a different currency.
- **Need:** all three need the money, and the ill sibling's contribution was limited by circumstance, not choice.

None of these cleanly outranks the others, so the aim is a justification you can state openly, not a formula.

## 2. Why this fits your ethical profile

Your profile leans toward principles over outcomes, toward the collective over the individual, and toward explicit reasoning over intuition. That points to a resolution that rests on a rule everyone can recognize as fair, not on a case-by-case sympathy ranking. It also points to the family as a unit whose standing matters, so a process the siblings accept together carries more weight for you than an optimal result delivered over their objections. And because you favor articulated reasons, the justification for any adjustment should be written down and shareable, not merely felt.

Role-based thinking, in the spirit of Confucian ethics, fits here without being a label for you. Your roles as child, sibling, and executor each carry obligations. As executor you owe fidelity to your parent. As sibling you owe each of the others fair treatment and the preservation of the relationship. A transparent process honors both.

## 3. What to watch for

- **Filling the gap with your own preferences.** Any amount you choose for the caregiver is your number, not your parent's. Be honest about that.
- **Treating the caregiver's claim as automatically superior.** Unpaid care is weighty, but so is the other sibling's financial contribution and the third sibling's illness.
- **Treating the written instruction as automatically decisive.** A conflicting note is evidence that your parent's views may have changed or been unsettled.
- **Selective disclosure.** Revealing the note to some siblings and not others, or at a convenient moment, would damage trust more than the money would.
- **Resentment from a pressured agreement.** A sibling who agrees to an adjustment out of guilt or fear of conflict may not truly endorse it. Make it easy to disagree.
- **Emotional history the numbers miss.** Old family dynamics will shape how each sibling reads the note, so expect that and don't dismiss it.
- **Your own stake.** If you are one of the three, you have a conflict of interest. Consider having a neutral party, such as the lawyer or a mediator, facilitate.

No option here is free of moral cost. Equal division may slight the caregiver, an adjustment departs from an explicit instruction, and any outcome may strain the family. The aim is to choose the cost you can defend openly.

## 4. A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Photograph or copy the note without altering it, and keep the original safe. Then contact the estate attorney, or find one if there isn't one, and ask three questions: What legal status does this note have? What are my duties and limits as executor? Would a mediated family discussion be appropriate before any distribution? Hold off on any distribution or any conversation with your siblings about amounts until you have those answers.
