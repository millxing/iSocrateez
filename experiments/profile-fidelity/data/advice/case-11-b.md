## Core advice

Decline the content condition, and decline the secrecy as a separate matter. Accepting would mean the library removes materials because a donor's industry dislikes them, and then hides that fact from the public it serves. Refusing the condition does not end the library's obligations. It creates a duty to work hard, and quickly, to keep the room open by other means.

Three distinctions should guide the decision:

- **Content versus cost.** A donor who pays for a room is shaping the library's resources. A donor who conditions money on which books exist is shaping the library's judgment about what the public may read. The second is a transfer of editorial control, which is a different kind of price.
- **Secrecy as its own variable.** Even if removing titles were defensible, concealing it would be a further wrong. It would deceive patrons and remove the accountability that lets the public catch and correct a mistake.
- **Doing versus allowing.** Weeding the collection is an act. Closing the room is a failure to provide a good. The library owes students both, and the asymmetry does not make the closure costless.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

You weight principle heavily and treat the library's integrity as a commitment rather than a bargaining chip. Framed collectively, that integrity is a trust held for every current and future patron, not a private purity. The library's independence protects the people who rely on it, including the students who need the room. Trading it away for five years of access spends a public good that the public did not agree to spend.

Your reasoning-first orientation also means you should not accept the comfortable answer without testing it. The strongest objection to refusal is that it can look like moral self-regard paid for by students who did not choose the trade-off. Take that seriously. Refusal is defensible only if the library treats the closure as a real loss it must work to mitigate, not as an acceptable side effect of principle.

## What to watch for

**The consequentialist counterargument is serious.** Five years of access for many students may outweigh removing a handful of titles that may exist elsewhere. Ask honestly how many titles, how central they are, and whether patrons can get them through interlibrary loan or other branches. If the answer is "few, peripheral, and easily obtained," the case for refusal weakens. Don't dismiss that possibility.

**Precedent is a risk, not a certainty.** One accepted condition may signal that holdings are purchasable and invite further demands. That risk is real, reasoned from incentives, but it isn't guaranteed. Note it as a consideration, not a prophecy.

**Compromises can be disguised capitulation.** Distinguish them:

- *Preserve independence:* accepting an unconditional gift, publicly naming the donor, offering recognition without a role in selection, or seeking co-funders to dilute influence.
- *Concede it:* quietly weeding, "rebalancing" the collection, or restricting the placement or access of critical works. If the donor's condition is implemented through any of these, the library has conceded the point even if the word "censorship" never appears.

**Two tests.** Would the library defend this decision if it were published? Would it accept the same condition from any donor? If the answers are no, the decision is a rationalization, whatever the outcome.

**Legal constraints.** Public-library governance, open-records rules, and board obligations may limit how the secrecy could be maintained at all. Check these with counsel rather than assuming. This is not a legal conclusion.

**Facts that could change the judgment:**
- the number and importance of the affected titles;
- whether they are available elsewhere in the system;
- whether the board and public could be told;
- whether the closure is truly irreversible, such as a lease that cannot be renegotiated or a room that could reopen if funding arrives later.

If the titles are few, available, and the closure is reversible, the case against accepting is weaker. If the condition is broad, the titles central, and the closure permanent, refusal is much stronger.

**The remaining discomfort is real.** Even with a clear recommendation, students will lose a room they depend on. That loss should stay on the table, not be argued away by the principle.

## A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Ask the library director, with the board chair copied, to send a written reply to the donor today that:

1. declines any condition on the collection and any agreement to keep it confidential;
2. offers an unconditional gift, with public recognition if the donor wants it;

In the same meeting, direct staff to start an emergency public appeal and to contact the school district and community partners about temporary study space or extended hours elsewhere.
