## Core advice

The weight of the case favors telling your brother, carefully and in person, while acknowledging that you are breaking a real promise. Your brother is making decisions about his own heart based on a family history you know to be inaccurate for him. Your silence is no longer a neutral omission. It keeps in place a premise he is relying on, and that makes it a form of participation.

Your promise was real and it still binds you. Breaking it is a wrong you will carry, even if disclosure is right. Treating that residue as something to name, not dissolve, is part of acting well here.

## Why this fits your ethical profile

You reason from duties and universal commitments, so the question is a conflict between two principled obligations, not a contest between principle and outcome:

- **Promissory fidelity.** A promise made to a dying person does not lapse with her death. Trust in promising depends on keeping promises, and your mother's request may have come from love and fear of harming your brother.
- **Honesty and respect for persons.** Your brother is a rational agent entitled to accurate premises about his own body. Letting him decide on false premises, when you know they are false and he is relying on your family's testimony, treats his agency as something to manage.

The promise, read as a universal rule, is "keep promises even when doing so means knowingly letting someone make serious decisions about their own body on false information." That is hard to will as a rule for everyone. A promise cannot rightly commit you to wronging a third party in this way.

Your mother's intention is uncertain. She may have meant "don't make this a wound in my son's life," or "don't take his identity from him," or something else. Asking what she most plausibly meant, and whether she would have wanted the promise kept once her son's heart condition became a medical matter, is interpretation, not proof. It helps show whether the promise had implicit limits, but it does not settle the matter alone.

## What to watch for

- **Separating the medical fact from the full story.** Your brother needs to know that the family history does not reflect his biological inheritance. He may not need every detail of his origins at once, and you can honor that. But a partial disclosure that leaves him still misled would not satisfy the honesty duty.
- **Alarm.** Saying only "the family history may not apply to you" without explanation can be evasive and frightening. Be clear and direct.
- **Bypassing him.** Telling his doctor or pursuing genetic testing on his behalf without him may keep the promise's letter while violating his agency. A genetic counselor can explain how much adoptive status matters medically, but that conversation should inform him, not replace him.
- **Grief-driven certainty.** You have lost your mother recently. Avoid framing her as either a villain or a saint. She acted from a real motive, and the decision you face is still hard.
- **Uncertainty about stakes.** How serious the heart condition is, whether his doctors already know of the adoption, and how he will respond are all unknown. Don't let these unknowns become a reason to delay indefinitely.

## A concrete next step in the next 24 hours

Write down, in your own words, the exact wording of what your mother asked and what you promised, along with the reasons you think she had. Then contact your brother and ask for a conversation in person or by call within the next few days. Tell him there is something important about your family history that he needs to hear before his doctor relies on it, and ask him not to finalize decisions based on that history until you have talked.
